<aside>Goddess of lust</aside>

Before drawing the image of Venus, I want to make a sketch of her nature, because it will be of no small help towards understanding the reason for various things that I shall say afterwards about that image. According to the fables, then, Venus was the goddess of lust and of lasciviousness, as if she sent into the hearts of mortals libidinous desires and lascivious appetites, and as if the desired fulfilment of these was granted with her help. Hence they made her the mother of Cupid, because it hardly seems that a man and a woman ever come together if Cupid does not come between them.

> Image of Venus, born from the foam of the sea, goddess of beauty and of lust, mother of Cupid, symbol of lasciviousness, who was also held to be the goddess of weddings and of marriage, understood as the planet Venus, also called Lucifer and Hesperus, which induces the generative power into things.

To her likewise the ancients gave, besides Hymenaeus and Juno, the care of weddings, since these are made so that carnal union may follow from them, and from that the generation of children. Beauty too was given into Venus's keeping, so that she could give it and take it away as it seemed good to her.

<aside>Venus according to the natural philosophers</aside>

But according to natural things, which are signified in various ways under the name of this goddess, she shows that hidden power by which all animals are drawn to the desire to generate. Hence those who hold that the human soul descends from Heaven into our bodies and, passing from sphere to sphere, draws from each of them particular affections, say that from Venus it takes the concupiscible appetite, which moves it to lust and to lascivious desires. Some also maintain, likewise drawing the fables towards natural things, that Venus, Juno, the Moon, Proserpina, Diana and some others are one single goddess, but that the names are so many, and so different, because so many are the different powers that come from her. This will also be seen from the various designs of her image, beginning with the one that shows her first birth.

<aside>Birth of Venus</aside>
<aside>Ovid</aside>

For the fables relate that she was born of the foam of the sea, Saturn having thrown into it the testicles that he cut off from Caelus his father. This has been expounded by many, and perhaps most clearly of all by Leone Ebreo in his dialogues of love. Wishing, then, to show that Venus was born of the sea, the ancients painted her coming out of it, standing in a great sea shell, young and beautiful, as far as it was possible to depict her, and wholly naked; and they also depicted her going off swimming through the sea at her pleasure. Hence in Ovid, who has this in view, she says to Neptune:

> And I too have something to do
> among these waves, if it is true that I was once
> thick foam in the sea, from which
> I took the name that I still keep today.
>
> *(Ovid)*

<aside>Aphrodite</aside>
<aside>Virgil</aside>

For the Greeks called her Aphrodite from the foam, which they name with a word not very different from this. In Virgil likewise Neptune answers her in this way when she begs him to calm at last the storm at sea by which her son Aeneas was already so afflicted:

> It is most just that you trust
> in my realms, since from them is your first origin.
>
> *(Virgil)*

> Temple of Venus at Paphos, a city of Cyprus, with a hieroglyph showing her and her nature. Chariot of Venus drawn by swans and doves sacred to her, with her image on the said chariot, naked, with the three Graces beside her, as the Saxons depicted her, with three golden apples in one hand and a ball in the other; and it shows that gold opens the way to lasciviousness, and the whole denotes the natural carnal desire to generate.

Thus among the other idols that were in the temple of Jupiter among the Eleans in Greece, as Pausanias writes, there was one of Venus, who, rising from the sea, was received by Cupid.

<aside>Sea shell given to Venus</aside>

Sometimes Venus was represented as a most beautiful woman with a sea shell in her hand and a wreath of roses on her head, because roses are proper to this goddess, as I shall say later, giving the reason for it; and the sea shell always shows that Venus was born of the sea, whether she holds it in her hand or stands in it with her feet. Some, however, claim that the sea shell is given to Venus because in coition it opens wholly and shows itself wholly, in order to show what is done in Venereal unions and in amorous pleasures.

To these pleasures the island of Cyprus seemed to be given beyond measure, whether because the part of Heaven to which it is subject so willed, or because the nature of its inhabitants was such for some other reason. For this reason the people of Paphos, a city of this island, said that when Venus came out of the sea she appeared first near them, and so they worshipped her with the greatest reverence, and among them there was a temple dedicated to her, in which her statue was not, like the others, made in human form, but was a certain round thing, wide at the bottom, which narrowed little by little towards the top.

<aside>Cornelius Tacitus</aside>
<aside>Jupiter Ammon</aside>

Of this thing, as Cornelius Tacitus reports, it does not seem that any explanation is known. Still, I remember having read that this figure represents the navel of the human body, and is given to Venus because it is believed that lust in women resides and begins in this part. But even if this were true, what shall we say then of the idol of Jupiter Ammon, which in a certain part of Egypt was likewise made in this fashion, as can be seen in his image? I want to believe that some mystery was contained in this figure, which perhaps the first who made it did not wish to state, either to give those who came after them something to think over, or because it was always the opinion of the most ancient that it was well to hide the things of religion, or to show them in such a way that they could not be known except by someone who put great study into them and attended only to them, since it seemed to them that in this way they would be far more regarded by all, and held in greater respect, as I have said elsewhere.

<aside>Chariots given to the gods</aside>

A chariot was then likewise given to Venus, as to the other gods, on which, besides the sea shell, she travelled through the air and over the sea and wherever it seemed good to her. Claudian, though, when he pictures her going to the wedding of Honorius and Maria, shows Triton carrying her on his slippery back, shading her with his raised tail.

<aside>Doves, birds of Venus</aside>

And because each god has animals of his own that draw his chariot, Venus's is drawn by snow-white doves, as Apuleius says, because these birds, more than any other, seem to be like her, and for this reason they are also called the birds of Venus. For they are excessively lascivious, and there is no time of the year when they are not together; and it is said that the male dove never mounts the female dove without kissing her first, just as lovers do.

<aside>Fable of Peristera</aside>
<aside>Aelian</aside>

The fables relate that the dove was so dear to Venus because Peristera, a nymph much loved by her, was changed into this bird. Besides this, Aelian shows that doves were consecrated to Venus from this: that on Eryx, a mountain of Sicily, certain festival days were celebrated, which all the Sicilians called days of passage, because they said that on these days Venus passed over into Libya, and for this reason in all that country not so much as one dove was then to be seen, as if all had gone to accompany their goddess.

<aside>Anacreon</aside>

Nine days after that, one was seen flying back from the Libyan sea, very beautiful and not made like the others but red, as Anacreon says Venus is, where he calls her purple; and behind this one came the flocks of the other doves. For this reason the people of Mount Eryx celebrated at that time, because these had now returned, the days of the return, those who were rich giving fine and copious banquets, as Athenaeus reports.

<aside>Swans given to Venus</aside>

Swans also drew the chariot of Venus, as Horace, Ovid and Statius put it — either because this is a most harmless bird, which does no harm to anyone, or else because of the sweetness of its song, since song seems to help lasciviousness and amorous pleasures greatly.

> Image of Venus drawn in a chariot by swans, guided by little Cupids; to show that the song and the placidity of their nature are very much in keeping with the pleasures of Love.

<aside>Why Venus is naked</aside>

This goddess was depicted naked to show, as some claim, that for which she is always ready, namely lascivious embraces, and because we enjoy these better naked than clothed; or else because whoever always goes after lascivious pleasures often remains stripped and deprived of every good, since he loses his riches, which are devoured by lascivious women, weakens his body, and stains his soul with such foulness that nothing beautiful remains in it. Or else Venus was made naked to give to understand that amorous thefts cannot remain hidden, and if they do remain so for a little while, they are discovered afterwards all the same, and it often happens that they show themselves just when they are least thought of and least feared.

<aside>Miraculous statue</aside>

Whether it was this, then, or something else that Praxiteles, that noble sculptor, had in mind, he made for the people of Cnidus a Venus wholly naked, of whitest marble, so beautiful that many sailed to Cyprus, drawn by the sole desire to see this statue. Of it we read that one man fell so much in love with it that, with no regard for any danger, nor for any harm that might befall him from it, he hid one night in the temple where it stood; and, embracing it, pressing it and kissing it, giving it all those caresses that are given to the most delicate girls when they are very dear, he fulfilled his amorous desire, as a result of which a certain stain always remained afterwards on one flank of the beautiful statue.

<aside>History of the Saxons</aside>
<aside>Giraldi</aside>

Venus goes swimming through the sea, they say, to give to understand how bitter is the life of lascivious men, tossed continually by the stormy waves of uncertain thoughts and by the frequent shipwreck that their plans suffer. We read in the histories of the Saxons that among them this goddess stood upright on a chariot drawn by two swans and as many doves, naked, with her head girt with myrtle, and she had on her breast a burning torch; in her right hand she held a certain round ball in the form of the world, and in her left she carried three golden apples, and behind stood the Graces, all three, with their arms entwined together, as appears in the design noted above. What this image or statue signifies would not be too difficult to say; but since Giraldi, who reports it where he writes of the gods of the Gentiles, has said nothing more about it, I leave everyone to interpret it in his own way.

<aside>Myrtle given to Venus</aside>

I will say, at least, that we read that myrtle was given to Venus because it was believed to have in itself the power to give rise to love between people and to preserve it. And Plutarch says that it is a plant signifying peace, which is why among the Romans those who celebrated a certain small triumph for having defeated the enemy with very little effort and without killing were crowned with myrtle, a plant proper to Venus, because she greatly hates violence, wars and discord; and others have said that it was rather because myrtle happily springs up and grows on the coastal lowlands and around the shores of the sea, where, as we have already said, Venus was born.

<aside>Roses given to Venus</aside>

Roses were given to her too, because they have a sweet scent, which represents the sweetness of amorous pleasures; or else because, as roses are red and can hardly be picked without feeling the stings of the sharp thorns, so lust seems to carry with it that we blush every time we remember its foulness, and then the conscience for the errors already committed pricks and pierces us in such a way that we feel the gravest pain. Besides this, the beauty of the rose, by which it gives delight to those who look at it, lasts a very short time and soon fades, as amorous pleasures also do; and for this reason they put wreaths of these on Venus's head.

<aside>Red roses</aside>

Roses were not always red, however; on the contrary, at first they were all white, but they were dyed afterwards with the blood of this goddess one time when she, running to give help to her beloved Adonis, whom Mars, who had become jealous of him, wished to kill, set her feet on the sharp thorns of the white roses and was gravely pricked by them, so that the blood that came out was the cause that from then on red roses grew.

<aside>Athenaeus. A pleasant story. Venus Kallipyge.</aside>

And although what I am now about to say has little to do with depicting Venus, nevertheless, because it seems to me a graceful and delightful thing, I shall tell it as Athenaeus tells it, saying that the ancients of those times were greatly given to lascivious pleasures, and so dedicated a temple to Venus, calling her Kallipyge, which properly means one who has beautiful buttocks, for this reason.

Two daughters of a peasant, young girls, beautiful and graceful, fell into a dispute with each other over which of them had the more beautiful buttocks, and, unable to agree between themselves, because neither wished to yield to the other, they went out onto the public road; and, finding there by chance a young man not known to either of them, they showed themselves to him so that he might give judgement on it, each promising to abide by what he judged. The young man, having looked very carefully at that part over which the dispute had arisen, and having considered it diligently within himself, judged that the elder had the more beautiful buttocks; and, being in love with her for that reason, he took her home, where he had a brother, to whom he told what had happened.

The brother felt a wish to see what it was, and, going off to where his brother had shown him, he found the other of the two sisters standing there quite downcast, because she had been judged to have the less beautiful buttocks. He had her show them to him, and they seemed to him so beautiful that he fell in love with her at once; and, comforting the girl, he begged her to be of good cheer, as one who had buttocks so beautiful that it was not possible for another to have more beautiful ones, whatever his brother had judged of it. Then he persuaded her to go with him, which she did willingly; and so the two brothers took as wives the two sisters with the beautiful buttocks, who in a short time became very rich — though we do not read how, but each can easily imagine it for himself — and they afterwards built a temple to Venus, calling her Kallipyge, which we should say is "with the beautiful buttocks", because all their good fortune came from this part.

<aside>Apuleius</aside>

If in those girls this part was beautiful and loved, let everyone who has even a little judgement think what it must have been in Venus, who was most beautiful in her whole body, as Apuleius describes her very well when he describes her being represented on the stage, saying that she was of most beautiful aspect and of a sweet and joyful colour, and, almost wholly naked, showed her perfect beauty in full; for she had about her nothing but a most subtle veil, which did not cover but only shaded those beautiful and so delicate parts. These, remaining hidden with it almost always, were sometimes revealed when the gentle wind, blowing lightly, lifted it a little, swelling it so that the fair flower of youth might be seen, and sometimes pressed it and drew it close to the beautiful limbs in such a way that it almost no longer appeared. The beautiful body was all white, so that one could easily say that it had come down from Heaven; the subtle veil was sky-blue, for such is the colour of the sea, from which this goddess first came forth.

Before her went charming Cupids with burning little torches in their hands, as was the custom of the ancients, that five boys with lit torches in their hands went before the new bride the first time she went to the house of the bridegroom; and on one side she had the Graces, on the other the most beautiful Horae, who with beautiful wreaths of flowers in a thousand charming ways seemed to adorn the goddess of pleasures.

> Image of Venus, goddess of pleasures, mother of Cupid, accompanied by little Cupids, by the Horae and by the three Graces, signifying amorous delights, and the good augury that the ancients made with such images for new brides: of harmonious marriage and of ardent love.

<aside>Horace</aside>
<aside>Homer</aside>

This is the portrait that Apuleius makes of Venus. Some others depict the Graces going behind her, whereas he puts them at one of her sides, and depict Cupid at one of her hands and Anteros at the other. Horace, singing of her, shows her cheerful and laughing, and says that Jocus (which means jesting with cheerful and pleasant words, and was likewise depicted by the ancients in human form) flies about her together with Cupid. And Homer calls her almost always lover of laughter, because laughter is a sign of cheerfulness, which accompanies lasciviousness.

<aside>Peter Apian</aside>

So among the ancient things collected by Peter Apian there is found one that serves this purpose: a naked boy with wings, crowned with myrtle, who sits on the ground and plays a harp that he holds between his legs, and on his head is written VENVS; before him stands another like him, upright on his feet, who looks at him, holding with both hands stretched out on high one of two plaits, at the top of which is a beautiful woman's face adorned with a cloth that hangs down to the middle of the plaits. Above this head is written IOCVS, and above the boy, CVPIDO.

<aside>Verticordia</aside>

And since from Venus come honest thoughts no less than lascivious desires, the Romans, on the counsel of the Sibylline books, vowed a temple to her, so that she might turn the minds of their women (who had given themselves up to lust too licentiously) to more honest desires, and afterwards they called her Verticordia, because she turned, as Ovid writes, the hearts of those lascivious women to a more honest life.

This was perhaps the temple that Marcellus built after he had conquered Sicily, about a mile outside Rome, so that all lasciviousness might stay as far from Roman women as that temple was from the walls of Rome. To it, we read, girls who were already grown went to offer certain little figures made either of stucco or of rags, with which they are accustomed to play in their childhood.

<aside>Heavenly Venus</aside>

This Venus of the Romans was similar to the one that was called Apostrophia by the Greeks, whom we may call the Averter, because she was opposed to dishonest desires and removed libidinous wishes from human minds; for Harmonia, wife of Cadmus, so named her for the Thebans, as Pausanias writes. Among them there was also a Heavenly Venus, from whom came that pure and sincere Love that is wholly alien to the union of bodies; and there was another, called Popular and Common, who made the Love from which human generation comes; and she was once made by Scopas, an excellent sculptor, in this way.

<aside>Plutarch</aside>

She sat on a goat and with one foot trod on a tortoise, as Alexander ab Alexandro reports, and Plutarch had already written of this in the precepts that he gives to husbands, and gave the reason too, saying that Phidias once made for the Eleans a Venus who stood with one foot on a tortoise, to show women that it fell to them to have the care of the house and to speak as little as possible, because in a woman silence is judged a most beautiful thing.

<aside>Nature of the tortoise</aside>

In another place the same Plutarch, wishing to expound what this image signifies (of which Pausanias also makes mention), says that girls, while they are virgins, must be under the custody of others; but once they are married, they must have the care of the government of the house and keep quiet, as if their husbands had to speak for them. For Pliny writes that the tortoise has no tongue. And reading in the same author, and in Aelian too, about the nature of this animal, I find that the ancient sculptors gave women a fine and holy admonition by putting the tortoise under the foot of Venus; for the tortoise knows the danger she runs when she couples with the male, seeing that she must turn over with her belly upwards, and the male, when he has done his business, goes away and leaves her, who cannot right herself by herself, as prey to the other animals, but above all to the eagle.

For this reason she abstains from coition with the greatest continence and, fleeing the male, puts safety before libidinous pleasure, to which she is nevertheless forced to consent afterwards, touched by a certain herb that sets her wholly aflame with lust, so that after that she no longer fears anything. Women too, then, must consider to what danger they expose themselves when they lose their honour, and for this reason they must flee lascivious pleasures and libidinous appetites, unless the duty of marriage forces them to these for the succession of new offspring.

> Images of Venus, of Cupid, of Jocus and of the goat, which signify generation; and the image of the tortoise, a hieroglyph, which denotes the danger of married women and of women in childbirth, and what their duty must be in the care of the family and in bringing up children, and that silence is necessary to women above every virtue.

<aside>Venus with Mercury</aside>
<aside>Peitho</aside>

Besides the Graces and the Cupids, Plutarch writes, the ancients were accustomed to place with the statue of Venus that of Mercury too, wishing in this way to give to understand that amorous unions have need of sweet and gentle attentions and of pleasant words, because these often give rise to Love between people and preserve it. For this reason they also placed among the Graces who went with Venus the one who was called Peitho by the Greeks and Suada by the Latins, and she was the goddess of persuasion.

<aside>Megarians</aside>
<aside>Ovid</aside>

Suada, in the temple of Jupiter among the Eleans in Greece, presented a crown to Venus, who was rising from the sea and was received by Cupid, as I said above. And the Megarians likewise placed the idol of Suada in the temple of Venus; and the first who had the one and the other worshipped among the Athenians was Theseus, as Pausanias relates, after he had gathered into one city those people who before were scattered through the fields. In other places of Greece too there were temples of the goddess Suada; from which it can be seen that she too was worshipped by the ancients and often placed in the company of Venus, because, as Ovid says:

> Venus was the first who made
> men refined out of the rude beings that they were.
>
> *(Ovid)*

<aside>Arcadians</aside>

The first eloquence was that of lovers, who sought to persuade the young women they loved to be easy to their desires, and, to please them too, found a thousand fine things that were not known before. So the Arcadians, worshipping Venus, called her Contriver and Inventor, and rightly, says Pausanias, seeing that on account of the pleasures that come from Venus men have found various ways of drawing beautiful young women to their wishes, afterwards leading a joyful life with them, because Venus seems to care only for cheerful and pleasant things. For this reason Jupiter in Homer warns her to keep away from sad wars, when she wished to help Aeneas against Diomedes, who wounded her in the hand, because these things are proper to Mars and to Minerva, not to her, to whom belongs the care of amorous pleasures.

<aside>Lactantius</aside>

But not for this did the ancients refrain from depicting Venus armed. The reason for this, as Lactantius writes, was that while the Lacedaemonians were besieging Messene, the Messenians went out secretly to sack Lacedaemon and to plunder all the country round about, believing that they could do it easily, since all the fighting men of the place had gone to the siege. But their plan did not succeed for them; for the Lacedaemonian women, who learned of this, armed themselves, all those who were fit for it, and going against the enemy not only defended the city and the country from being sacked but also put the Messenians to rout and forced them to go back.

In the meantime the Lacedaemonians, having noticed the deceit of the enemy, went out to meet them; but since the Messenians were returning, fleeing by another road, they could not find them, and so came upon their own women, all armed. The women, thinking them to be the enemy, were drawing up in order to fight, when they revealed themselves and made themselves seen by their men, who recognised them at once, and all went immediately to embrace one another. And because there was no time then for each man to find his own, they amorously took their pleasure together for a while, armed as they were, each with the one that chanced to fall at his feet, as though it were the dearest and most welcome reward that they could give to those valiant women warriors for their labours.

<aside>Ausonius</aside>

So, in memory of this event and of the fine deed done by the women, the Lacedaemonians set up a temple to Venus with a statue of her armed, on which Ausonius made a fine epigram, and he imagines that Pallas, seeing Venus armed, as she herself also always went, wishes to come again to a contest with her, even under the judgement of Paris; but Venus mocks her as rash, for having the audacity to challenge her now that she sees her armed, if she was defeated by her already when she was naked. The epigram, put into the vernacular, is this.

> Pallas, seeing at Sparta the fair
> Venus armed in the manner of a warrior,
> said: "Now it is time to end that
> quarrel which makes you go so haughty,
> and let Paris be the judge of it." And she
> answered: "Ah, rash one, does your spirit
> then hope to conquer me, armed, now,
> when naked and unarmed I conquered you before?"
>
> *(Ausonius)*

> Image of Venus armed, of Venus Victorious and of Venus in fetters, denoting the steadfastness that there must be in married people and lovers; this image also denotes the valour of the Lacedaemonian women against the Messenians, who were going to sack their city, which was valiantly defended by them.

<aside>Venus Victorious</aside>

Whether for this reason or for another, Venus was sometimes also called Victorious; and it is found that in a certain part of the Corinthian country there was a statue that held out a Victory in its hand and was therefore called Nikephoros, a Greek word, which among us means bearing Victory. And Pausanias writes that this was dedicated by Hypermnestra, after she was freed from the trial that her father Danaus had brought against her because she had not wished to obey him in killing her husband, as all her other sisters had done.

The Romans depicted Venus Victorious in this way, as is seen on a medal of the emperor Numerian. They painted and carved a most beautiful woman in a dress long to the ground, who with her right hand held out a small image of Victory, and in the left had a certain thing made in this fashion; some held that it represented the image which the people of Paphos worshipped under the name of Venus, as I have already said, and some others have held that it is rather a mirror, because Philostratus writes in the painting that he describes of the Cupids that the nymphs set up a statue to Venus, because she made them mothers of such beautiful offspring as the Cupids are, and dedicated to her a silver mirror, with some gilded ornaments for the feet.

<aside>Medal of Faustina</aside>

In another way too Venus is seen on an ancient medal of Faustina Augusta: with her left hand she holds a shield resting on the ground, which has two small figures carved in the middle, and with the right she holds out a Victory, and there are letters around that say, Venus Victorious. I remember having seen another medal, also ancient and also of Faustina, where there were letters that said, Venus, with a woman standing, dressed, who with her left hand held the hem of her dress on one side and pulled it up; with the other she held out something, I do not know what, which seemed to be an apple, perhaps in memory of the one that Paris gave her when he judged her more beautiful than Juno and Pallas.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

So Pausanias too puts an apple in her hand when he reports a certain statue of Venus that was among the Sicyonians in Greece, saying that there was a temple dedicated to this goddess, into which never more than two women could enter; and of these, one, who had the keeping of it, always remained chaste and did not lie with her husband while she was in this office; the other had to be a virgin, because she handled the things of the sacrifices, and she did not remain in this charge for more than a year. And all the others who went to this temple to pray to the goddess for something stood outside, before the doors. Her statue was of gold, seated; with one hand it held some heads of poppy, and with the other an apple, and on the top of its head it had a certain thing that represents a pole, or let us say a hinge. And the one that was made by Tyndareus had a certain veil, such as the women of those times used to wear for adornment.

<aside>Morpho — Venus with her feet bound</aside>

Of this one the same Pausanias says that among the Lacedaemonians, above the temple of armed Venus, there was, as we should say, a chapel, where she sat; she was called Morpho there, with a certain veil on her head, as I said, and with cords or fetters (whichever they were) on her feet; it suffices that she had them bound, to show, as some say, that women must be of the firmest fidelity towards those to whom they are already bound by the marriage knot. But some others have said that Tyndareus made Venus thus in fetters to avenge himself for the adulteries committed by his daughters, as if this had come about through her fault.

Pausanias scoffs at this and does not wish to believe it, saying that it would be too foolish a thing to think that any harm could be done to Venus by making a statue of her out of cedar, like this one of which we are speaking, and putting fetters on her feet. And it seems to me that he speaks very well; for the ancients made the statues of the gods neither out of contempt nor out of any vengeance that they wished to take on them, but out of the reverence they bore them, for the help and favour they expected from them in all things, and sometimes also to show in their statues, to those who did not know it, their various powers. So, as can be seen on some other images too, the ancients put fetters on the feet not only of Venus but of the other gods as well, and not out of contempt nor out of vengeance but for other reasons, which I know I have given elsewhere, and so I do not repeat them.

But I say that although Venus seemed to be the chief numen of courtesans, as if she had once invented their art and put it into use, so that they celebrated her feast solemnly, praying her to give them grace, beauty and elegance, so that they might be loved by all, to their profit and gain, nevertheless she was also worshipped with no less devotion by honest girls, who thought that she could give them such charm and such good looks that it would then be easy for them to marry, because, as I have said at other times, the ancients gave Venus the care of marriage too.

Among the Greeks there was a certain cave, where, Pausanias writes, sacred honours were paid to Venus, and people went there for many reasons, but it seemed to be especially the custom for widows to go there, as they did, to pray the goddess to give them a second marriage happily. Married women likewise prayed to her, and not only there but also in her other temples, that she keep them always united with their husbands in mutual love and make them glad with new offspring and with a fair succession. So Venus was a numen common to all kinds of women, who, as being perhaps more bound to this goddess than the others, acknowledged as coming from her almost all that happened to them happily, and men too thanked her for every benefit that had come from her.

<aside>Claudian</aside>

So, since the women all cut off their hair to make from it the ropes for hauling the machines that were then used in war, when the Romans, besieged by the Gauls on the Capitol, were in extreme need of everything, these, freed from the siege, dedicated, as Lactantius reports, a temple to Venus, where they depicted her Bald, and so named her in memory of what the women had done for the public benefit, though otherwise Venus is always depicted with most beautiful hair, as Claudian describes her, saying:

> Venus then on a fair gilded seat,
> busy arranging her lovely blond hair,
> had around her the Graces, of whom
> one sprinkles with sweet nectar
> the golden locks, and another
> draws them out and loosens them with ivory teeth,
> the third ties them in fair order
> with a white hand and gathers them into lovely plaits.
>
> *(Claudian)*

<aside>Bearded Venus</aside>

The ancients depicted her not only with hair but with a beard too: such a statue was worshipped in Cyprus as Venus, as Alexander ab Alexandro reports, which in face and aspect seemed a man, but then had about it women's garments. And Suda writes that the statue of Venus was made with a comb in the hand and a beard on the face because a certain illness once came upon the Roman women, so that all their hair fell out, as often happens in our times too, and they no longer had need to use a comb. For this reason the women, afflicted by so foul an illness, vowed themselves to Venus, and with endless vows begged her to provide for their misery; and she, who was always kind, accepting their devout prayers, brought it about that the women's hair no longer fell out, and what had fallen grew again.

<aside>All the gods male and female</aside>

As a sign of gratitude the women afterwards set up for her a statue that held a comb in its hand. And they gave her a beard too, so that this goddess might have the insignia of male and female, as one who presided over the universal generation of animals; and so from the middle upward they depicted her in the form of a male, and from the rest downward she was female. The ancients said this not only of Venus but of all the other gods too, giving each of them the name of both male and female, as though among them there were not the difference of sex that there is among mortals.

> Image of Venus male and female, signifying that she presides over the universal generation of things, being taken for the air; and that among the gods there is no difference of sex, as among mortals; and image of Venus grieving for the death of Adonis, killed by the boar, understood as the wintry and cold season.

<aside>Remarkable custom</aside>

We read that among the Carrhenians, a people of Arabia, this was observed: that all those who believed the Moon to be female and called her by a female name were under the women's rule and obliged to serve their wives; and on the contrary he who believed her male and so named her was never deceived by women, and his wife obeyed him and was subject to him, as duty seems to require.

<aside>The god Lunus</aside>

The Egyptians, although commonly they called the Moon by a female name, nevertheless in their mysteries called her not goddess but god. And for this reason the calf so celebrated among them was worshipped for her. And the Parthians worshipped the god Lunus, and Philochorus, who holds that Venus is one and the same as the Moon, as some of the Egyptians also believed — who for this reason made horns on her statue (because the Moon is depicted with horns, as can be seen in her image) — says that anciently men in women's dress used to sacrifice to her, and women dressed as men.

Not very different from this is what Seneca wrote in his *Natural Questions*, where he sets down that the Egyptians made two of each of the four elements established by them, one male and the other female. For they said that of the air the wind is the male, and the female is that which does not seem to move and is almost always misty; that the sea is the male of the waters, and fresh water is wholly female; that of fire that which burns is male, and female that which gives light and does no harm; and that of the earth the harder is male, such as stones and rocks, and female that which is softer and can be cultivated.

<aside>The Adonia</aside>

An idol of Venus was also made like the one that was seen on Mount Lebanon, which had a cloak about it that, beginning from the head, covered it all, and it seemed wholly sad and disconsolate, and with a hand likewise wrapped in the cloak it supported its drooping face, and, as Macrobius says, everyone who saw it believed that tears fell from its eyes. There Venus was shown so grieved for the death of Adonis, killed by a boar.

For this reason some days were kept as sacred, called the Adonia, and at that time the women everywhere in the cities laid certain images like dead bodies on little beds made for the purpose, and, as if they were persons only just dead, carried them weeping to the tombs; this, says Plutarch, they did in Athens in remembrance of the tears shed by Venus at the death of Adonis, her lover. And among the Argives, as Pausanias writes, the women went to weep for Adonis in a certain chapel not far from the temple of Jupiter Saviour.

<aside>Venus as half of the earth</aside>

This, if it is referred to the things of Nature, is thus interpreted by Macrobius: of the whole earth, this upper half, which is ours, was understood by the ancients under the name of Venus, and they called the other half, below, Proserpina. Besides this, of the twelve signs of the Zodiac, which surrounds it, six are called upper and the other six lower, these of winter, those of summer.

<aside>Adonis — the Sun</aside>

When, then, the Sun, who is signified by Adonis, goes in the time of summer through the six upper signs, Venus has her lover with her and is wholly glad. But then she is believed to weep and shows herself sad when she sees him descend, at the time of winter, into the lower signs, as if he were dying then, and Proserpina kept him for herself.

<aside>Adonis killed by the boar</aside>

And the fables said that a boar killed him because this animal seems to represent winter very well, seeing that it is covered all over with hard, rough bristles, likes to stay in muddy places, and feeds on acorns, which are fruits of winter; and winter is almost a mortal wound to the Sun, because it makes him shine for us for a very short time and gives us little of his heat. These two things death does, which deprives of light and of heat. Thus the image of Venus weeping under the cloak represents to us the earth in the time of winter, when it is for the most part covered with clouds and seems wholly afflicted because it does not see the Sun. Then the springs, which are the eyes of the earth, pour out abundant waters, and the fields, deprived of every adornment, show themselves all sad.

<aside>Interpretation of Venus</aside>
<aside>Marcus Tullius</aside>

Speaking of Venus in a natural sense, Eusebius too says that from her comes the power of generating, and that it is she who gives strength to the seed. They depict her in the form of a woman, to show that generation proceeds from her; they represent her as beautiful, because she is that star which, of all the others that are in Heaven, seems to be the most beautiful, called Hesperus in the evening, as Marcus Tullius says, and in the morning Lucifer.

Cupid stands at her side as a sign that from her is born every lascivious desire and every libidinous cupidity; her breasts and genitals are covered, because within the latter the seed is shut up, and in the former the nourishment of one who is already born of the conceived seed; and they say she was born of the sea because the water of the sea is believed to be hot and moist, and to move often, and when strongly agitated to make much foam, which things are all in the seed, since it is likewise white and foamy, and by its nature moist and hot.

Much else could be said of Venus by someone who wished to discuss her as a planet, and of the effects that come from her star, which adorns the third Heaven; from this one could also learn for what reason the ancients imagined that Mars, a god so terrible and fierce, stays so gently with her. But because this would divert me too much from my purpose of speaking about the images of the gods, not about their nature, I shall say no more of her, since I do not remember having read that the ancients depicted her in any other way. And it could well be that they did, but I do not know it, nor in writing can everything be set down so fully that something does not always remain, and it is right that it should, so that everyone may have something to say. It suffices that, by reading this little that I write, anyone who wishes to paint or carve the gods of the ancients will not lack a fairly good example; and he will also know why he does so.

I shall pass on, then, to speak of the company of Venus, which is the Graces and the Horae, as I have promised, but first putting down what Mars says while he holds this goddess in his arms, having taken amorous solace with her, when Jupiter commands him to go and stir up war for the kingdom of Thebes between Eteocles and Polynices, as Statius writes: from which, without saying more of it, one will be able to understand very well what and how great is the power of Venus; so that no one will have cause any longer to wonder when he sometimes sees the steadiest spirits and the firmest minds conquered by her, so that afterwards they have given themselves up as prey to amorous pleasures. These, then, are the words of Mars, rendered into the vernacular, with which I put an end to the image of Venus.

> O my sweet repose, kindly pleasure,
> true peace of the troubled mind,
> you can oppose me without ever fearing
> me at all, even if I am angry;
> you alone can curb and restrain
> these steeds from their unbridled course
> in fierce battles, and if it seems good to you,
> you alone can disarm [?1] these hands.
>
> *(Statius)*
