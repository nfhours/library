# Saturn

<aside>Virgil</aside>

> The first was Saturn, who, descending
> from high Heaven, fleeing his son Jupiter
> and forcibly deprived of his kingdoms,
> came to show men, who at that time
> wandered scattered like wild beasts
> over the high mountains, the way to gather
> together and to obey certain laws.
> And the country where at first he stayed
> hidden (*latente*) was therefore called Latium (*Latio*).
> Under his government, it is said,
> there was the happy age of gold,
> so justly did he rule his
> peoples, giving them rest and peace.
>
> *(Virgil)*

In this way Virgil sings of Saturn, mixing history with fables, inasmuch as history relates that Saturn went to Italy, driven out of Greece by his son, whereas the fables have afterwards pretended that he was first Lord of Heaven, and that Jupiter drove him out of it and made him come down below; because Greece lies more toward the East, and is therefore higher than Italy, which faces the West.

Saturn, then, having withdrawn to Italy, was taken into a share of the kingdom by Janus, king of that country where Rome was afterwards founded (who lived with his peoples the rough life of the most ancient mortals), because he showed him the cultivation of the fields and the making of money from metal, which before had been of leather. And on this money there was therefore afterwards made, on one side, a ship, because Saturn had gone to Italy by sailing, and on the other a head with two faces, because such was the image of Janus, as we shall see later. These two kings together built towns and fortified places nearby, which they named after themselves: Saturnia after Saturn, and Janiculum after Janus.

<aside>Sterculius</aside>

So highly was Saturn esteemed by those peoples that, together with their king, they began to revere him as a god, because in those days those were held to be gods who knew how to discover, and taught there, some art useful to human life; and this art of cultivating the earth, and of making it more fruitful by skill than it is by its own nature, is most useful; and Saturn therefore deserved sacred honours for it, and was called Sterculius, from manuring (*stercorare*) the fields, that is, from giving them dung, whence they afterwards become more fertile. For this reason some have held that his statue had the sickle in its hand, to give us to understand that the cultivation of the fields was taught by him in Italy from the beginning, since with the sickle one reaps the grain produced by well-cultivated fields.

> Image of Saturn, or of Time devouring his children, that is, consuming everything, except Jupiter, Juno, Neptune and Pluto, understood as the four elements, fire, air, water and earth, which are not destroyed.

<aside>Macrobius</aside>

In the sacrifices of the Saturnalia lit candles were also used; explaining which, Macrobius says that it was because under Saturn's rule men passed from a rude life full of darkness to the bright and beautiful knowledge of the good arts.

<aside>Saturn as time</aside>
<aside>Image of Saturn</aside>

Besides this, the ancients understood time under the name of Saturn, about which the Latins gave many reasons, all consistent with time, but not bearing on our subject. And the Greeks likewise called him Cronus, which means time; and what the name signifies was shown in the image of this god; for they nearly always depicted him as an old man, badly dressed, with nothing on his head, with a sickle in one hand, and in the other he had a certain thing wrapped in a cloth, which he seemed to thrust into his throat, as if he wished to devour it; and four little boys were there beside him.

<aside>Exposition of Saturn</aside>

These things are interpreted in this way: Time is old and badly dressed because it either has always been, or began to be together with the world, that is, when, after the separation of Chaos, the elements were distinguished, and a beginning was given to the generation of things, Heaven then beginning to revolve around us, from whose movement men likewise began to measure time; and hence it was that the fables among the Greeks said that Saturn was the son of Uranus, which means Heaven.

He was also called Vitisator, as if a cultivator of vines, because they say that, having passed into Italy, as has been said, and having been received by the Latins, he had by the daughter of one of them, named Oenotria, some sons, among whom Janus is counted; to whom he taught the way to plant and cultivate the vine, and to make wine; which they having done, and having gained from it the name of inventors, it happened that one day some of them, who had perhaps drunk more than was fitting for them, fell asleep and slept a very long sleep, from which, when they woke and realised that this had come about through the wine they had drunk, believing it to be something poisonous, they stoned and killed Janus, as its inventor; for which reason four daughters of his who were left, out of grief, tying a rope about their necks, took their own lives; but they were placed by Saturn in Heaven in place of stars, and they appear to us a little before the time of the grape harvest.

Then, when the Romans were once afflicted with pestilence and had therefore consulted the oracle of Apollo, they received in reply that the wrath of Saturn, incurred through the death of his son Janus, had to be appeased first; moved by which, the Romans built him a temple on the Tarpeian Rock, and set up in it Janus with four faces, either from the number of the daughters, or from the four seasons of the year.

<aside>When history began</aside>

The ancients used to place on the top of the temple of Saturn a Triton with the buccina (a sounding shell) at his mouth, and to bury the tail of it there underground, wishing by this to show, as Macrobius says, that with Saturn history began to be made manifest and to be known, because without doubt, before the times were distinguished, it could be nothing but mute and unknown; which the hiding of the tail signified.

Saturn was dressed so shabbily because in that beginning of the world people did not seek pomp in clothes, but were content to be covered. Or else the clothes appeared to be all worn out in order to suit his old age better. He had his head bare because in those first times, when he was believed to govern everything, and the age of gold was running, truth was open and manifest to all, and not hidden, as it was afterwards under so many lies and so many deceits. And for this reason also the ancients sacrificed to Saturn with the head uncovered, and covered it when sacrificing to the other gods.

<aside>Fable of Saturn</aside>

The sickle in the hand of Saturn shows that time reaps and cuts down all things. And what he puts to his mouth in order to devour it shows that all things born in time are also devoured by time; on which the ancients invented a fable of this kind. Saturn, fearing to be driven out of the kingdom by one of his sons, as the Fates had predicted to him, commanded Ops, who was also called Rhea, his wife, that every time she gave birth she should present to him at once what she had borne, because he did not want in any way that any male son should be raised, even if he himself had to devour them all.

Ops bore Jupiter and Juno together the first time; but she presented Juno alone to her husband, knowing that, being female, she would not be harmed by him, and hid Jupiter; Saturn, having noticed this, began to cry out to have him; whereupon Ops presented to him a certain stone wrapped in a cloth, saying that it was the son whom he demanded. And he, without looking at what it was, thrust it down his throat and devoured it; but he threw it up again afterwards, as he also did with the sons, whom, after having devoured them, he threw up again.

<aside>Stone devoured by Saturn</aside>

Hence we read in Pausanias that at Delphi, in the temple of Apollo, there was a stone, not very large, guarded with the greatest respect, because those people said that it was the stone which was devoured by Saturn in place of Jupiter; and every day, but more on feast days, they poured oil on it, then wound unwashed wool around it. And the Romans believed it to be the one that on the Capitol would not yield to Jupiter, and was worshipped as the god Terminus.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

Neptune was likewise saved by his mother with a similar trick: she pretended to have borne a little foal, and gave it to her husband to devour, as the people of Arcadia said, and Pausanias reports it. Pluto, too, was saved because he was born in the same birth with his sister Glauca, who alone was presented to the father, who, apart from these, devoured all the other sons, throwing them up again afterwards, as I have said.

> Image of Saturn, of Time, and of the year, which signifies the sad effects that come from this planet, and the renewal of the year; with the coldness and slowness of the planet Saturn.

But some others, who also seem to me to explain better the cause of the devouring of the children, say that since Titan, the brother of Saturn, was older than he and therefore wished to reign, Saturn, at the persuasion of his mother and sisters, would in no way consent to it, but instead made himself king. As discord was about to arise from this between the brothers, they finally settled on this condition: that Saturn should continue in the kingdom, but should put to death all the sons born to him male, so that Titan might be sure that the kingdom would in the end fall back to him or to his sons.

For a time Saturn carried out the condition, and for this reason it is said that he devoured his sons; but Jupiter and Juno having been born to him in one birth, there followed with them, and then with Neptune, and likewise with Pluto, what was said above. When Titan learned of this, he attacked his brother Saturn so suddenly that he took him prisoner together with his wife, and so he held them until Titan was overcome by Jupiter and they were released and freed.

All these things are meant to show, as I began to say above, that all things produced by time are also consumed by time, which then makes them be born again as well, except for the elements, which are the four sons, Jupiter, Juno, Pluto and Neptune, that is fire, air, earth and water, which did not pass through the voracious throat, because these last forever.

<aside>Image of Saturn</aside>

Those of Saxony, wishing to describe Saturn, depicted an old man standing upright on a fish, and holding a vessel and a wheel; but what this was meant to signify has always been a secret, and therefore I too do not explain it here. Martianus Capella, describing Saturn, shows him holding out with his right hand a serpent which bites its tail, showing in this way that time is understood by him; and he says that he goes with a slow and sluggish step, and has his head covered with a veil that is turning green, the hair and the beard all white, and although he is so old, it seems nevertheless that he can become a child again.

<aside>Exposition</aside>

This, one may say, is the renewal that time makes from year to year; and therefore the green veil over the white hair shows the beginning of the year, when in spring all the earth turns green, which in winter is then covered with whitest snow, and one passes so quickly from one season to the other that they seem to be joined together.

The slowness of the step can be referred to the slow revolution made by the sphere of Saturn, which of the seven spheres of the planets is the largest, because it is above all the others; and therefore, more than the others, it is slow in completing its circuit, which takes thirty years. And because sad effects come from this planet for the most part, they depicted him old, sad, squalid, with his head wrapped, lazy and slow, because his nature is cold, dry and wholly melancholy, as can be seen in those who write about these things.

> Image of Saturn, signifying time present, past and future, the evil nature of this planet, its coldness, and time consuming and destroying everything.

<aside>Martianus Capella</aside>

Therefore the same Martianus Capella, when in *The Marriage of Mercury and Philologia* he describes her ascending from Heaven to Heaven, says that, having come to that of Saturn, she found him there, dwelling in a cold place, all frozen and covered with frost and snow, and having as adornment of his head at one time a serpent, at another the head of a lion, and at another that of a boar, which showed terrible teeth. These three heads could perhaps show the effects of time, which I do not affirm, because I do not find it written by an author worthy of belief.

I will say, however, that to this there fits well that image signifying the three times, past, present and future, which likewise had three heads, of a lion, a dog and a wolf, placed by the Egyptians with the idol of Serapis, their principal god, which I shall depict later in its place.

<aside>Eusebius. Image of Saturn</aside>

Now let us see what is read in Eusebius about the effects of time shown in the image of Saturn. He writes that Astarte, daughter of Heaven, sister of Saturn, and his wife together with the many others that he had, made for her husband a royal ornament that had four eyes, two in front and two behind, of which two closed and slept by turns, so that two were always open; and on the shoulders she placed likewise four wings, of which two stood stretched out, as if he were flying, and two folded and gathered in, as if he were standing still; wishing to signify that although he sleeps, he yet sees, and that while he keeps watch he sleeps likewise, and that standing still he flies, and flying he stands still; all things proper to time.

Eusebius then adds that the same Astarte placed two wings on the head of Saturn, wishing by the one to show the excellence of the mind, and by the other the senses. For the natural philosophers say that the human soul, when it descends into the mortal body, brings with it from the sphere of Saturn the power of understanding and the faculty of reasoning, which it then shows both in the things that it comprehends with the mind alone, and in those that it knows through the senses.

<aside>Plato</aside>

I could say how the Platonists understood by Saturn the pure mind, which is almost always wholly intent on the contemplation of divine things, whence the occasion arose of saying that in his time was the age of gold, and a quiet and happy life: such being precisely the life of anyone who seeks to lay down the weight of earthly passions, and to raise himself, as much as he can, to the consideration of the things of Heaven. I would say also that Plato often takes him for that supreme intelligence which provides for the being, the life and the order of all things.

<aside>Saturn with feet bound</aside>

But this contributes nothing to the image of this god, so I leave it, and come to say that the ancients made him, as Macrobius writes, with his feet bound with woollen thread, and kept him so all the year, except that they then untied him in December, on certain days that were consecrated to him, wishing in this way to show that the creature in its mother's womb is bound with tender and soft knots, which are loosened when, in the tenth month, the time of the mature birth has already come.

> Image of Saturn and of Time, who, with his feet bound with woollen thread, signifies that the vengeance and punishment of God are slow, awaiting amendment. It also denotes that the reckoning of birth and the production of things go together, joined.

Hence, Macrobius says, was born among the Latins that proverb that the gods have feet of wool. But some have interpreted it also in this way, that the divine goodness does not run in haste, nor with noise, to punish the one who errs, but goes slow and tardy, and so silently that the sinner does not become aware of it until he feels the punishment.

It is also said that Saturn stood with his feet bound either because all the things produced in this world seem to be knotted together (so they come one after the other), or because nature, by a certain and ordered law, holds the times so bound together that they never cease to go on succeeding one another.

<aside>Chiron the Centaur</aside>

And because the times run away most swiftly, the fables perhaps invented that Saturn changed himself into a horse, a most swift animal, when, having enjoyed Philyra, a most beautiful nymph, of whom Chiron the very learned Centaur was afterwards born, he was suddenly surprised, without noticing it, by his wife, from whom he freed himself in that way, by becoming a horse and running away. Whence Virgil, when he describes a beautiful horse, says that

> Such was Saturn once, when he wished,
> changed into a fine steed, to flee his wife.
> Whence swiftly he went over the high mountains,
> and at times, tossing his head high and shaking
> his hard mane, he made the high caves
> resound with fierce neighing.
>
> *(Virgil)*

<aside>Janus</aside>

But all this would be more fitting for one who wished to expound the fables of the gods of the ancients than for one who, like me, wishes to draw their images. Therefore I leave it, and, having no other image of Saturn left to draw, I shall speak of Janus, his companion; for, as I have already said, the histories hold that both reigned together for a time in Italy, and Macrobius writes that Janus was the first who began there to build sacred temples in honour of the gods, and ordained the manner of sacrificing to them.

<aside>Janus invoked in all sacrifices</aside>

Therefore he too was afterwards worshipped as a god, and for him, as the discoverer of sacrifices, they observed this ceremony: the ancient Romans never sacrificed to any god whatever without first invoking him. This was done also because they believed that Janus stood continually at the gates of Heaven, so that the prayers of mortals could not pass to the other gods unless he gave them entrance.

<aside>Prayers, what they are like</aside>

And perhaps it was needful that he should also give them a hand and help them to walk, because prayers, whom Homer depicts as women, are lame, as the same poet describes them. Hence it happens that when one wishes to pray, one bends the knees, for one goes to pray with a doubtful mind, not knowing whether one will obtain that for which one prays. They have, moreover, a sad face and squinting eyes, since it seems that one cannot look straight on, nor with a cheerful face, at those whom one has already offended, when with prayers one asks pardon of them.

<aside>Gates of Heaven</aside>

The gates of Heaven are two: one of the East, through which the Sun enters, when he comes to give light to the world; the other of the West, through which he goes out, when he gives place to night. Whoever, therefore, understands the Sun by Janus, as Macrobius does, says that he has the guard of the gates of Heaven, because entering and going out is free to him.

<aside>Image of Janus</aside>

And for this reason they depicted him with two faces, showing that the Sun has no need to turn back to see the one part and the other of the world. And they put in his hand a rod and a key; so that by the former it should be known that the Sun governs and tempers the world, and by the latter that he opens it when the day comes to illuminate it, and shuts it when, departing, he leaves it to be shadowed by the night. He also had twelve altars under his feet, which signified twelve colonies that he established, or, according to some, which is perhaps more true, the twelve months of the year.

<aside>Portunus</aside>

From this it came about, too, that Janus was believed to be one and the same numen as Portunus, who was held to be a guardian god and keeper of the gates; and therefore the ancients put a key in the hand of the latter, as in that of Janus. From him came another numen, that of the hinges (*cardini*, or *gangheri*, as we would call them) of the doors.

<aside>Crane</aside>

For Ovid relates that Janus, having fallen in love with a nymph called Crane, managed so that he gathered the fruits of love, and in recompense gave her to be over the hinges of the doors, and to have entire dominion over them, so that they opened and shut as it pleased her. And he gave her also a rod of hawthorn called the Janual rod, with which the Striges were driven away from those houses where there were little babies in cradles.

<aside>The goddess Cardea</aside>
<aside>Ovid</aside>

This nymph was afterwards called the goddess Carna, or Cardea; whose power, besides the hinges, extended also over the heart, the liver and the other inner organs of man. And it was the custom among the Romans to eat on the Calends of June, in honour of this goddess, the lard of a pig, either because they thought that with her favour it helped to keep man healthy, or because they wished in that way to renew the memory of the frugality of those good ancients, who were content with simple foods, as Ovid says.

I do find that a temple was built for her on the Caelian Hill in Rome by that Brutus who feigned madness until the occasion came for him to drive out the wicked king Tarquin, as though through her he had succeeded happily in dissembling what he had in his heart; but whether an idol was made of her, and what it was like, I have not yet found. So I have recounted all these things about her so that whoever should presume to make one may have something of which to compose it.

> Images of Janus, understood also as the Sun, as Time, as the god of the year and of peace; they signify also the two lights of our soul, the divine light and the natural light.

<aside>The god Forculus</aside>
<aside>The god Limentinus</aside>
<aside>St Augustine</aside>

They had also the god Forculus, to whom were entrusted the doors, which, turning upon the hinges, open and shut (called by the Latins *fores*), and Limentinus, god of the threshold (*limitare*), or *soglia*, as we say, of the door. Whence St Augustine, mocking them, says that a single doorkeeper, one man alone, does all that they have three gods do together, namely the goddess Cardea, Forculus and Limentinus.

Now I return to Janus, that is, to the Sun, who not only opens in the morning and shuts in the evening the day, as I said, but does the same through the whole year as well; for he opens it when in spring he makes the earth begin to produce herbs and flowers, and, all joyful, spread wide her ample bosom; and shuts it again in winter, when she, deprived of all her adornment, contracts into herself, and stands covered with snow and ice.

<aside>Faces of Janus, what they signify</aside>
<aside>Bede</aside>

The two faces of Janus show also the time that is continually coming: therefore the one is young, and this is what has already passed [?1], and the other is of greater age and bearded. Pliny writes that Numa, king of the Romans, made a statue of Janus with the fingers of the hands so arranged that they showed three hundred and sixty-five, so that it might be known thereby that he was the god of the year: for the year has as many days as he showed with his hands: since the ancients, bending the fingers or extending them in different ways, showed all the numbers that they wished, as can be seen in the Blessed Bede, who devotes a little book to it.

<aside>Suda</aside>

Suda also reports that, in order to show that Janus is the same as the year, some placed in his right hand three hundred, and in the left sixty-five; and that others gave him the key in the right hand, to make him known as the beginning of time and the doorkeeper of the year.

<aside>M. Tullius</aside>
<aside>Faces of Janus. Plutarch</aside>

The people of Phoenicia, as Marcus Tullius writes and Macrobius reports, thought that Janus was the World; and therefore, when they wished to make his image, they depicted the serpent that bites its tail and devours it; because the world feeds on itself and keeps turning always in itself, as the birth of things shows us, and their death, and that the same things are afterwards born again. Of the two faces of Janus Plutarch says that they showed that he (whether he were the Genius of the country, or a king among those most ancient peoples) changed the rough and savage way of living into a domestic and civil one, drawing the order of human life from one form into another.

<aside>Living images of the gods</aside>

Others hold that the two faces of Janus show the prudence of wise kings and of discerning princes, who, besides knowing how to dispose of the present with the best counsel, have a face in front as well because they see from afar and know how to recognise things before they happen; and they have one behind likewise, because they keep the past in mind, so that they see everything. And princes are shown in this way [?2], because, as Plutarch says, they are among mortals the living images of the gods.

<aside>Antevorta and Postvorta</aside>

And as the ancient Romans worshipped Antevorta and Postvorta, companions of the Divinity, the one because she knew the future, the other the past, understanding thereby that the divine wisdom knows everything, so in the image of Janus the two faces show the prudence of the king, from whom there must be hidden none of those things that are needed for the good government of peoples.

Some have also said that Janus was believed by the ancients to have been Chaos, that confusion of all things which was before the world was made, and that for this reason he has that face bearded, dreadful and dark, and has the other young, beautiful and cheerful, which shows the beauty that came from the distinction of things and from the marvellous order given to the universe; and that therefore he was worshipped as the god of beginnings, to whom the commencements of things were consecrated.

<aside>Faces of Janus in the soul</aside>
<aside>Platonists</aside>

But closing the eyes of the head and opening those of the intellect, let us consider a little the image of Janus with the two faces in relation to the human soul, as briefly as possible, yet in such a way that everyone can understand it. Our soul, according to the opinion of the Platonists, as soon as it has come out of the hands of God, by a certain natural movement of its own turns toward him, like a loving daughter who longs to see her Father again.

This desire is as proper and natural to it as it is for flame to ascend always, its nature drawing it toward the place from which its birth and beginning come; and because fire on earth is kindled by the virtue of the upper bodies, the flame, as far as it can, always tends toward them; so the soul, which feels itself created by God, turns toward him and desires him.

But this desire, or light, whichever we wish to call it, does not endure in it always in one and the same way, because the more it unites with her [?3], the less resplendent it becomes, and so it comes to be equal to her very self, whence she sees nothing any more except herself and the things down here, and no longer looks at God, nor at divine things. Yet she does not withdraw from those so far that she can no longer see them: rather that first desire, which appeared in her and then hid itself, if some little divine light presents itself to it, discloses itself immediately, and with this she returns to the consideration of the things of Heaven.

<aside>The soul has two lights</aside>

The soul, then, has two lights, the one natural, its own, and born with it, and with this it sees itself and knows the things of the world; the other divine, and infused by the goodness of God, under whose guidance it raises itself to Heaven, and there contemplates divine things. These two lights are recognised in the two faces of Janus; the divine in the young one, and in the old and bearded one the natural.

For the things produced here by nature change and grow old, and their consideration made by the natural light alone has something of the murky and the obscure; therefore the soul sees and gazes at them with the bearded face. And with the other, which is young and polished, our soul, guided by the divine light all clear and resplendent, goes to behold the eternal God of the blessed souls and the celestial circles, which things never change, and always preserve the beauty of their youth.

<aside>Ovid</aside>

Much more could be said about the soul, drawing it to this image of two faces. But because these things are a little too obscure, I leave them for now, and reserve myself to speak of them in another place, if perhaps I should ever manage to put together a certain fable of the soul, which I have already gathered in several pieces. The ancients also made the image of Janus with four faces, because such a statue was once found in a certain place in Tuscany. And this showed very well that whoever made it took Janus for the year, which has four faces, because four are the seasons that make it change face and aspect: Spring, Summer, Autumn and Winter. These the ancients likewise painted with different faces and garments, as Ovid sketches them briefly when he describes the royal throne of Phoebus, saying that there was:

> Spring, crowned with flowers,
> naked Summer with her hair girt with ears of grain,
> Autumn with feet stained with pressed grapes,
> and Winter, frozen, dreadful and sad.
>
> *(Ovid)*

The seasons of the year are also sometimes shown in this way: Venus is put for Spring, Ceres for Summer, Bacchus for Autumn, and for Winter at times Vulcan, who stands at the burning forge, and at times the winds with Aeolus their king, because these produce the storms, which in winter are more frequent than in the other seasons.

> Images of the four seasons of the year, denoting their effects and occupations, with the animals sacred to them, which also show the nature of the season.

<aside>Temple of Janus</aside>

Twelve altars were also placed under the feet of Janus, by which were understood the twelve months of the year, or else the twelve signs of the Zodiac traversed by the Sun throughout the whole year. And in Rome there was a temple of his which had four doors, and four columns supported the vault above, in each of which there were niches with figures representing the months, which are divided into the four seasons of the year.

<aside>Patulcius. Clusius</aside>
<aside>Gates of war. Virgil</aside>

At first his temple had only two doors, when it was built by Numa, before which Janus sat on a fine royal throne, and was called there Patulcius and Clusius, from two Latin words, of which one means to open, the other to shut, because both were believed to come from his hand, as I have already said; and these were called the gates of war, about which Virgil wrote thus:

> The gates of war, which were called
> so by the ancients, are two,
> and out of religion and out of respect
> for fierce Mars once, sacred and dreadful,
> which a hundred hard and thick iron bars
> keep shut with marvellous strength;
> and before them stands, as guardian,
> Janus, who with two faces watches over both.
> To these, after some war had been
> decided on by the Senate, girded
> in the manner of the Sabine people
> with the fine royal purple mantle, went
> one of the consuls, and, opening them, heard
> the heavy creaking made by the hinges.
>
> *(Virgil)*

<aside>Ovid</aside>

The Senate, therefore, having resolved to make war, one of the consuls opened the doors already mentioned, and as long as the war lasted they stood so always, and when it was finished they were shut at once. This was ordained by Numa, and afterwards always observed by a certain law, as Plutarch writes. Hence it was said that he had peace and war in his hand, as he himself says in Ovid, when the poet asks him the reason for his festivals, since his temple, open, showed war, and shut, showed peace.

<aside>M. Tullius</aside>

There are many reasons for this, but for now let us say only this: that Janus was believed by many to be Heaven (as Marcus Tullius also holds), which, revolving around, is the cause of the conjunctions of aspects and of the other positions of the stars, from which we are inclined to many of the actions that we perform, and therefore it is often said that many changes in human affairs come from Heaven; among which peace and war may be reckoned. And this was perhaps the mystery among the Romans of opening and shutting the temple of Janus.

> Temple of Janus, god of peace and of war, which stood shut in time of peace and open in war, understood as Heaven, which, revolving, influences now peace, now war.

Of him we read also that there were several statues of him in a certain place in the City, where usurers ordinarily conducted their business; for he, who was believed to be the god of beginnings, was also held to be the master of the Calends, which are the first days of the months, whence he was also called Juno, since these days were likewise consecrated to Juno, and on the Calends usurers were accustomed to collect their interest.

Besides this, those arches too which in the pomps of triumphs were erected through the City with four faces, in the likeness of the temple which I said had four doors, were called Jani. Hence Suetonius, speaking of the pride and vainglory of Domitian, says that he erected through the City many Jani with triumphal ornaments.

> Image of the Sun or Phoebus, and of Jupiter, held by the Assyrians to be one and the same thing, understood by them as the soul of the world, their power being joined together.

## Doubtful passages

- [?1] Scan 0076: “the one is young, and this is what has already passed [?], and the other is of greater age and bearded” — the logic is broken (the young face is the past); a shift in the typesetting is likely: “quello, che già è passato” seems to refer to the time that the faces show. Translated as printed, with [?].
- [?2] “Princes are shown in this way [?]” (scan 0076, “Et questo fù così mostrato da i Principi”) — the construction “mostrato da i Principi” is unclear; translated by sense: it is in the image that Janus is shown two-faced precisely as a prince.
- [?3] “the more it unites with her [?]” (scan 0077, “quanto più si vnisce con lei”) — the subject is unclear: whether the desire unites with the soul, or the soul with the body; translated literally. Further on, “si fà eguale a se medesima” is feminine, so the subject may also be the soul; the translation “it comes to be equal to her very self” fixes one of the readings.
- Scan 0069: the source has a mark “hò[?] detto”; translated as “as I have said”; the printer’s mark was not carried into the translation.
- Scan 0067: “vi poserò Giano” — a misprint for “posero”; translated as “set up”.
- Scan 0081: in the Virgil verses the text has “Sabino” (in the Latin original the reference is to the Gabine way of girding); as printed — “Sabine”. Not corrected.
- Scan 0079: after “ne gli altri tempi” there is no full stop before “furono anco posti sotto i piedi di Giano”; the sentence is divided by sense.
- Scan 0081: Janus “was also called Juno” (“Giunone”) — a slip of the author; not corrected.
- Scan 0074: “verga Gianale” rendered as “Janual rod” (provisionally); “Streghe” — “Striges” (per the glossary).
- Scan 0073: the Virgil verses on Saturn as a horse (6 lines) are set in italics in the source with no indication of rhyme; translated without rhyme, the author being named in the text.
- Engraving captions (0065, 0068, 0070, 0072, 0075, 0078, 0080) have been moved to follow the relevant paragraph, since they stood in the middle of sentences; the purely decorative vignette of scan 0080 is omitted.
- The engraving of scan 0082 (“Image of the Sun or Phoebus, and of Jupiter among the Assyrians”) stands before the heading “Apollo”; by content it belongs to the beginning of the chapter on Apollo (the account of the Assyrians), but it is left at the end of this chapter, as was done with the engraving of Vertumnus in the chapter “Neptune”.
- The marginal note “GIANO” on scan 0073 is rendered as the note “Janus” — it is a section heading within the chapter; there is no separate section with a heading in the file.
- The marginal note on scan 0071, “Eusebio. Imagine di Saturno.”, is rendered as a single note.
- The author’s explanations and etymologies (Latium from *latente*, Sterculius from *stercorare*, Janus–Juno, and others) were not corrected.
