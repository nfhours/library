# Minerva

Source: books/cartari/_text/TEXT_reading.md, scans 0337–0381
Rules: books/cartari/_translation/en/RULES.md

---

It is said that among the marvellous things given by God to human nature, two are especially wonderful: one is speech, the other the use of the hands. For the former, expressing the thoughts of the mind with marvellous force, persuades others of whatever it wishes; the latter, with great skill, puts to work everything that can preserve the lives of men and defend them, as do all the arts already discovered or that will be discovered in the future.

And since fine speech is not beneficial but rather harmful and does damage whenever it is not accompanied by good will and prudence, and prudence cannot be of use to the world when it does not know how to persuade others to flee evil, follow good, and do the things that civil life requires, the ancients showed this by joining together Mercury, of whom I have already spoken, and Minerva, of whom I shall speak now, who was held to be the goddess of prudence and the inventor of all the arts.

<aside>Hermathena</aside>

And so, joining the statues of both these gods, they made one of them and called it by the Greek word Hermathena, because the Greeks call Mercury Hermes and Minerva Athena. They kept it in the Academies, to show those who trained there that eloquence and prudence must be joined together, since the latter by itself is of little benefit, and the former by itself likewise often does harm, and perhaps always, as Marcus Tullius discusses at considerable length at the beginning of the *On Invention*. He also writes to his friend Atticus about the statue I mentioned, in this way:

> Your Hermathena pleases me very much, and is so well placed in the Academy that she seems to hold all of it.
>
> *(Marcus Tullius)*

<aside>How Minerva is depicted; Minerva's eyes</aside>

If one wants to depict Minerva, then, whether alone or accompanied by Mercury, one should give her an almost masculine face, quite severe in appearance, with sky-blue eyes, for Homer always gives her this colour as her own. And Pausanias, after writing of a certain idol of Minerva that was in Athens in the temple of Vulcan, adds that he had found a certain story that makes her the daughter of Neptune, and that she had sky-blue eyes because those of her father were the same.

But Cicero, where he speaks of the nature of the gods, says that the eyes of Minerva were grey-blue (*caesii*), and those of Neptune sea-blue (*caerulei*), which might show some difference between them, but I do not think it was great, because in Latin one word and the other both mean a very light greenish colour, such as is seen in the eyes of cats and owls; unless they perhaps mean to say that in these eyes of Minerva there was a more fiery gleam, like that shown by the eyes of lions.

<aside>Minerva armed</aside>
<aside>Ovid</aside>

Let her likewise be depicted armed, with a long spear in her hand and a crystal shield on her arm, as in Ovid she depicts herself when she competes with Arachne at embroidery; and, following that design, he says:

> She portrays herself with spear and shield, and arms
> her head with a helmet, and her breast with a cuirass,
>
> *(Ovid)*

<aside>Minerva's helmet</aside>
<aside>Claudian</aside>

These things show the nature of the prudent man, as I shall say later. Claudian and others also described Minerva in the same way, perhaps taking the likeness from Homer, as they have often done with many other things. In Homer, when she goes, persuaded by Juno, to help the Greeks against Mars, who was then fighting for the Trojans, she is described in the form of a valiant woman warrior and given a helmet on her head, all gilded, because the mind of a shrewd man, armed with wise counsels, easily defends itself against whatever may do it harm, and shines out entirely in the fine and worthy works that it performs. And the gold on Minerva's helmet also means that she is often taken for the divine splendour that illuminates human intellects, and from which all prudence and all knowledge come.

<aside>Birth of Minerva</aside>

It was also feigned that Minerva was born from the head of Jupiter, as Pausanias writes, saying that there was an idol of it on the citadel of Athens. Vulcan opened Jupiter's head with a sharp axe of diamond, and Minerva was born without the help of Jupiter's wife, because the intellective faculty of the soul resides in the brain; and it, and all its knowledge, descends from the supreme intellect, which is Jupiter, since all wisdom comes from God and is born from the mouth of the Most High, not from these low and earthly things, signified by Juno.

<aside>Against women</aside>

And this is a better interpretation than the one given by Martianus Capella in contempt of women. Since he was perhaps not too much their friend, he says that Minerva is feigned to have been born without a mother because women have no counsel nor any prudence; or perhaps he said so in order to follow Aristotle, who writes in his moral works that women have no good counsel at all.

<aside>Women: in their defence</aside>

I do not dare to oppose him, but I do say that many women in our times show themselves so prudent and shrewd that they make him a liar. And were it not that their worth makes them quite well known to the world, I would, by naming names, give endless examples of their good sense and prudence, showing what others have perhaps not wished to see: namely, that although Minerva was born without the help of a woman, she was nevertheless born a woman, and so it is only right that it should befit women more than men.

> Image of Vulcan, who with a diamond axe opened the head of Jupiter, from whom Minerva is born, goddess of wisdom, which denotes that all knowledge comes from God and that the intellective faculty resides in the brain. It also signifies that in women there is neither counsel nor knowledge.

<aside>Sphinx with Minerva</aside>

Besides this, they covered her head with a helmet to give us to understand that the prudent man does not always reveal everything he knows; he does not make his counsel known to everyone, nor does he always speak in a way that everyone understands, but only by someone who is like him, as affairs require; so that his words then seem to others like the tangled sayings of the Sphinx.

This is perhaps why in a certain part of Egypt they set a Sphinx in front of the temple of Minerva, who was worshipped there and believed to be Isis, although we also read that this was done to show that matters of religion must stay hidden under sacred mysteries, so that they are no more understood by the common people than the riddles of the Sphinx were.

<aside>Griffins</aside>

Pausanias writes that in Athens there was an idol of Minerva that had on its helmet, in the middle, by way of a crest, as one might say, the Sphinx, and on either side two griffins, which are neither beasts nor birds but share in both, for they have the head of an eagle, and wings, and are lions in the rest.

<aside>Arimaspians</aside>

These fierce and terrible animals (if indeed any are to be found, since Pliny believes them fabulous) are found in Scythia, where they guard the gold mines, as Dionysius Afer writes, so that the Arimaspians, a people of that country who have only one eye in the forehead, cannot gather the gold without great danger, and there is therefore almost continual war between them. From this one can see what guard each person should keep over his own mind, lest the Arimaspians come to rob him of its mines.

The ancients also sometimes put the cock on Minerva's helmet, as a certain statue of hers made by Phidias for the Eleans, of gold and ivory, showed; Pausanias seems to believe that this was because the cock is bold and fierce, as one must be in wars; but let us add that it also showed the vigilance that wise and valiant captains must have. For they believed that Minerva had charge no less of the arts of war than of those of peace, and so they depicted her armed, as I said.

<aside>Pallas</aside>
<aside>Palladium</aside>

And the fables feigned that she killed with her own hand Pallas, a most ferocious giant, from whom some held that she was afterwards called Pallas. And some others say that she was so called from a certain Greek word meaning to move or to shake, because her statue was made in such a way that it seemed to shake the spear it held in its hand, in the likeness of the Palladium, a wooden idol of this goddess, which truly did shake it by itself and moved its eyes, and was believed to have descended from Heaven, as I said in the image of Vesta. In Vesta's temple it was guarded so secretly that not only could no one touch it, but no one could even see it other than the one of the Vestal Virgins to whom this charge was given.

<aside>Tritonia</aside>

And Minerva was at first surnamed Tritonia, whether from a certain marsh in Libya of this name, whose daughter some have afterwards made her, perhaps because she was first seen there; or because the parts of wisdom are to know present things, foresee those that are to come, and remember those that are past; or because the wise man must above all do three things: counsel well, judge rightly, and act with justice.

<aside>Minerva</aside>

I leave aside the other reasons that are read for this name, because they are of no use for what I have to say, just as it is of little use to report that Minerva was called so either from admonishing (*ammonire*), because the wisdom signified by her always gives good admonitions, or from diminishing (*minuire*) and lessening the strength of those who are always intent on continual study, or from threatening (*minacciare*), because as goddess of war, and armed, she always seemed terrible and menacing.

<aside>Caesar</aside>
<aside>Bellona</aside>

Nevertheless this last one also suits my purpose quite well, because some have held that Minerva was the same as Bellona, who was likewise worshipped as goddess of wars. And Caesar writes that in Cappadocia they held her in such great reverence that those peoples wanted her priest to be the first after the king in authority and power, since it seemed to them that the majesty of the goddess deserved it.

<aside>Statius</aside>

But from what the images show, one can say that between Minerva and Bellona there was this difference: that Minerva showed the shrewd foresight, the good government and the wise counsel that prudent and valiant captains use in waging war, and Bellona the killings, the fury, the slaughter and the ruin that are seen in feats of arms, because the poets depict her as the charioteer of Mars, as Statius does when he says:

> With a bloody hand Bellona guides
> the fierce steeds, and strikes, and lashes.
>
> *(Statius)*

<aside>Silius Italicus</aside>

And they depict her for the most part spattered with blood, which is why Silius Italicus shows her running through the armed squadrons, and describes her thus:

> She shakes the blazing torch and her fair hair
> spattered with much blood, and goes running,
> great Bellona, through the armed squadrons.
>
> *(Silius Italicus)*

<aside>Statius</aside>

Nevertheless Statius gives the same force to Minerva too, and depicts her as no less impetuous and violent than Bellona, when he has Tydeus, praying to her, speak thus:

> O fierce goddess, honour of the great father,
> terrible mistress of wars,
> whose head is adorned with a lovely horror
> by the strong helmet, and whose breast by the Gorgon,
> spattered with blood, and than whose force no greater
> is possessed by Mars or by Bellona
> in battles, accept now my vow,
> which I offer, humble and devoted, to your numen.
>
> *(Statius)*

<aside>Blood spilled for Bellona</aside>
<aside>Lycophron</aside>

So Bellona was among the ancients a goddess wholly full of wrath and fury, and they believed that she took great delight in seeing human blood spilled, which is why in her sacrifices, instead of a victim, the priests themselves pricked their arms and shoulders with knives and appeased her with their own blood. She was depicted sometimes with a whip in her hand, with which she began fierce battles, and sometimes they also showed her with a trumpet at her mouth, as though she were giving the signal for the engagement; and at times they showed her with a burning torch in her hand. For we read in Lycophron that the ancients used, before trumpets were invented, when they were about to give battle, to send ahead of the armies some men with lit torches in their hands, which they hurled at one another from one side and the other, and then the bloody battle began.

<aside>Claudian</aside>

This is what Statius meant when he said that at the beginning of an engagement Bellona was the first to show the burning torch. And Claudian likewise spoke according to this custom of the ancients, saying:

> Tisiphone shakes the lit pine
> with a hand that always brings misery,
> and to the sad standards gathers
> the pale shades ready for battle.
>
> *(Claudian)*

> Image of Bellona, goddess of war and charioteer of Mars, and of her priests, who wound themselves, and of the pine sacred to her; she is understood as the bringer of killings, ruin and slaughter, the proper effects of wars.

<aside>Columna Bellica</aside>

We read also that before the temple of Bellona there stood a certain column, not very large, which the Romans called the Columna Bellica, because when they had decided to wage some war, one of the consuls went to it, after opening the temple of Janus, and from there hurled a spear towards the region where the enemy people were, and it was understood that war was then declared, as we should say. But before the Romans had so widened their borders, they declared war in this way. They sent to the enemy a priest appointed for the purpose, who there set out the just reasons they had for making war, and then hurled a spear at the heads of the enemy. War was also proclaimed and declared in other ways among the ancients, as I have already said in the image of Janus, and shall say in that of Mars, if it comes to the point.

<aside>Herodotus</aside>
<aside>Olive given to Minerva</aside>

And to conclude about Bellona, I say that she differed from Minerva at least in image. On Minerva's helmet, to return to her design, Apuleius places a wreath of olive, for this tree was given to her by the ancients as her own, because she was its discoverer, as Virgil too calls her, and as the fable tells of the contest between her and Neptune over the possession of Athens. On this Herodotus writes that it was the same olive that Minerva had caused to grow then, and that it burned together with the city when the Persians burned it, but that the same day it sprouted again and grew to the height of two cubits.

<aside>Minerva's lamp</aside>

And some say that this was feigned because Minerva was the first to show the way of pressing oil from olives, and also because the sciences cannot be acquired without frequent study and long vigils. Hence we read that in Athens [?1] a golden lamp was dedicated to this goddess, which burned continually, and oil was put into it no more than once a year; and this, says Pausanias, was because the wick was of a certain kind of flax that does not let itself be consumed by fire. The same writer recounts that among the Corinthians, Epopeus, having built a temple to Minerva for a certain victory, prayed her to show some sign of holding it dear, and that at once, there before the dedicated temple, a spring of oil burst out of the ground.

<aside>Arts of Minerva</aside>
<aside>Minerva with the distaff</aside>

From this one can see that the olive was rightly given to her, not only for the study of learning but also for the practice of the arts discovered by her, such as spinning, sewing, weaving, and doing other things that are proper to women. For these arts the Greeks had a large wooden statue of this goddess, which sat on a high seat and held a distaff in both hands. And the Romans, on a certain day of the festivals celebrated in March for Minerva, had the mistresses invite their maidservants to table and serve them with their own hands, as if they wished to show that they owed to her the benefit they drew from the servants by spinning, weaving, sewing and doing the other things of which she had been the inventor; and that the servants likewise had from her this reward for the labours endured all year long in the arts discovered by her.

<aside>Owl with Minerva</aside>

The owl too was sometimes placed on Minerva's helmet, as her own bird, and so loved by her that, whether on her head or at her feet, she nearly always has it with her. Some hold the reason for this to be that in Athens, a city dear to this goddess above all others (as shown by the name she had in common with it), because of the study of the sciences and good arts that all flourished there long ago, there was a great supply of these birds. Hence arose the proverb about bringing owls to Athens, said of those who want to give someone something of which he already has great abundance.

> Image of Minerva, inventor of spinning, weaving, sewing and other womanly pursuits; inventor of the olive, symbol of long and necessary study, with the birds sacred to her. The owl signifies the counsel of the prudent man, the cock the vigilance of the wise man and the courage of soldiers.

But the fables say that Minerva formerly loved the crow, having made it a bird from a beautiful young girl, which it had been at first, in order to defend her from the force of Neptune, who, in love with her, ran after her along the seashore. Minerva kept the crow in her service until it accused the daughters of Cercops [?2]; for the goddess, angered then by the bad office this bird had done, at once made it turn from white, as it was before, to black, as it is now, and drove it away from her, and in its place took the owl. From then on there was always, and there still lasts, a most grave enmity between these two birds.

<aside>Justin</aside>
<aside>What the owl signifies</aside>
<aside>Hiero</aside>

And the owl signifies the wise and good counsel of the prudent man, as we read in Justin: when an owl flew onto the spear of Hiero the first time he went to war, still a youth, it was interpreted that he would be of very shrewd counsel; and this was true, because he became king of Syracuse, although he was born of low station. And because the eyes of Minerva are of the same colour as those of the owl, which sees very well at night, it is understood that the wise man sees and knows things however difficult and hidden they may be, and that, having lifted from his mind the veil of lies, he penetrates to Truth with the sight of the intellect; for she lies hidden, and does not let herself be seen by everyone.

<aside>Democritus</aside>

That is why Democritus placed her in the depths of a well, saying that she never came out of it, unless Time, or Saturn her father (as Plutarch says), sometimes drew her out.

<aside>Hippocrates</aside>
<aside>Truth</aside>
<aside>Opinion</aside>

And Hippocrates, writing to a friend of his, depicts Truth in the form of a beautiful woman, tall, modestly adorned, and all bright and shining, but much more so in the eyes, because these seem two most luminous stars; and he adds afterwards of Opinion that she too is a woman, but not so beautiful, and yet not ugly, and that she shows herself wholly bold and quick to seize upon whatever is presented to her.

<aside>Epiphanius [?3]</aside>

In Epiphanius we read that some heretics depicted Truth with Greek letters in this way. They set α and ω as the head, and β and ψ as the neck, and so, coming down, they formed the whole body, always taking those two letters that, in turn, are closest to the first and the last. And Philostratus, saying that Truth was painted in the sacred cave of Amphiaraus, shows her dressed in whitest garments, and in another place calls her the mother of Virtue.

<aside>Virtue</aside>
<aside>Honour</aside>
<aside>Valerius</aside>
<aside>Valerius Maximus</aside>

The ancients likewise believed Virtue to be a goddess, and worshipped her, and the Romans set up a temple to her, as to the other gods, in front of that of Honour; for of a single temple vowed to these two by Marcellus, as Valerius Maximus reports, two had to be made, because the pontiffs said that religion did not allow a single temple to be dedicated to two numina: since if some prodigy occurred in it, one could not know to which of them sacrifice should be made. So to Virtue and to Honour each was given its own, and no one could enter the latter except by passing through the former, wishing thereby to show that there is no other way to acquire honour than that of virtue, as though honour were the true reward of virtue, which was therefore depicted with two wings, since honour and glory, like very light wings, lift virtuous persons from the earth and carry them in flight to the no small wonder of everyone.

This was perhaps not so in the time of Lucian, as it has not been in other times either, not to speak of today's, when everyone sees only too well how matters stand. For in a certain dialogue of his he describes virtue as wholly sad and grieving, dressed in a few rags, and so badly treated by Fortune that she was even prevented from going to show herself to Jupiter. And I shall say this little about our own times too: that some have painted Virtue in the form of a pilgrim, as if she found no lodging here, and therefore went away.

We find also that the ancients depicted her in the manner of a matron sitting on a squared stone; and on a certain ancient medal virtue is seen in this way: a woman leaning with her left arm on a column, and holding a serpent in her right hand.

<aside>Virtue as male</aside>
<aside>Medal of Gordian</aside>
<aside>Medal of Numerian</aside>

Then virtue was also male, as on a medal of the emperor Gordian: shown as an old man, bearded, quite naked, leaning on a club, with the skin of a lion wrapped around one of his arms, and around him there are letters that say: To the virtue of Augustus. And a medal of Numerian also has the same figure.

<aside>Medal of Vitellius</aside>

But on one of Vitellius, Virtue is in the form of a young man dressed in short clothes, with a helmet on his head and a crest of some feathers. He holds his left hand raised, leaning on a spear planted upright in the ground, and rests his right hand, with a sceptre, on his right knee, raised higher than the other, because he has a tortoise under his foot, and he has little boots on his legs. He stands upright and looks fixedly at a young woman opposite him, representing Honour, who, raising her right arm, holds a spear like the other, and on this side is bare to below the breast. In her left hand she has the horn of plenty, and a helmet under her foot, and her head is adorned with beautiful blond tresses, which are wound about it in a pleasing manner.

> *Image of Virtue and Honour, who look at each other, so carved on a medal of Vitellius, denoting that from virtue and virtuous actions comes honour, and with honour abundance of everything and all human happiness.*

The philosopher Prodicus, as we read in Xenophon and as Marcus Tullius reports, feigned that Hercules, while he was a youth, went, I do not know how, to a certain deserted place, where he found two roads that went in different directions. Not knowing which of them he should take, while he stood in suspense and wholly pensive about it, two women appeared to him. One of them was Voluptuousness, fair to look at, all wanton and alluring through the artful adornments she had about her, who persuaded him to walk by the way of pleasures, wide at the beginning, level and easy, full of green grasses and coloured flowers, but narrow in the end, stony and full of the sharpest thorns. The other, more severe in appearance, simply dressed, was Virtue, who showed him her road at first narrow, steep [?4] and difficult, but afterwards leading into flowery meadows and most pleasant fields full of the sweetest fruits. And because Hercules joined himself to her, he got so glorious a name.

<aside>Dante</aside>

Dante, feigning in his *Purgatory* that he had seen Voluptuousness in a dream, describes her as a stammering woman, with squinting eyes, crooked feet, maimed hands and a pallid colour, who then began to speak fluently, straightened up all over, and coloured her faded face as love requires; and she would have drawn him to herself with her sweet words, had there not appeared a holy and honest woman, of whom he says:

> She seized the other, and opened her in front,
> rending her garments, and showed me the belly;
> that woke me with the stench that came out of it.
>
> *(Dante)*

<aside>Virgil</aside>

These things suit very well the ways of vicious pleasures and of virtue. But anyone who wished to show these two ways in yet another manner might depict the letter of Pythagoras, on which Virgil wrote those few verses, showing that it represented human life for us; they come to say this in our language:

> The letter once given to Pythagoras
> shows the form of human life,
> with the two horns into which it is divided.
> For on the right goes the steep ascent
> of virtue, by a narrow path,
> difficult at the beginning and ill-liked,
> but then easy for one who does not stray from the way,
> because, ascending, he comes to where are forgotten
> the labours left behind his back.
> On the left goes a wider way,
> easy and level, but which then leads a man
> to where there is only weeping and repentance.
> Therefore whoever restrains his desire
> and does not let it follow vain pleasure,
> which at first seems joy and in the end is pain,
> and follows virtue with a firm will
> to endure the hardships that fortune
> lays upon him on whom she ought least of all to lay them,
> gains so much honour that afterwards more than one
> age keeps memory of it, and makes his fame
> illustrious and clear, which would have been dark.
> But whoever holds only idleness and wantonness dear
> lives in blame, and that life in the end,
> which seemed so sweet to him, he feels bitter,
> and pricking thorns pierce his heart.
>
> *(Virgil)*

<aside>Honour</aside>

For worldly pleasures give in the end nothing but repentance and shame; but virtues, besides calming our mind within ourselves, also win us glory and honour among others. The ancients depicted the image of Honour, as Alciato describes it, as a boy dressed in a purple cloth, with a laurel wreath on his head, to whom the god Cupid gave his hand and seemed to lead him to the goddess Virtue, who went ahead.

<aside>Volupia</aside>

The ancients worshipped also a goddess of pleasures, whom they called Volupia, as Varro writes, and her statue was a woman pale in the face, who sat on a high seat in the manner of a queen, and seemed to hold Virtue under her feet.

> *Image of the goddess Volupia, goddess of pleasures, trampling the goddess Virtue under her feet, denoting the detestable and infamous life of those given to pleasures, wholly despisers of virtue, given only to every sort of vice, almost irrational.*

<aside>Angerona</aside>
<aside>Stimula</aside>
<aside>Horta</aside>

In the temple of this goddess Angerona was placed on an altar, likewise held to be a goddess of pleasure, or (as St Augustine reports from Varro) of doing, which the Latins call *agere*. That is how she got her name, because she seemed to move men to actions, as the goddess Stimula goaded them (*stimulaua*) and Horta exhorted them (*essortaua*). And, as Plutarch writes, the temple of Horta always stood open, so that she who constantly exhorted men to some worthy work should always be seen by everyone.

Some have also said of Angerona that she was so named from distress and affliction (*angore*), which she removed at once when sacred ceremonies were ordained to her, no less than to the other gods, bringing to an end the disease of the throat known as quinsy (*squilantia*), called *angina* by the Latins, which was killing a great number of people in Rome; and for this reason perhaps her idol had some cloth around its neck, which also bound its mouth. But Macrobius holds that Angerona, with her mouth bound and sealed, showed that whoever knows how to suffer and keep silent, concealing his troubles, overcomes them in the end and then enjoys a glad and pleasant life.

<aside>Silence necessary</aside>
<aside>Tacita</aside>

Pliny and Solinus write that this goddess was depicted thus to show that one should not speak of the secret mysteries of religion so as to divulge them; as Numa too wished to make known when he introduced the worship of a certain goddess named by him Tacita, since, as Plutarch writes, one must keep silent about the things of the gods.

<aside>Harpocrates</aside>

For this reason the Egyptians likewise worshipped the god of silence, and kept him in company with their chief gods. His name among them was Harpocrates, and among the Greeks Sigalion, and his statue, according to Apuleius and Martianus Capella, was of a youth who held his finger to his mouth, as one does when showing another by a sign that he should be silent. The god of silence was also sometimes depicted as a figure without a face, with a small cap on its head and a wolf's skin around it; it was nearly all covered with eyes and ears, because one must see and hear a great deal but speak little. And anyone can always keep silent when he pleases, but cannot always say what he wishes; which is shown by the cap, a sign of liberty, as has been said elsewhere.

> *Image of the goddess Angerona, held by some to be the goddess of pleasure and of human actions, and also over the disease of the throat, of silence and of endurance, and image of the god of silence called Harpocrates or Sigalion.*

<aside>Wolf and silence</aside>
<aside>Harpocrates' peach tree</aside>

Of the wolf we read that it makes hoarse anyone it sees before it is seen, and that when it has seized something it flees away so silently that it scarcely dares to breathe. To Harpocrates the peach tree was dedicated, because this tree has leaves like the human tongue and its fruits resemble the heart, as though the tongue revealed what is in the heart, though it ought not to do so, if one considers it well.

<aside>Crow driven away by Minerva</aside>
<aside>Crow in Minerva's hand</aside>

And so keeping silent at its proper times is a virtue, as Minerva showed by driving away from her the crow, a garrulous and talkative bird; for the prudent man must not waste time in many vain words, but, keeping silent, must consider things very well before he reasons about them, and then say of them only what is needed. This perhaps is what the statue of this goddess among the Messenians was meant to show, which, as Pausanias describes it, held a crow in its hand, as though speech should be so in the hand of the wise man that he can loosen and tighten it as the occasion presents itself that need requires.

Minerva then had a long spear in her hand, as I said, which all the poets give her; and Apuleius likewise describes her as brandishing it with her hand and, raising her arm, lifting her shield, and two figures like boys go with her, who with bare knives in their hands seem to go threatening. One of them is Dread, the other Fear, because wars are never without these.

<aside>Statius</aside>
<aside>Terror</aside>

So Statius, feigning that Mars, commanded by Jupiter, goes to bring war between the Argives and the Thebans, says that he took Dread and Terror and made the latter go before him, and he sketches him in part and in part describes the effects that come from him, in this way:

> From the cruel rabble around him he chooses
> Terror, and sends him ahead on a steed,
> to whose power none is equal
> in making others fear, still less surpasses it.
> Because of him a man seems to despise the truth,
> if in the timid breast it happens to lodge,
> the horrible monster, who has infinite voices
> and hands always ready and bold for evil.
> The face is not always a single one,
> but many, and all in varied aspects,
> which change every hour, provided it pleases him
> to match them with the fearful words
> that he drives so hard into human hearts
> that they are compelled to give them every credence.
> And with so much Dread he often assails
> the cities, that they then believe every evil.
> They will believe that there is no longer one sun alone,
> and it will seem to them that they see what is not there,
> if wretched mortals to the words
> of tremendous Terror, rarely true,
> lend their ears, and that a storm-cloud steals the stars,
> from which we must all then fall,
> that the earth trembles and shakes all over,
> and the woods are shaken together with it.
>
> *(Statius)*

<aside>Pausanias</aside>
<aside>Shield of Agamemnon</aside>

Pausanias reports that the ancients depicted Terror in two ways: one with the head of a lion, which was carved so, as he says, among the Eleans on the shield of Agamemnon; the other with the face and dress of a woman, but more frightening than can be said. And an image of Dread made in this way was dedicated by the Corinthians to the sons of Medea, whom they had killed for the pernicious gifts that those sons had carried to the daughter of Creon, so that she perished with the whole royal house.

<aside>Fear worshipped</aside>

But Fear was not always held to be harmful, because Plutarch writes that it was worshipped by the Lacedaemonians, not because they were afraid of it, as of some other demons whom they wanted to keep far from the city, but because they thought that the republic was preserved by it, when the laws and the magistrates were feared. And so the ephors, who were the supreme magistracy, on entering office, at once (as Aristotle says) gave orders, and had it proclaimed through the city, that everyone should cut off his beard and be obedient to the laws, so that they themselves should not be forced to do harm to anyone; and they did this to accustom the young to obey even in light matters.

<aside>True fortitude</aside>

Moreover, the ancients did not believe that true fortitude was to fear nothing at all, but rather to be afraid of suffering something unworthy; and they considered that he who feared to offend the laws would always be bolder against the enemy than he who took no account of them at all, and that the fear of acquiring a bad name made men stronger to bear every labour and every danger. And this is the fear that peoples ought to have; and for this reason the Lacedaemonians placed the temple of Fear beside the house of the ephors.

<aside>Tullus Hostilius</aside>

Of this, perhaps, Tullus Hostilius too, king of the Romans, was thinking when he ordained (as Lactantius reports) that Fear and Pallor should be worshipped together, because it rarely happens that he who fears does not grow pale. And he well deserved, having found them so fine, as Lactantius himself says, to have his gods always with him, and that they should never abandon him.

<aside>Shield of Minerva</aside>

But returning to Minerva: while she shakes the spear and raises the shield, with the company that Apuleius gives her, she shows the threats of war; and if we consider her in peace, the shield, which was of most limpid crystal and covered the body against whatever might come to harm it, showed that the mind of the prudent man is covered by the earthly limbs only to guard and protect it, and not so that its sight is obscured by them in such a way that it can no longer see the truth of things.

<aside>Martianus Capella</aside>

And since shields are commonly of round shape, although Minerva's is sometimes seen made otherwise, Martianus Capella wrote that the shield on Minerva's arm signified that the world, which is likewise of round shape, is governed with supreme and infinite prudence, and not by chance as Democritus and Epicurus held.

<aside>Minerva's spear</aside>
<aside>Claudian</aside>

And the spear means that the prudent man can do harm to others even from afar; or that the force of prudence is so great that it penetrates every hardness of all the most difficult things, and often rises so high that it goes as far as Heaven. So Claudian depicted Minerva's spear so long and high that it passed beyond the clouds.

<aside>Minerva with the Talaria</aside>

And Homer, perhaps to express this still better, feigns that Minerva, wishing to go to Telemachus, to put it into his mind that he should go and look for Ulysses his father, puts on her feet the golden Talaria, of the sort we described in the image of Mercury, and carries with her nothing but the spear. We find also in Marcus Tullius, where he writes of the nature of the gods, that there was a Minerva (since he tells of five) who was feigned to have wings on her feet.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

Pausanias likewise writes that there was a long spear in the hand of that idol of Minerva which had on its helmet, as I have already said, the Sphinx and the Griffins; and he goes on to describe it, saying that it stood upright with a certain tunic that covered it all the way to the ground, and beneath the cuirass (which lay at her feet) was the shield [?5]. They add the owl as well, and that at the butt of the spear there was a serpent.

<aside>Demosthenes</aside>

From this Demosthenes took his argument, when he was forced to go into exile, to say that Minerva, who was the proper numen of Athens, delighted too much in three strange beasts, which were the owl, the serpent and the people; for in the republic of Athens the people had a great deal of say, and took things in the worst way when they felt offended.

<aside>Minerva's serpent</aside>
<aside>Servius</aside>

But as I have already said of the owl, so I say of the serpent, that it was given to Minerva as a sign of shrewdness and prudence. And so in Rome, in front of the great idol of Minerva, down at her feet, there was the serpent, all coiled up on itself, except that it raised its head up behind the shield that she held on her arm, as Servius says, where in Virgil the two serpents that killed Laocoon and his sons go straight to the temple of Minerva and settle at the feet of the goddess, under the shield.

<aside>Minerva's dress</aside>
<aside>Aegis</aside>

Of the tunic that she wears with the cuirass over it, Herodotus writes that the Greeks took this manner of dress from the women of Africa who live around Lake Tritonis, and that there is no other difference, except that the tunic beneath this dress is of skins, and the fringes, or borders, as we may call them, of the jerkin above are not of little serpents but of leather cut into narrow strips. Those women of Africa likewise made this jerkin of goat's hide, and for this reason the Greeks called it aegis, because *Ega* among them means goat; and this is what we have called the cuirass, which perhaps had fringes all around it of small serpents, as Herodotus seems to have meant when he set out the difference, as I have said, between the dress of the women of Africa and the garb of Minerva.

<aside>Gorgon</aside>
<aside>Diodorus</aside>

Besides this, the ancients depicted on her breast the Gorgon, which was the head of Medusa with hair of serpents, sticking out its tongue, and they sometimes placed it on her shield too, which was likewise called aegis by some; because Diodorus writes that Jupiter covered it with the skin of the goat Amalthea, and then gave it to Minerva.

<aside>Aex, daughter of the Sun</aside>
<aside>Hyginus</aside>

But more often by the aegis is understood the armour of the breast, which Hyginus writes was so called not from *Ega*, taken for the goat, but from a daughter of the Sun of this name. She was, as the fables tell, of marvellous whiteness with a stupendous splendour, but not beautiful, indeed so horrible to see that as soon as she showed herself to the Titans, enemies of Jupiter, they were all left terrified and stunned. So Earth, begged by them to remove her from before their eyes, hid her in Crete in a certain cave, where she stayed until Jupiter took her out of it, when he wanted to have the head of Medusa too, because the Oracle had said that without this he could not conquer the Titans, as he then conquered them; and after the victory he gave to Minerva the aegis, made of the skin of Aex with the head of Medusa, and she then always wore it.

<aside>Virgil</aside>

Virgil, when he tells how Vulcan goes to set the Cyclopes to work making arms for Aeneas, as Venus had begged him, and tells of the works that they then had in hand, which were the thunderbolts of Jupiter, the chariot of Mars and the armour of Minerva, who is the same as Pallas, says this of her:

> And with golden scales of serpent
> they skilfully composed the dread
> aegis, with which angry Pallas
> often arms herself, and the entwined serpents;
> and the Gorgon head, which even when severed
> rolled its eyes with a dark and fierce look,
> they fitted to the breast of the goddess.
>
> *(Virgil)*

<aside>Gorgon</aside>

And so by the Gorgon is always understood the head of Medusa, which killed others merely by being seen, although Athenaeus writes that among the Nomads in Libya there was a certain beast of this name, similar to sheep, or, as others hold, to calves, with a breath so pernicious that with this alone it killed all the other beasts that came near it, and with its sight it likewise killed people, whenever, shaking its head, it lifted a certain mane that, descending down the forehead, covered its eyes. Some soldiers of Marius found this out when he went against Jugurtha: hunting this beast, they fell dead as soon as it saw them. And the people of the country then told Marius its nature, and also helped him to get it dead, because they knew how, lying in ambush, it could be killed from afar. The skin was of such marvellous variety of colour that, when it was sent to Rome, there was no one who could say what beast it was from, and as a marvellous thing it was placed in the temple of Hercules.

<aside>Medusa</aside>
<aside>Diodorus</aside>

Procles the Carthaginian wrote, as Pausanias reports, that among the many and diverse beasts that were in the deserts of Africa there were also wild and bestial men and women; he had once seen one of them brought to Rome, and was ready to believe that Medusa had been one of those women, who, having gone to Lake Tritonis, had done much harm there to the inhabitants of the country, until she was killed by Perseus with the help of Minerva, because Minerva was the proper numen of that place. Diodorus writes that the Gorgons were warlike women in Africa, who were overcome by Perseus, who also killed Medusa their queen, and this might be history.

<aside>Gorgon</aside>

But the fables say, as we read in Apollodorus, that the Gorgons were three sisters, of whom only Medusa could die; the other two, named Euryale and Stheno, were immortal, and they all had the head wrapped in scaly serpents, and had teeth as large as a pig's, hands of copper, wings of gold, with which they flew at will, and turned to stone anyone who was seen by them. Perseus, having found them asleep, cut off the head of Medusa, carried it away, and then gave it to Minerva, by whom he was greatly helped in doing this: for from her he had the shield, just as from Mercury he had the scimitar and the Talaria; the helmet of Orcus, which made one invisible, and a certain wallet, in which he carried the terrible head, he had from some nymphs, who were pointed out to him by three sisters of the Gorgons, in order to get back the eye and the tooth stolen from them by him; for of these we read that they were born old, and had only one eye and one tooth among them, and used them in turn, now one, now the other.

<aside>Medusa</aside>

And that is why in a certain part of Greece, as Pausanias writes, there was in the temple of Minerva a statue of Perseus, to which, as though he were about to go then to Africa against Medusa, some nymphs gave a helmet and fastened the Talaria to his feet. They say also, and this is the more common fable, that of three very beautiful sisters, called the Gorgons from certain islands of a similar name where they lived, Medusa was the most beautiful, and had hair of gold. Neptune, falling in love with her, lay with her in the temple of Minerva, who, angered and greatly enraged at this, made Medusa, from beautiful and pleasing to look at as she had been before, all terrible and frightful, changing her golden locks into ugly serpents; and she wished that whoever looked at her thereafter should at once be turned to stone. But since the world could not bear so strange a monster, Perseus killed her with the help I mentioned, and gave the head to Minerva, who then always carried it on her shield or on the breast of her cuirass.

<aside>Minerva's cuirass</aside>
<aside>Pausanias</aside>

Of this cuirass Homer, when he describes this goddess arming herself to go against the Trojans, says that it is surrounded by horrible dread, and that, besides the head of Medusa, there are also in it spirited daring, sure fortitude and fearsome threats, all things proper to the goddess of wars, as is Victory too. And so Pausanias says that the Athenians placed Victory on Minerva's breast together with the head of Medusa, and that among the Eleans she stood beside her without wings.

All these things show the force of knowledge and of prudence: for prudence, with marvellous works and wise counsels, amazes others and leaves them almost like an immovable stone with wonder, so that it then easily obtains whatever it wishes, provided it knows how to set it out suitably; which is why this horrible head shows its tongue.

<aside>Peplos, the garment of Minerva</aside>
<aside>Lactantius</aside>

And the head was sometimes covered [?6] by the fine mantle that they placed around the goddess, called by the ancients Peplos, which was a sort of garment used around the idols of the gods, without sleeves, as Lactantius says on Statius, white and all spotted with golden studs, which the matrons made with their own hands and then offered every third year. But since this was an invention of the Athenians, of whom Minerva was the chief numen, the Peplos was more often taken to mean that great garment, or mantle, whichever it was, which was offered and consecrated to this goddess every five years with most solemn ceremony, although Suda says that it was not a garment but the sail of a certain ship, which at the time I have mentioned was fitted out with most beautiful ornaments in honour of Minerva for certain festivals of hers. The ancients also used to offer the Peplos when, in some grave danger, they wished to obtain the favour of the goddess.

<aside>Homer</aside>

And so in Homer Hecuba, on the advice of Helenus her son, a soothsayer, when she sees the Trojans driven by the Greeks right inside the walls, prepares, with her finest and most precious garments, a great and rich Peplos, and, accompanied by all the noblest matrons, carries it to the temple of Pallas, and there has it offered by Theano, wife of Antenor, a woman then held in the greatest veneration among the Trojan women, and all together they pray the goddess to be favourable to them. This was imitated by Virgil when he depicts the Trojan war at Carthage in the temple of Juno, saying:

> Meanwhile they went, with loosened hair,
> the women of Ilium, to the temple of unjust
> Pallas, and humbly all of them the Peplos
> were bringing to the goddess, always with the hand
> beating their grieving breasts.
>
> *(Virgil)*

<aside>Giant</aside>

In this solemn mantle the Athenians used to weave, embroider or paint Enceladus, or whichever other Giant it might be who was killed by Minerva; besides this, they sometimes also depicted on it those who had been the most valiant in battle and therefore deserved greater glory. That giant was a man from the waist up and a serpent for the rest, for this is how the poets describe all those Giants who dared to go and assault Heaven.

> Image of Jupiter striking the Giants with lightning; with their serpent legs they represent the impious despisers of God, who never do anything that is straight or just.

<aside>Commodus, cruel and insolent</aside>

So Suda reports of the emperor Commodus, who was beyond measure insolent and cruel, that in order to be called Hercules and son of Jupiter he often wore the skin of the lion and carried a club in his hand, with which he killed many men for his pleasure; and as though he wished to appear to be fighting then for the gods, he first had the thighs and legs of these men fitted out in the form of a snake or serpent, so that they would represent the Giants.

<aside>Apollodorus</aside>

Apollodorus writes that these Giants had a horrible and frightening face, with long hair spread down to the shoulders, and a long beard descending over their bristling chests.

<aside>Interpretation of the Giants</aside>

By their lower part it is understood that impious men, despisers of God, never do anything that is straight, or just, or honourable, but the very opposite, and so they resemble the serpent, which cannot raise itself from the ground or walk in a straight line, but as it goes must twist all over. And to these Minerva gives death, because they always remain in the darkness of human ignorance and never lift their eyes to that divine light which guides others to glorious and eternal life; and this is the help and favour that Minerva gives to whoever goes to her, as we read of Perseus, of whom I have already spoken, and of Bellerophon, who killed the Chimera after receiving from her the horse Pegasus, tamed and convenient to ride.

<aside>Bridler Minerva</aside>

So the people of Corinth, as Pausanias writes, had an idol of Minerva made entirely of wood (except that the face, the hands and the feet were of white marble), which they called Bridler, because they said that she was the first to bridle the horse Pegasus and give him to Bellerophon.

Prometheus likewise, with her help, went up to Heaven and stole the fire from the chariot of the Sun, with which he afterwards gave the arts to the world; and these are said to have come from Minerva because human ingenuity has found out what is done among us, and still finds out every day, and does it by means of fire, since in all the arts two things are needed. One is industry and invention, the other is the putting into practice and carrying out of what ingenuity has designed. The first is understood by Minerva, and the second by Vulcan, that is, by fire.

<aside>Vulcan</aside>

For under the name of Vulcan fire is understood, which is our instrument for doing all things, because fire warms and gives light, and where light and heat are lacking, nothing can be done. It is quite true that craft cannot always put into effect all that ingenuity finds, because craft is bound to the body and cannot depart from it or do more than the body can; but ingenuity often leaves the body and ranges at its pleasure, considering the works of nature and what God does, and sometimes imagines that it too makes similar things, of which, however, no effect is ever seen, because they are vain imaginings.

<aside>Plato</aside>

So the fables feigned that Vulcan could never be united with Minerva, although he made every effort, Jupiter having granted her to him. Yet the ancients did not on that account refrain from often placing the idols of both in one temple. And Plato likewise puts them together, saying in his *Atlantic* that both are equally numina of Athens, since in those times all the arts were practised there no less than the study of the sciences flourished.

<aside>Neptune with Minerva</aside>

We read also of Neptune and Minerva that by order of Jupiter they both together had the government of Athens. For this reason the Athenians stamped on their coins the head of Minerva on one side, and on the other the Trident, the emblem of Neptune, whom they also called King, and to Minerva they gave the name of civic and urban, as though cities need to be governed peacefully and with prudence.

<aside>Minerva on the gates</aside>

This is needed no less in private houses, and so the ancients used to paint Minerva both on the doors of these and on the gates of the city, and they painted Mars outside, on the country estates, showing in this way that war must be kept far off as much as possible. And because the Romans took care not to keep in the city those numina whom they thought to be in charge of harmful things, they had outside the city the temple of Bellona, and that of Mars too. But there was also one of Mars in the city, where he was worshipped as peaceful and called Quirinus, as I wrote before in the *Flavio*, where I gave the reason for both. I shall say how he was depicted after I have spoken of Vulcan, of whom we read as follows in Eusebius.

<aside>Vulcan</aside>
<aside>Lame Vulcan</aside>

They say that Vulcan is the virtue and power of fire, and they make him a statue in the form of a man with a cap on his head of sky-blue colour, as a sign of the revolution of the heavens, near which is found the true fire, pure and unmixed; something that cannot be said of this fire that we have, because it does not sustain itself but continually needs fresh matter to feed and maintain it. And Vulcan was feigned lame because the flame seems to be so; for in burning it does not go straight up but twists and tosses this way and that, because it is not pure and light, as it would need to be in order to rise straight to its place.

<aside>Vulcan with mice</aside>
<aside>King Sethon</aside>
<aside>Mice sent by Vulcan</aside>

Alexander ab Alexandro reports, and I believe he took it from Herodotus, although the one speaks of Vulcan and the other of King Sethon, that in Egypt there was a statue that held a mouse in its hands, and that these peoples made it so because they believed that Vulcan had once sent a great multitude of mice against the Arabs, at a time when they were in very great numbers to occupy the Egyptians' country; so the Arabs were forced to return.

Herodotus tells the matter in this way. Sethon, priest of Vulcan and at the same time king of Egypt, finding himself abandoned by all the men of war, because he had never taken any account of them, and with Sennacherib, king of the Arabs, having marched against him with a very large army, did not know what to do in so strange a plight, and so he lamented and grieved over his misery. Meanwhile it happened that, having fallen asleep beside the idol of Vulcan, he dreamed that this god encouraged him to be of good heart, and told him to go boldly against the enemies and not to doubt that he would drive them off with the help that he would send him.

Sethon, then, having taken courage from this, went out with the few men he had and encamped not far from the Arabs, in whose camp the following night there appeared such a great multitude of mice that they gnawed through their bows, their shields and all their leather gear, and forced them to flee into Egypt [?7]. And so in the temple of Vulcan stood King Sethon himself, made of stone, with a mouse in his hand and with a motto that said:

> Let people learn from me to be pious and religious.

<aside>Mice hated</aside>

And perhaps the Arabs then conceived so much hatred for mice that afterwards they always wished them ill, because Plutarch writes that they killed all those they could get, as the Ethiopians did too, and the Magi of Persia, saying that the gnawing of these little animals was too tiresome and troublesome to the gods. Nor do I remember having read for what reason the ancients in Egypt believed that Vulcan had sent the mice; but perhaps by him might be understood the dryness of the season and of the country, since Pliny, writing of the fertility of mice, says that they multiply greatly in the fields when the weather is dry and parched, which is why in winter they then appear no more [?8], and one cannot know what becomes of them, because they are found neither alive nor dead, neither above ground nor below.

<aside>Vulcan thrown out of Heaven</aside>

The fables that are read of Vulcan are many, and all of them can give us occasion to make paintings of him in various ways, beginning with his birth; for we read that he was born of Juno, and that she, seeing him so ugly, scorned him and threw him away, so that the wretch fell on Lemnos, an island in the Aegean sea, and from that fall was left crippled, so that he was always lame afterwards. This means, as the natural philosophers explain it, that the thunderbolt, which is nothing but a fiery vapour, descends from the lower part of the air, which is the thickest, densest and murkiest.

<aside>Juno bound</aside>

Vulcan, grown up and mindful of the injury done to him by his mother, in order to avenge himself on her, or else to prevent her from doing what she was preparing to do, harm to Hercules, according to what Suda reports from Pindar and from Epicharmus, sent her as a gift a fine gilded seat, made with such art that, when she sat down on it, she remained bound in such a way that it was not possible, even for all the gods of Heaven, to free her from it. So they tried to draw him up there to free Juno, who was greatly distressed at remaining bound like that; but he, who trusted none of them, would never go. Yet at last he trusted Bacchus alone, who perhaps gave him plenty to drink, and with him went up to Heaven to free Juno from the artful seat.

Pausanias reports this from the fables of the Greeks, and says that among the other paintings the Athenians had was this one of Bacchus bringing Vulcan back to Heaven to free Juno, and that among the Lacedaemonians in the temple of Minerva there was likewise Vulcan untying his mother.

<aside>Vulcan at the forge</aside>

He is also depicted in a great cave, as he stands with the Cyclopes at the forge, working now at one thing, now at another [?9], because whenever the gods needed arms of any sort, whether for themselves or for others, they went to him as to their smith, as Thetis went to him for the arms of her son Achilles; and so it was depicted on the chest of Cypselus, according to Pausanias, who gives no other sign that the one who gave the arms to Thetis was Vulcan, except that he was lame and had behind him one of his men with a great pair of tongs in his hand. Venus too had arms from him, which she afterwards gave to Aeneas. And when poets wish to describe some great thing made with much art and great industry, they say it was made either by Vulcan or by the Cyclopes at the forge of Vulcan.

> Image of Vulcan, god of fire, with his forge and the Cyclopes, who made the arrows for Jupiter and the arms for the gods and the heroes. Vulcan is also understood as natural and generative heat.

<aside>Vulcan king</aside>
<aside>Iron, by whom first used</aside>

All this can be reconciled with what Suda tells of him as history: that he was king in Egypt and was esteemed a god because he knew all the secrets of religion; he was very warlike, so that, wounded in battle, he was left crippled and lame, and he was the first to use iron to make from it the arms for war and the instruments for cultivating the fields.

<aside>Image of Vulcan</aside>

Besides this, the fables feigned that Vulcan bound Venus and Mars with a very fine net of steel while they were amorously sporting together, and that he tried to force Minerva, and other similar things, which there is no need to relate now, since they do not serve his image, which was that of a lame man, black in the face, ugly and smoked, just as smiths are. Some depict him naked, and others neither naked nor clothed, but with only a few rags about him and a cap on his head, as I said. And in Herodotus we read that in Mensi [?10], a city of Egypt, the idol of Vulcan was like certain gods called Pataeci by the Phoenicians, who carried them on the prows of their ships, and they were in the form of Pygmies, which King Cambyses, having entered Vulcan's temple, mocked greatly.

<aside>Lions given to Vulcan</aside>
<aside>Dogs, guards of Vulcan</aside>

Lions were consecrated to him by the Egyptians, as Aelian writes, because they are of a very hot and fiery nature, which is why, from the heat they have within, they are greatly afraid when they see fire and flee. Alexander ab Alexandro writes that in Rome dogs stood at the temple of Vulcan as keepers and guards, and never barked except at anyone who had gone there to steal something from it. And near Mount Etna in Sicily dogs likewise guarded the temple of Vulcan and the sacred wood around it.

<aside>Sacrifice to Vulcan</aside>

Besides this, whoever remained victor in any war used to gather together the shields and the other arms of the enemies on a hill, and by burning them make a sacrifice to Vulcan, as in Virgil Evander says that he did to him when, still a youth, he was victor near Praeneste. Servius says this is taken from history, which tells that Tarquinius Priscus, having defeated the Sabines, burned all their arms in honour of Vulcan, and that others have always done the same since, the custom arising of burning everything that was offered in the sacrifices of Vulcan.

<aside>Proterwia, a sacrifice</aside>

And in a certain other kind of sacrifice, called Proterwia, as Macrobius writes, the ancients also used to burn everything that remained after the priests and the others had eaten; and from this Cato made his quip against a certain Albidius, whose house had burned, the only thing left to him of a large and rich patrimony that he had eaten entirely. Cato said, then, that Albidius had made the sacrifice of Proterwia.

<aside>Venus with Vulcan</aside>
<aside>Mars with Venus</aside>

The fables then paired Venus with Vulcan and made the two of them husband and wife, because the generation of things, shown by Venus, is not without heat, and nothing signifies that better than fire, understood by Vulcan. For this reason too they likewise placed Mars with Venus, meaning by him the ardour of the Sun; besides this, Aristotle says that it was with good reason that these gods were feigned to be joined together, because men of war are strongly inclined to lust.

<aside>Mars</aside>

So the Accitani, a people of Spain, made, as Macrobius reports, the idol of Mars adorned with rays, like that of the Sun, and worshipped it with great reverence. And it is natural, adds the same Macrobius, that the authors of celestial heat should differ only in name; for Mars was believed to be that ardour which comes from the Sun and kindles in us the blood and the spirits, so that we are then easily moved to angers, rages and wars. Of these things he was called the god by the ancients, as Minerva was called the goddess of them; and as she was born without the service of a wife, so he was born without the office of a husband.

<aside>How Mars was born</aside>

For the fables say that Juno, envious that Jupiter had had children without her, wished likewise to have them without him, and by virtue of a certain flower shown to her by Flora, as Ovid tells, or, as some others have said, by striking her private parts with her hand [?11], she conceived Mars, and went to give birth to him there in Thrace, where the people are beyond measure terrible and ready for wars. This shows us that wars for the most part are born of the desire to have kingdoms and riches, shown by Juno.

<aside>Horses of Mars</aside>
<aside>Image of Mars</aside>

The ancients depicted Mars as fierce and terrible in appearance, wholly armed, with a spear in his hand and a whip, and they placed him on horseback, sometimes on a chariot, and especially nearly all the poets, beginning with Homer, who says that his chariot was drawn by two horses, which are Terror and Fear. And elsewhere he then imagines that these are no longer horses but persons, who always go with Mars, and that Impetus, Fury and Violence likewise accompany him.

<aside>Statius</aside>
<aside>Armour of Mars</aside>
<aside>Rumour</aside>

Statius imitates this when he tells how Mars goes to stir up war between the two brothers Eteocles and Polynices in the kingdom of Thebes. After he has described the arms of this god, which were the helmet, so bright that it seemed to burn, as if it had the blazing thunderbolt for a crest, the gilded cuirass, all full of terrible and frightening monsters, and the shield gleaming with bloody light, he says that around him, adorning his head, stand Fury and Wrath, that Terror governs the reins of the horses, and that before these flies, shaking her wings, Rumour, who brings no less of the false than of the true, because this is a certain noise that rises from a small beginning and then grows so much that it fills cities and countries with itself. That is why Homer calls her the announcer and messenger of Jupiter.

The ancients made Rumour a goddess too, and depicted her in the form of a woman dressed in a thin cloth and all girt up, who shows herself running away swiftly with a shrill trumpet at her mouth.

And to show her speed better, they added wings to her and depicted her all laden with eyes, as Virgil describes her, who calls her a horrible monster and imagines her all feathered, and that as many feathers as she has, she has as many eyes too, vigilant and always awake, and as many mouths with as many tongues that never keep silent, and as many ears that stand listening always attentive; and he says that she goes flying always by night and never sleeps, and by day then settles on high towers, from where she frightens wretched mortals, bringing them for the most part evil tidings.

<aside>Double Rumour</aside>
<aside>Claudian</aside>

Nevertheless, because she sometimes brings good ones too, it was said that Rumour was not one but two; and the one that announced good was called good, and the one that carried ill was called bad; and this one, unlike the other, had black wings. So Claudian, writing against Alaric, says that Rumour spread her black wings, which some sometimes depict like those of a bat.

> Image of Mars, god of war, of his chariot, and of Rumour, his messenger and forerunner, who says more than is the case in fact. And by Mars is understood that ardour of the Sun which kindles the blood and the spirits so as to make them then ready for anger, wars and rages.

<aside>Seneca</aside>
<aside>Wrath</aside>

Rumour goes before the chariot of Mars because at the beginning of wars more is often said than follows afterwards, although the minds on both sides are kindled with the gravest wrath; since seldom does one come to fierce battles without it, and it, as Seneca writes, seems to have greater force in us than many other passions that disturb us, for it not only leads minds away from the straight path of reason, but often changes the body too.

<aside>Ovid</aside>
<aside>Fury</aside>

And so Ovid, and Seneca likewise, say that the face of angry people swells all over and as it were blazes, their eyes are inflamed, and the angry person becomes so terrible that he shows himself almost no less frightening than the horrible face of Medusa. I have made this brief sketch of the angry person because I do not find that the ancients made any image of Wrath, so that from it whoever wishes may make a portrait of her, who is also called Fury; and Fury is nothing but Wrath as far as she can be kindled and inflamed.

<aside>Petronius</aside>

The ancients depicted him terrible in face, almost bloodstained, showing that he raged, seated on cuirasses, helmets, shields, swords and other arms, with his hands bound behind his back with strong chains; for Virgil describes him thus, and places him inside the gates of war, which were those of the temple of Janus, which, as I have already said, stood closed in time of peace and were open in time of war. They have depicted him unbound too, as can be seen from the description by Petronius where he began to write of the civil war.

> Image of Fury and Wrath and of their evil effects, which are to despise every danger, even though it be manifest death and loss of honour, regarding neither God nor men nor friends or kinsmen, nor even one's own interest; the furious and wrathful man losing reason in fury and in wrath.

But returning to Mars, some put four horses to his chariot, so terrible and fierce that they breathed fire. And Isidore writes that Mars was sometimes depicted with his chest bare, because whoever goes into battle must go there with the intention of boldly exposing himself to all dangers.

<aside>Herodotus</aside>
<aside>A notable sacrifice</aside>

We read in Herodotus that the Scythians worshipped many gods but made temples, altars and idols to none other than Mars, although they sacrificed to all of them in one and the same way, which seems to me to deserve to be reported, and it was this.

The victim stood with its forefeet bound, and the sacrificer came up behind it and struck it on the head, and as it fell he called on the god to whom he was sacrificing it; then he put a noose round its neck, and twisting it with a stick strangled it, and after skinning it he put it to cook on a fire made from the bones of the same animal, having first removed all the flesh from them, because Scythia has a great shortage of wood. And if sometimes they did not have their cauldrons either, he put all the meat with water in the animal's own paunch and boiled it there, so that the victim made fire for itself from itself and also cooked in itself. This done, the priest then offered the sacrifice to the god to whom it belonged.

<aside>Victim of Mars</aside>
<aside>Idol of Mars</aside>

And among the other animals that those peoples sacrificed, the horse was the principal victim, especially of Mars, whose temple, because the rains and the bad climate of the air of that country quickly ruined it, they rebuilt every year in this way. They gathered together a hundred and fifty cartloads of brushwood, and made of them a sort of great woodpile, so that on three sides it was high and the fourth side sloped down in such a way that one could comfortably go up by it; and on top they placed a certain knife that they used, called an acinaces, which perhaps was like a scimitar, and was the Persians' own knife. This was for them the idol of Mars; this they worshipped, and to this they made more frequent sacrifices than to any other god.

So did the people of Arabia Petraea, according to Suda, to a certain black, square stone without any other figure, four feet high and two wide, which stood on a golden base, because they held it to be the true idol of Mars, who was chiefly worshipped by them.

> Image of the temple of Mars, god of war, which was of this kind among the Scythians, and of the figure of Mars among the people of Arabia Petraea, understood also as the Sun and its force in all human actions.

<aside>House of Mars</aside>

Describing the house of Mars, Statius imagines it to be in Thrace, where he too was born, as I have said, because the peoples of that country love war greatly; he says that it is all of iron, neither polished and shining, nor rusty and dark, but almost white-hot, and that merely to look at it frightens and saddens. Here are impetuous Fury, raging Wrath, cruel Impiety, pale Fear, and the hidden Treacheries, which go about in secret and do not let others see the sharp knives that they keep covered; and Discord, armed in both hands with cutting iron.

<aside>Discord. Virgil</aside>

The ancients placed her among those gods whom they worshipped, not because they could do good, but so that they should not do harm; for wherever she is found there is never peace or rest, and for this reason Jupiter drove her out of Heaven, and she was not invited to the wedding of Thetis and Peleus, where nearly all the other gods were; and angered at this, she threw among them the apple from which the ruin of Troy was born, through the judgement that Paris made of it. Discord was depicted in the form of an infernal Fury, as Virgil describes her when he says:

> A bloody band knots and binds
> the viperous hair of mad Discord.
>
> *(Virgil)*

Petronius said the same of her.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

Aristides imagines her as a woman who holds her head high, with livid and pale lips, squinting, ruined eyes, brimming with tears that continually streak her pale cheeks; she never keeps her hands to herself and is very quick to move them; she carries a knife driven into her breast, and has twisted legs and thin feet, and around her a dark and gloomy mist that surrounds her all, like a net. Pausanias writes that on one side of the chest of Cypselus were carved Ajax and Hector, who were fighting together in the presence of Discord, who was there beside them and was a woman with a very ugly face. He says nothing more about her, and still less how Calliphon of Samos depicted her; he, as Pausanias adds, painted her, after the model of that one, in the temple of Diana of Ephesus, where he depicted the war that took place not far from the ships of the Greeks.

> Image of Discord according to Aristides, who for her evil effects was driven out of Heaven and was not invited with the other gods to the wedding of Peleus and Thetis, parents of Achilles, so that she should not disturb it with her poisons; and yet she did disturb it by throwing the golden apple, which signifies that for plotters there is never a lack of occasions for discord.

<aside>Ariosto</aside>

But whoever does not know how to make a portrait of Discord from the ancients, let him make it from what the moderns have said of her, and among these let him be content with Ariosto alone, who paints her very well when he tells how the angel Michael goes to find her, and says thus.

> He knew her by her dress of a hundred colours,
> made of unequal and endless strips,
> which now covered her, now not, for steps and wind
> kept opening them, since they were worn out;
> her hair was part of gold, part of silver,
> and black and grey, and seemed to be at strife;
> some gathered in a plait, some in a ribbon,
> many loose on her shoulders, some on her breast.
>
> *(Ariosto)*

<aside>Palace of Mars</aside>

Then the palace of Mars resounded all with threatening voices; and in the middle of it stood Virtue, sad and grieving, and opposite her Fury showed himself glad. Here sat Death with a bloodied face, and on the altars was the blood shed in cruel battles, of which sacrifice was made to the terrible god with fire taken from burned cities. And all around hung the spoils brought back from almost every part of the world, and on the walls and on the doors were carved killings, burnings and the other ruins that wars bring with them.

<aside>Statue of Mars bound</aside>
<aside>Gods bound</aside>

This is the whole sketch that Statius makes of the house of Mars, the statue of whom the Lacedaemonians kept bound, as Pausanias recounts, with tight knots, thinking in this way to hold him too, so that he should never leave them and should then, by his favour, make them victors in every war; and many other nations did the same, and the Romans likewise bound some idols, and especially those of the gods to whom the city was entrusted.

<aside>Gods called out of cities</aside>

For of the many gods worshipped by the ancients, each city had one or two who guarded it more than the others, and even the enemies feared to offend these. From this came the custom of calling out and inviting to themselves, with certain words prescribed for the purpose and spoken by the priest, the gods who were guardians of that city against which war was being waged, showing in this way that they did not want a contest with the gods.

<aside>Numen concealed</aside>

And so the Romans did not wish the true name of the god to whom the city was given in particular guard ever to be known, so that he should not go away when called by the enemies. And where Virgil names the mother Vesta, guardian of the Tiber and of Rome, Servius notes that this is said poetically, not that it was the proper name of the true numen of Rome, because, he adds, the laws of religion did not wish it to be known, and a tribune of the plebs who dared to name it was put to death by the hand of justice.

<aside>Quintus Curtius</aside>
<aside>Apollo bound</aside>

Since, then, the sacred laws are not always wholly observed by everyone, the ancients kept some gods bound so that they should not leave them, as Quintus Curtius recounts: the people of Tyre in Phoenicia bound with golden chains the idol of Apollo, one of their chief gods, and fastened it to the altar of Hercules, to whom the city was entrusted, as though Hercules were to hold Apollo back so that he should not go away, because a citizen said he had seen him in a dream abandoning the city and going away, at the time when Alexander was around it to take it by storm.

<aside>Victory without wings</aside>
<aside>Heliodorus</aside>

With this, it seems to me, agrees what the Athenians did in keeping Victory without wings, as we read in Pausanias, so that she should not fly away; and she had, as Heliodorus says, a pomegranate in her right hand and a helmet in her left. And the Romans, so that she should stay with them more willingly, gave her the Capitol for her seat (as Livy writes) and dedicated to her the temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus, when Hiero, after the rout that they had suffered from the Carthaginians at Cannae, sent them as a gift one made all of gold, with other gifts of great price, all of which they sent back, and kept only the idol of Victory as a good omen.

<aside>Victory</aside>
<aside>Laurel, sign of Victory</aside>

The ancients depicted her for the most part with wings, in the form of a beautiful maiden flying through the air, and with one hand holding out a wreath of laurel or of white olive, and in the other holding a branch of palm, as is seen on ancient medals and on ancient marbles; and sometimes we see her with the wreath alone, and sometimes with the branch of palm alone. The Romans often depicted her with a branch of laurel in her hand, because they took this alone too as a sign of victory, and they attached it to the letters that carried the news of it; and when they rejoiced over some victory they went to place some leaves in the lap of Jupiter Optimus Maximus, and the most worthy commanders, when triumphing, made a wreath of it for themselves.

<aside>Eagle, sign of Victory</aside>
<aside>Standards of the Romans</aside>

The Egyptians in their sacred letters showed Victory by the eagle, because it overcomes all other birds in valour; from this perhaps it came that among all the other emblems that the Romans carried in war on their standards, the eagle was the chief and the most frequent. For we read that they also carried the wolf, because it was a beast of Mars; they carried the Minotaur, to show that the commander's counsel and every design of his must remain as hidden as that beast was in the Labyrinth; and they carried the pig too, because without it a truce was never made or peace formed, and they used such a ceremony in it.

> Image of Victory with her emblems, the eagle, the palm and the laurel, the eagle being queen of the birds and of good omen, the palm resisting every force and giving part of nourishment, the laurel always green and not struck by lightning; so the victorious man overcomes difficulty with Virtue, and remains immortal.

<aside>Ceremonies of truce or peace</aside>

Some persons appointed for this purpose gathered together, from the one side and the other, of those who were about to make peace or truce; and the priest to whom this office was given, who was called Fetial, after some solemn words and after reciting the conventions and pacts agreed between them, struck with a certain stone and killed a pig that was present there for this purpose, praying Jupiter that he would strike in the same way whichever of them first broke the truce or peace, whichever it might be.

<aside>Josephus</aside>

Setting aside now the bundle of hay on top of a long pole, which was the first standard of the Romans, and the open hand, and a certain veil or sendal, which was, just as we see in our day, the general's pennant, I shall say only that the horse too appeared on the Roman standards, and the ox. But it is true that these two, and the other three that I have mentioned, stayed almost always within the palisades, and the eagle alone went into battle, because they reckoned, as Josephus says, that this was the true emblem of supreme command, and that it carried with it against the enemies a good omen of victory.

<aside>Justin</aside>
<aside>Eagle, standard of the Persians</aside>

Hence we read, and Justin reports it, that because an eagle flew onto Hiero's shield when, still a youth, he began to go to war, it was said that he was to be king and very valiant, as he was, although he came from a low and humble house. Cyrus too carried a golden eagle with open wings at the head of a long spear, as Xenophon writes, and the other kings of the Persians have likewise carried it ever since.

<aside>Athenaeus</aside>

Pausanias says that in the temple of Jupiter among the Lacedaemonians there were two eagles bearing two Victories, each its own, which Lysander had offered there in memory of having twice defeated the Athenians. In the great spectacle that was presented by Ptolemy Philadelphus (Athenaeus tells of it as a marvellous thing) there were some winged Victories, who had garments woven with various animals, with many golden ornaments about them, and they carried in their hands golden censers made in the shape of ivy leaves, perhaps because they were then serving Bacchus; they went before an altar adorned likewise with branches of ivy made of gold.

<aside>Claudian</aside>

Claudian, when he praises Stilicho, describes Victory dressed in trophies with the green palm in her hand and with wings at her shoulders, which show the uncertain outcomes of wars, since Victory often seems to be on one side and at once on the other; and to the victor she adds strength and causes him to live long in the memory of posterity, just as the palm grows stronger against any weight that is placed on it, and its wood does not decay like others, and its leaves stay green a long time.

<aside>Victory, a common goddess</aside>

And because the end of wars is doubtful, Victory was called a common goddess, as though she stood in the middle and drew near to whoever knows best how to draw her to himself. And for the same reason Mars too was called a common god, because among enemies winning and being beaten are common. Some have also depicted Victory armed, cheerful and merry in appearance, but all covered with dust and sweat, and holding out with bloodstained hands the spoils and the prisoners to the victors.

> Image of armed Victory and of the same Victory without wings. The first is made to represent its causes, which are toil and sweat. The second, the desire of those who so figured her, which was not to be abandoned by Victory.

<aside>Prudentius</aside>

Of her, and of those who worshipped her, thinking that her favour would be of use to them, Prudentius, a Christian poet, makes mockery, and says that victory is to be sought from the eternal and true God and from one's own virtue.

> And not from her whom foolish peoples
> feigned beautiful, young and bold,
> with fair hair now bound, now loose,
> the thin cloth girded across the breast,
> so that the garment, moved by a light wind,
> billows so that the white foot is uncovered.
>
> *(Prudentius)*

<aside>Horse sacrificed</aside>

And still less from Mars, as the ancient Romans did, who, by sacrificing to him the horse that had been the most victorious in the race, wished to show that they acknowledged victory as coming from him; though some say that this was done to punish speed, than which nothing else helps the fugitive better, and to give to understand that one must not hope in flight.

<aside>Animals of Mars</aside>

Besides this, various animals were given to Mars, sometimes in sacrifice and sometimes only as companions, such as the dog and the wolf, which may be added to his image: the former because it is fierce, as Pausanias writes, and the strongest of the other animals that live with man; the latter either because, as it has so good an eye that it sees by night, so shrewd captains must see a great deal, so that they do not fall into the hidden ambushes of the enemies, or because it is by nature rapacious and willingly kills and sheds blood, all things fitting for the god of wars.

To him among the birds was given the cock, to show the vigilance that soldiers must have, or because, as the fables tell and Lucian writes, Alectryon, a soldier very dear to Mars, was changed by him into this bird because he did not keep the good watch that Mars had commanded him on the night when he lay in bed with Venus; so that, without his noticing it, Vulcan entered the chamber and, throwing over them the very fine net, caught them, embracing as they were.

<aside>Vulture sacred to Mars</aside>

The vulture too was given to Mars, because of it we read that it follows dead bodies with very great eagerness, and therefore goes behind armies, as if nature had taught it that these gather to make slaughter. Indeed it has taught it even more, that it knows, as Pliny writes, three and four days beforehand, and some say seven, where the battle is to be, and knows on which side more will die, and keeps looking at that side more than at the other, as though a greater prey were being prepared for it there. Hence in ancient times kings, when they drew up their armies for battle, used to send to spy out where the vultures looked most, and from this they then judged on which side the victory would be.

<aside>Woodpecker, bird of Mars</aside>

With Mars the woodpecker too is sometimes depicted, and so it was called the Martian Woodpecker, as though it belonged properly to Mars; either because, as this bird, striking the hard oak with its strong beak, hollows it out, so soldiers with frequent batterings beat the walls of cities until they force a way to enter within; or because this bird was much observed in auguries, to which soldiers seem to pay great attention; indeed everyone in ancient times attended to them so much that they seemed not to know how to do anything, public or private, without first taking an augury in some way, as I said before in the *Flavio*, where I also told the way that the ancients used in taking auguries.

<aside>Boccaccio</aside>
<aside>Couch-grass given to Mars</aside>

Of trees I have not so far found any that was consecrated to Mars as his own, but of couch-grass I have indeed read that the ancients gave it to him, perhaps because, as Boccaccio writes, it grows for the most part in spacious and open places, where armies usually encamp almost always. And the Romans had no wreath more worthy or of greater honour than that of couch-grass, which they gave only to those who in some extreme danger had saved the whole army, or had lifted the siege from around it.

<aside>Herodotus</aside>
<aside>Festival of Mars</aside>
<aside>Ridiculous ceremony</aside>

There remains nothing else for me to say of Mars, except that I do not wish to pass over in silence the solemn festival that was held in his honour in Papremis, a city of Egypt, because it seems to me that the ceremony recounted by Herodotus deserves to be reported. Now, when the time of the festival came, to which almost all the people of the country went to the city I mentioned, a few priests stayed in the temple around the altars to perform the sacrifices and the other things belonging to them, and all the others stationed themselves at the doors of the same temple with good wooden clubs in their hands; against them came about a thousand foreigners, who had come from outside for the festival, likewise with thick sticks in their hands.

These, having prepared the day before a great shrine of wood, all gilded, with the idol of Mars inside, and having placed it on a cart with four wheels drawn by a few of them, wished to enter the temple with it; and the priests who were at the doors forbade them, so that they began to beat each other strangely there with sticks, the one party not wishing that god to enter the temple and the others straining to make him enter, which in the end they did. And although they gave each other nasty blows on the head and many of them were badly wounded, none ever died of it.

The thing was arranged in this way because, the ancients said, the mother of Mars living in that temple, he, already grown up, went there to lie with her; but the priests, noticing this, and not knowing who he was, did not let him enter, so that he was forced to go away; but not long after, having gathered with him people from a certain city not far from there, he returned, and having given the priests a good drubbing, he entered in spite of them to take his pleasure of his mother. This is the event represented in the ceremony I have described, which without doubt contains some mystery; but since Herodotus has not told it, I do not report it either, and leave it to be sought by whoever is curious to know it.

<aside>Festival of Minerva</aside>

In its place I shall tell of a certain other ceremony, written of likewise by Herodotus, which in part is similar to the one already told, and was held in honour of Minerva, so that with her name I may put an end to the image that was begun from her name. It was celebrated every year in a certain part of Africa around Lake Tritonis, where at the time appointed for the festival almost all the young maidens of the country gathered, and there, divided as into two ranks of soldiers, they fought fiercely together with stones and sticks; and the one who by common judgement had shown herself the most valiant and had used her hands best was taken from all the others and carried apart. They armed her all over, with a fine helmet on her head, and, setting her on a chariot, led her all around the lake, and all accompanied her with solemn pomp. And those who remained dead in this fight, for often many died there, were believed not to have been truly virgins, and that Minerva had let them perish.

<aside>Minerva a virgin</aside>

For she was always a virgin, since true wisdom, shown at times by her, feels no stain of mortal things, and is always wholly pure and clean in itself. In the sacrifices of Minerva it was also observed to give her pure victims, which were sometimes a ewe lamb, sometimes a white bull, and sometimes an untamed heifer with gilded horns, to show that virginity is not subject to the yoke of lust, and is wholly pure and white.

> Image of Bacchus, signifying the various effects of wine, of which he was the inventor, and moreover the effects of drunkenness, which are revelations of hidden things, fury, lust and the like. These two, he with Hercules, both Thebans and sons of Jupiter, surpassed all the ancients in glory.

## Doubtful passages

- [?1] "in Athens" (scan 0344): the printed clause is defective ("che pur andò in Athene fu dedicata a questa Dea vna lucerna d'oro"). It is read as "that in Athens a golden lamp was dedicated to this goddess"; "pur andò" has no clear sense and is probably a misprint for "pure in".
- [?2] Cercops (scan 0346): printed "le figliole di Cercope", the daughters of Cercops. The name is kept as printed; in the usual version of the story the daughters are those of Cecrops, king of Athens, and no correction is made here.
- [?3] Epiphanius (scan 0346): the text has "Epifanio", but the marginal note prints "Epitafio" ("Epitaph"). The aside is rendered "Epiphanius", following the text, which names Epiphanius.
- [?4] "steep" (scan 0348): the printed text has "stretta, & certa, & difficile" ("narrow, and certain, and difficult"). "Certa" makes no sense of a road; it is read as a misprint for "erta" (steep), as the context of the road of Virtue requires.
- [?5] shield under the cuirass (scan 0355): "& era sotto la corazza (che le giaceua a' piedi) lo scudo". It is not clear whether the shield lay under the cuirass, which itself lay at her feet, or whether the clause is otherwise out of order; translated as printed.
- [?6] "covered" (scan 0358): "Et era coperto talhora dal bel manto". The subject is not stated; it is taken to be the head of the Gorgon (the "horrible head" of the previous sentence), which is covered by the Peplos at times.
- [?7] "into Egypt" (scan 0362): "a fuggirsi nelle Egitto" (the grammar is also faulty: "nelle" for "nello"). It is odd that the Arabs, who were the invaders, flee into Egypt; translated as printed.
- [?8] "no longer seen in winter" (scan 0362): the printed text says "l'inuerno appaiono poi più" ("in winter they then appear more"), which contradicts the sentence that follows ("one cannot know what becomes of them, because they are found neither alive nor dead"). It is read as "appaiono poi più" being a misprint for "non appaiono più", and translated "appear no more".
- [?9] Vulcan "now at one thing, now at another" (scan 0364): the printed text reads "a fabricare quando l'altra", with the first member of the pair ("quando l'una") missing. Translated by supplying the obvious sense.
- [?10] Mensi (scan 0364): the city of Egypt where Herodotus puts the statue of Vulcan is Memphis; the printed form "Mensi" is kept.
- [?11] Juno "striking her private parts with her hand" (scan 0367): the printed words are "battendosi la natura con mano", where "natura" is the euphemism for the genitals. The more usual version of the story (the Homeric Hymn to Apollo, Natale Conti) has Juno strike the earth with her hand, so "natura" may be a corruption of "terra"; translated as printed.
