# Catalogue of Authors

Source: books/cartari/_text/TEXT_reading.md, scans 0023–0024 and 0037–0039. The catalogue is printed twice in the book: the first printing is scans 0023–0024 (pages XII–XIII, before the Index), the second is scans 0037–0039 (pages XXVI–XXVIII, after the Index; scan 0040, page XXIX, is blank). Both are translated in full; the differences of the second printing are noted in the Doubtful passages. The Index (Tavola, scans 0025–0036) is not translated by assignment. There are no marginal notes on these scans. Vignettes and ornamental borders are not translated.

## First printing (scans 0023–0024)

**Catalogue of ancient and modern authors, extant, who have expressly described the histories, or images, of the ancient deities.**

- Apollodorus the Athenian — on the origin of the gods.
- Diodorus Siculus — the same.
- Cicero — on the nature of the gods.
- Phurnutus, or, as others call him, Cornutus — the same.
- Philostratus — some images.
- Julius Hyginus and Vettius Bassus — the fables and the genealogies.
- Palaephatus — some of the fables.
- Antoninus Liberalis — the same.
- Ovid in the *Fasti* and in the *Metamorphoses* — the festivals and the fables.
- Pausanias often describes the statues of the gods.
- Callistratus describes some of them.
- Fulgentius — the allegories.
- In passing they are treated by Lactantius Firmianus, Minucius Felix, Julius Firmicus, Arnobius, Tertullian, and almost all the Greek and Latin Fathers.

**MODERN.**

- Albricus the Philosopher — the images.
- Giovanni Boccaccio — the genealogy.
- Lilio Gregorio Giraldi — the images and the surnames.
- Basilio Zanco, Giuliano Aurelio, Pier Giacomo Montefalco, Hubert Goltzius, Janus Gruterus — the surnames.
- Georg Pictorius — the consecration.
- Melchior Barlaeus — in verse, in three books.
- Guillaume du Choul — on religion.
- Pier Giacomo Montefalco and Vincenzo Cartari, in the *Flavio* — the festivals and celebrations.
- Likewise all those who have explained the urban and rustic calendars of the ancients, such as F. Orsini, Pedro Chacón, Aldus Manutius and others.
- Natale Conti — the allegories, or mythologies.
- Ulisse Aldrovandi has described the statues of the deities, and others that are in Rome.
- Abraham Ortelius has brought to light the heads of LIV deities, taken from ancient medals. On the occasion of this labour Andreas Schottus, a most learned man, composed a most graceful dialogue, printed in Antwerp with the dialogues of A. Agostini translated into Latin; and Franciscus Sweertius has illustrated the said Ortelius with a graceful account, printed in Antwerp in 1612.
- Lorenzo Pignoria, writing on the Table of Isis, has described many curious particulars of the deities of Egypt.
- The same, in the *Mysteries of the Great Mother of the Gods*, the antiquities of Phrygia; and he has gathered many things in his Annotations on Cartari.
- John Selden — on the deities of Syria.
- Girolamo Aleandro — on the Sun and on Pluto, carefully, in his *Heliaca*.
- In passing, all those who have set out and explained the medals of the ancients.
- Conrad Dinner — the Greek epithets of the gods.
- Joannes Ravisius Textor and Basilio Zanco — the Latin ones.

**Testimonies about this book.**

The author is wrongly named Vicenzo Catarro by Ortelius, and Carterius by Gesner and by the author of the *Bibliotheca Classica*.

Cartari himself says thus in Book II of his dialogue, which he entitled the *Flavio*:

> I will not tell you that the stork was the bird of Concord according to some, and according to others the crow, nor how the ancients depicted Concord; for I know that soon you will see a little book in which all these things are related in full, with the images of almost all the gods and the reasons why they were so depicted; etc.
> *(Cartari)*

## Doubtful passages

- "che sono in essere; & di proposito" (title): *che sono in essere* literally means "which are in existence"; it is said of the writings rather than of the authors (many of whom died long ago), and is rendered "extant". *Di proposito* means "on purpose, with intent", that is, having written of the subject as such and not incidentally (compare "Di passaggio", "in passing", below).
- Braces: in the first printing the names Zanco, Aurelio, Montefalco, Goltzius, Gruterus are joined by a brace to the single words "I cognomi" (the surnames); Montefalco and Cartari by a brace to the words "Le Feste, & Celebrità". In the translation the braces are replaced by a dash after the list of names. In the second printing there is no brace; the words "I cognomi" stand to the right of the column of names.
- The word "Moderni" stands on a line of its own in the first printing ("MODERN."), but in the second on the same line as the end of the list of ancient authors; in the translation it is set off by a heading in both cases.
- "Cognomi": rendered "surnames", the word this translation uses for the names given to the gods (Greek epithets, *Epiteti*, are rendered "epithets").
- "Consatratione" (first printing), "Consucratiene" (second): misprints for *Consecratione*; "the consecration" is the literal sense; perhaps deification, apotheosis (*consecratio*) is meant. Rendered "the consecration".
- "Gio. Rocaccio" (second printing) is a misprint for *Boccaccio*; "Geraldi" for *Giraldi*; "Arnochio" for *Arnobio*; "Dinneuo" in the second printing (apparently a misprint for "Dinnero" of the first); "Auersa" for *Anuersa*; "Schotto" for *Scotto*. All are silently brought to a single form, except "Dinneuo": following the glossary note it is rendered as printed, "Dinnevo" (u = v), while the first printing gives "Dinner".
- [?1] "Libro 11." (second printing, scan 0039): for *Libro II* (the numerals "11" are misprinted in the second printing); rendered by sense as "Book II" with [?]; the first printing correctly has "Libro II". In the second printing the quotation from the *Flavio* runs on in the same line as the introductory words; in the translation it is set off as a block, as in the first.
- "la dipingessero" (scans 0024, 0039): in the quotation *la* could refer either to Concord (*Concordia*) or to the bird; by sense the goddess is meant, so the object is named explicitly: "how the ancients depicted Concord".
- "Cornacchia" (scan 0024, quotation from the *Flavio*): *cornacchia* is the crow.
- "Libretto" (in the quotation): "little book", as Cartari calls his own book.
- "Abramo Ortelio ha posto in luce le teste di LIV. Deità": the Roman numeral LIV (54) is kept.
- "Dialoghi fatti Latini di A. Agostini": the Latin translation of the *Dialogues* (on medals) of Antonio Agostini; rendered "translated into Latin".
- "Misterij della Gran Madre delli Dei": title of a work by Pignoria; rendered *Mysteries of the Great Mother of the Gods*.
- "Heliaca": title of a work by Aleandro; kept as *Heliaca*.
- "Bibliotheca Classica": kept as printed (the title is not identified; "the author" means its compiler).
- "Vicenzo Catarro", "Carterio": distortions of the name Cartari; given as they are ("Vicenzo Catarro", "Carterius").
- "Gio. Rauisio Testore": Ravisius Textor (glossary); the first name "Gio." is given in its Latin form, Joannes, as with the other non-Italian modern authors (Andreas Schottus, Franciscus Sweertius).
- "Fornuto, o come altri lo chiamano, Cornuto": the two forms are distinguished, "Phurnutus" and "Cornutus".
- Name forms follow the glossary (Latinised or the usual English forms: Goltzius, Gruterus, Pictorius, Barlaeus, du Choul, Chacón, Sweertius, Selden, Aleandro, and so on), not the Italianised spellings of the text.
- The endings "Firmianus", "Firmicus", "Liberalis", "Bassus" follow the usual Latinised forms of these names.
