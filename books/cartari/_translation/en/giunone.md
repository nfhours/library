# Juno

Rules: books/cartari/_translation/en/RULES.md

<aside>Sister of Jupiter</aside>

Those who said that the ancients, under the names of various gods, worshipped the elements took Juno to be the air, and the fables therefore afterwards made her the sister of Jupiter, by whom they understood the element of fire. And as he is king, so they called her queen of Heaven, because fire and air are the two upper elements, which have far greater force in created things than the other two.

<aside>Wife of Jupiter</aside>
<aside>Virgil</aside>

And at times they also said that she was the earth, and therefore the wife of Jupiter; for they hold that from the higher bodies there falls to earth a certain seminal virtue, which gives it the power to produce everything that it produces, just as a husband, spreading his seed in the womb of his wife, makes her conceive what she afterwards bears in her time. For which reason Virgil said:

> Great Jupiter descended with fertile rains
> into the bosom of his joyful wife.
>
> *(Virgil)*

<aside>Eyebrows guarded by Juno</aside>

And some, wishing to place this goddess still higher, made her one and the same with the Moon, and gave her some of the Moon's epithets: thus they called her Lucina, as if it were she who, by helping women in childbirth, gave light (*luce*) to the children being born. From this it came about that the ancients, when they divided the human body and gave each god his own part to take care of, placed the eyebrows under the guardianship of Juno, because these are above the eyes, by which we enjoy the light that is given to us by her, and they seem to defend them from whatever might fall and come to trouble them.

Though we also read that the arms were likewise consecrated to her. Hence in Homer, who gives each god one limb more beautiful than the others, Juno has beautiful, white arms. And from this it came about that some of the ancients represented her with a clean and pure body, having regard, perhaps, to the body of the Moon.

<aside>Lucian</aside>
<aside>Syrian Goddess</aside>

Lucian writes that although the Syrian Goddess, so greatly revered at Hierapolis, a city of Assyria, was Juno, nevertheless her statue, which stood there in her temple, showed her to be not one only but many, since one saw in it something of Pallas, something of Venus, of Diana, of Nemesis, of the Parcae and of other goddesses; for she sat upon two lions, and held a sceptre in one hand and a spindle in the other, and on her head had certain rays and certain other things that are proper to different images. Hence Lucian in effect shows that the Syrian Goddess, that is, Juno, was a numen worshipped in different ways under different names.

> Image of Juno Lucina, and of the Syrian Goddess of Hierapolis in Assyria, who is one and the same as Juno; and of the birds sacred to her, signifying that Juno is queen of Heaven, ruler of the air, lady of kingdoms and of riches. She was understood also as virtue.

And so it is no wonder that she was believed to be Lucina as well, and that women called on her for help in childbirth, as Glycerium does in Terence when she cries out:

> Juno Lucina, help me, and guard me, I pray you, from death.
>
> *(Terence)*

And when they wished to make statues of her, or to paint her, the ancients depicted her, as can be seen on the ancient medals of Faustina, in the form of a woman of already mature age, dressed in the manner of a matron, who holds a cup in her right hand and a spear in her left.

And there are few images of the gods to which the ancients did not give spears, as can be seen in those already described, and will be seen again in those that remain to be described; and so it does not seem to me that I should put off giving the reason any longer. Though perhaps it would have been better elsewhere, still it will not be amiss to give it here too, where someone could easily be surprised that a spear is given to Juno, a peaceful and quiet goddess.

<aside>Homer</aside>

But she was not always such; on the contrary, at times she has shown herself very terrible and fierce, as when with all her strength she wished to help the Greeks against the Trojans and dared to go into battle together with Minerva, as Homer relates, who paints her chariot in this way because in those times commanders and the most distinguished persons fought in chariots. The beam that supported it crosswise was of iron; the wheels were of copper and had eight similar spokes, but the rims that go around them were of gold, covered on the outside with copper, and the hub from which these spokes came out was encircled with silver. Above, where the goddess stood, was a seat made with straps of gold and silver; the pole was of silver, the yoke of gold, and likewise of gold were the ornaments of the horses, because although at other times Juno had herself drawn by birds, then she needed horses.

And Virgil likewise gives her a chariot and arms, when he says that she loved Carthage so well that she kept her chariot and her arms there.

So it should not seem wrong to anyone that the ancients gave a spear to Juno also, nor that I, speaking of her, say why spears were given to the statues of the gods, according to the reason Justin gives for it, who says that in the earliest times kings carried a spear in place of the diadem and the royal emblem; and that then, at the beginning of the world, men had no other statues of the gods than spears, and therefore bowed down before these and worshipped them reverently. But after they began to make the gods in human form, they worshipped no longer the spears but the statues; nevertheless, in order still to preserve the memory of the ancient religion, they afterwards added spears to the sacred idols.

When Anchises in Virgil shows Aeneas his progeny that is to come, he begins with a youth leaning on a spear, and here Servius notes that the spear among the ancients was an honoured prize for those young men who, defeating the enemy in battle, had begun to show their valour. And likewise he says that the ancients esteemed the spear above all other arms, and that it was a sign of pre-eminence and of command, and was therefore given to valiant men; that things sold in public were sold under the spear; and that the Carthaginians, wanting war with the Romans, sent them a spear.

<aside>Suda</aside>

Suda reports that it was a custom in Athens that when someone who had been killed was carried to burial, the relatives who accompanied him had a spear carried with him, or planted it at the head of the grave, thereby making it known to the one who had killed him that he would not get past it without vengeance. So the spear was highly esteemed by the ancients, and among them was a very notable emblem. Hence it is no wonder that they often gave it to sacred statues.

<aside>Boccaccio</aside>

Of the chariot of Juno described by Homer it could be said that it signifies the various colours that are sometimes seen in the air; but Boccaccio holds otherwise, and says that it is made so richly because she was believed to be the goddess of riches, and that the arms given to her signify that for the most part men fight one another over riches.

<aside>Goddess of riches</aside>

And therefore they painted her with a sceptre in her hand, as though it were in her power to give riches and kingdoms, as she promised to do to Paris when she wanted to be judged by him the most beautiful of those two other goddesses. And this, others too say, is only too true, if by her we understand the earth, as Fulgentius writes, who depicts Juno with her head wrapped in a cloth and with a sceptre in her hand, showing by this that to rule is nothing other than to possess countries, and by the other that riches lie covered and hidden in the earth, because it holds in itself the veins of all the metals and in it the precious gems are found.

<aside>Peacock given to Juno</aside>

The peacock was given to this goddess as her own bird and consecrated to her. Hence Pausanias, describing the things that were in the temple of Juno in a certain part of Greece, says that there was a peacock there made entirely of gold and gleaming gems, offered and dedicated to the goddess by the emperor Hadrian as the bird consecrated to her; of which, besides the fable that is told of Argus, they say the cause is that riches draw our minds to themselves just as the peacock by its beauty draws to itself the eyes of onlookers.

And Boccaccio, where he recounts the progeny of the gods, makes a long discourse, wishing to show that the rich and powerful resemble the peacock in almost all their affairs, in that they speak proudly, are arrogant and always want to stand above others, like being praised, though falsely, and other similar things, which, as in Boccaccio's time, so today might well be found in many.

<aside>Birds given to Juno</aside>
<aside>Sign of nobility</aside>

Nor was the peacock alone given to Juno, but the ancients consecrated other birds to her as well, among which was a certain kind of hawk, and the vulture too, as Aelian says, according to the Egyptians, who for that reason crowned the statue of Isis with the feathers of this bird, because Isis was the same for them; and they also placed them around the entrances of houses; and Alexander ab Alexandro reports that in Egypt they did this as a sign of nobility and of the antiquity of the family.

The goose likewise was consecrated to Juno, and the Romans kept several in her temple, which were the best guard of the Capitol when the Gauls besieged it and would have got in there one night in secret, if these had not cried out; so they were afterwards fed there at public expense, and the censors in particular had charge of them, and one was made of silver in the same temple of Juno. And in order to show themselves duly grateful to this beast, which had done them so great a service, the Romans ordained that every year at a certain time a goose should be carried about the city with great ceremony on a handsome, well-adorned litter, and that at the same time a dog should be impaled, the stake being of elder, to punish it for the bad guard it kept on the Capitol, defended by the goose, as I have said.

<aside>Iris</aside>

Besides this, the poets said that Iris, who signifies the celestial rainbow, was the herald and messenger of Juno, and that she was the daughter of Thaumas, which signifies wonder, because when she appears she seems marvellous on account of the colours she shows, just as riches make fools marvel, and these go away as quickly as we see Iris vanish. She too was likewise called a goddess by the ancients, and was depicted in the form of a woman in a garment of various colours, and sometimes yellow, girt up all around, in order to be swifter on her way whenever her goddess commanded her, and with wings likewise of various colours, as Virgil said, where Juno sends her to cut Dido's fatal lock.

> Image of Iris, messenger of Juno, daughter of Thaumas, or of wonder: by her colours a symbol of riches, which are a marvel to fools and quickly vanish.

<aside>Nymphs of Juno</aside>

Juno also had fourteen nymphs in her service, as in Virgil she says to Aeolus, promising him Deiopea, the most beautiful, as his wife, if he would let loose the winds, of which he was believed king, and send them to trouble the sea, so that Aeneas could not reach Italy. These, it is said, showed the changes of the air, which is understood by Juno, and the various incidents that appear in it, such as clear weather, gusts of wind, rains, snows, lightning flashes, thunders, mists and other such things.

<aside>Martianus Capella</aside>
<aside>Image of Jupiter [?1]</aside>

Martianus Capella likewise shows these same things when he imagines that Juno sits beneath Jupiter, and describes her in this way. She has her head covered with a certain bright and white veil, over which is a crown adorned with precious gems, such as the green Scythis, the fiery Ceraunius and the whitening Hyacinth, placed on it by Iris; her face almost always shines, and is very like her brother's, except that he is always cheerful and is never troubled, whereas Juno changes in countenance and sometimes shows a cloudy face. The garment that she has underneath seems of glass, clear and bright, but the cloak above is dark and murky, yet in such a way that if some light touches it, it shines; and a band of various colours girds her knees, which sometimes shines with wondrous beauty, and sometimes the variety of colours grows so thin that it no longer appears.

<aside>Hesiod</aside>

Her shoes are likewise of a dark colour, and have soles so black that they represent the darkness of night; although Hesiod imagines them to be gilded, and the other poets do the same. This goddess holds the thunderbolt in her right hand and a resounding tympanum in her left.

<aside>Statue of Juno</aside>

And this image shows the properties of the air, and what comes from it, so openly that there is no need to say more about it, and so I go on to give a great statue of Juno, which, as Pausanias writes, was in the country of Corinth, made of gold and ivory by Polykleitos, with a crown on its head on which the Horae and the Graces were carved with wonderful art; and in one hand she held a pomegranate and in the other a sceptre, on which a cuckoo sat; because, say the fables, Jupiter, once in love with Juno, changed himself into this bird, and she, in play, as young girls do, took it; and so he then had the opportunity to lie with her.

> Image of Juno, queen of the gods, wife of Jupiter, understood as the air, and image of Juno of Corinth and of the bird the cuckoo, into which Jupiter changed himself when he first lay with the said goddess Juno, his sister.

<aside>Apuleius</aside>

To this Pausanias added that although he does not believe such things, nor the other similar ones that are told of the gods, he does not think they are to be scorned, as though he meant to say that they are mysterious and show something other than what the words sound like; but what meaning they have he does not say, and so I too do not say it, because I have already said several times that I do not wish to set down anything about which the ancients have not written. And although it may be that perhaps someone has already written of this, I have nevertheless not yet found it. But then Apuleius, when he describes the judgement of Paris performed on the stage, says that there came forth a young girl who resembled Juno, with a modest face, her head bound with a white diadem and a sceptre in her hand, accompanied by Castor and Pollux, who had on their heads a helmet with a crest of a star; and made thus they are seen on some ancient medals.

<aside>Castor and Pollux</aside>

We read that they were sons of Jupiter and so loving toward each other that, as the fables feigned, dividing life between them, they lived and died by turns, for which they deserved to be placed in Heaven, where they make the sign of the Gemini, which even today are figured by those who draw the things of Heaven in the way that the Lacedaemonians once made an idol of them; and it was in this fashion: they set two beams at an equal distance from each other, crossed likewise by two other beams, as if this image suited the equal love of the two brothers, of whom one was the strongest at wrestling and the other on horseback; so they were also sometimes depicted on two white horses, and these were perhaps the ones which they say Juno gave them, having herself had them first from Neptune, named one Xanthus, the other Cyllarus. And thus, on horseback, they were among the Athenians in a certain very ancient temple of theirs.

<aside>Justin</aside>
<aside>Locrians</aside>
<aside>Crotonians</aside>

In this way they also appeared to Vatinius, as Tullius writes, when he was returning from Rieti to Rome, and told him that on that day King Perseus had been taken prisoner. We also read, and Justin writes it, that in a certain battle in which fifteen thousand Locrians were victorious against a hundred and twenty thousand Crotonians, two young men appeared on two white horses, tall and handsome, armed differently from all the others, with purple garments about them, who fought valiantly before all the others on behalf of the Locrians, and vanished immediately after the victory. These were believed to be Castor and Pollux, because the Locrians, not having been able to get help from the Lacedaemonians, had asked help of them.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

And how Castor and Pollux were represented was also shown by two Messenian youths, according to Pausanias, when with a cunning pretence they wished to deceive the Lacedaemonians on a day when they were solemnly celebrating their festival in the camp. For, having put on two white tunics with purple cloaks over them, and with spears in hand, on two most beautiful horses, they showed themselves suddenly. The Lacedaemonians thought that they were Castor and Pollux, come to the festival celebrated for them, and went out to meet them, all unarmed, worshipping them and praying that they would be willing to stay among them with a favourable numen, when the two youths, throwing themselves among them, wounding now these, now those, with their spears, killed many, and having made no small slaughter of the enemy, returned without being in the least hurt by them.

Besides this, Castor and Pollux had caps on their heads, as Pompeius Festus says, because they were from Laconia, where they used to go into battle with caps on their heads. And therefore Catullus in a certain epigram of his calls them Pileate brothers, because pileus, which is a Latin word, means cap in the vernacular. Pausanias likewise writes that in a certain place in Laconia there were some little figures wearing pilei, which he does not know very well whether they were made for the Castors (for under the name of the one the ancients understood both brothers), but he does think so.

> Image of Castor and Pollux, gods of sailors, signifying calm at their appearance, who are also protectors of horses, being stars most swift in their course, and one of the twelve signs of the Zodiac, called the two twins, sons of Jupiter and Leda.

<aside>Sign of liberty</aside>

Nor shall I now fail to say that the pileus among the Romans was the badge of liberty, because it was their custom that when they wanted to give freedom to a slave they had his head shaved and gave him a cap to wear. This ceremony was performed in the temple of Feronia, because she was the goddess of those to whom freedom (*libertà*) was given, and they were called Libertini.

<aside>Plautus</aside>

Hence in Plautus a slave desirous of freedom speaks thus:

> May God grant that I may today, with shaven head, take the cap.
>
> *(Plautus)*

And we read that in Rome, after Julius Caesar was killed, spears with the pileus on top were planted in the squares, wishing in that way to call the people and the whole city to their former liberty. When the Romans had need of soldiers, or when someone then wanted to stir up tumult and sedition, they called the slaves to the pileus, meaning thereby that they gave freedom to all, so that they would fight for it. Hence too it is that on certain ancient medals of Brutus a cap is seen placed above two daggers, showing thereby that he killed the tyrant and restored liberty to his country.

When Nero died, the populace in Rome, as Suetonius writes, and in the provinces as well, went about rejoicing with caps on their heads, wishing in that way to show that they were freed from grievous and cruel servitude. And we read in Plutarch that Lucius Terentius, a most noble Roman, went behind the triumph of Scipio with a cap on his head, as if he had been his freedman; and this because he had been freed by him from the Carthaginians, who had earlier taken him prisoner; and many Romans did the same in the triumph of Titus Quinctius, ransomed by him after he had conquered Macedonia, as, besides Plutarch, Livy also writes.

Besides this, the cap was a sign of virtue and of great learning, and for this reason it is still given today together with the title of doctor and master. And the ancients also sometimes put slaves up for sale with a cap on their heads, as Gellius reports, but only those who had no defect; so that the cap meant that the buyer could not be deceived, and that the seller was therefore free and was not afterwards bound to anything, as though it were a sure sign of the soundness and goodness of the slave sold.

But to return to the Castors: since, as I said, Pollux too is understood under this name, Bibulus, who was consul together with Caesar, made a jest of it when he saw that his colleague had so usurped all the authority of the consulship, and that whatever the two of them did was said to be done by Caesar alone, saying that what had happened to him was what happened to Pollux, who in the temple dedicated to him and to his brother had no name, because it was called the temple of Castor alone, or of the Castors.

<aside>Aelian</aside>
<aside>Why the Castors are called upon by sailors</aside>

These, then, were represented, as Aelian says and Suda reports, as tall young men, beardless, like one another, with military garments about them, with swords at their sides and spears in their hands, and in place of the stars I spoke of, they sometimes also put little flames on their heads. For we read that when the Argonauts were once strangely troubled by a heavy storm at sea, so that all feared to perish, and Orpheus had made vows for the safety of all, two stars, or flames, appeared above the head of the Castors, which gave them a sign of safety; and hence it came about afterwards that the Castors were called upon by sailors in their dangers.

Hence Pausanias, writing of a certain statue of Neptune which was among the Corinthians, says that on its base the Castors were carved, as those who were believed to be numina that bring safety to ships and to sailors. They were also believed to be certain stars, or lights, which, as Seneca and Pliny write, are accustomed to appear at sea in great storms and give a sign of calm. And since these show themselves in the air, and the air is represented by Juno, it was reasonable that the two brothers Castor and Pollux were placed in the company of this goddess.

<aside>Theopompus. Hellanicus</aside>

And of her the fables feign, as Theopompus and Hellanicus relate, that Jupiter once bound her feet with chains of gold, adding very heavy weights of iron, so that she hung in the air; wishing by this to signify that the lower part of the air, which is farthest from the element of fire and is therefore denser, where the clouds, the mists and other such things are formed, easily unites with water and earth, which are heavy elements and always descend.

<aside>Varro</aside>
<aside>The flammeum, veil of brides</aside>

We read in Pausanias that in a certain part of Boeotia there was a temple consecrated to Juno, in which was a very large idol of her, standing upright, and there she was called the bride. But it seems to me that she had this name with better reason on the island of Samos. For Varro writes, and Lactantius repeats it, that this island was first called Parthenia after Juno, who stayed there while she was a girl and a maiden, and was married there as well to Jupiter; and so in her temple there was a beautiful idol made in the form of a bride, which must have had that coloured veil with which new brides covered their faces, and it was called the flammeum, perhaps from the colour of flame, because it was red and showed that the girl who was to be joined to a man blushed with shame. So some have said of this veil, although some others hold that it should be understood otherwise, as I shall say later when I draw Hymenaeus.

And so Varro writes that the ancients observed the custom that newly married couples should come together only at night, as though honest girls would have less to be ashamed of in the darkness of night. And the brides went to the husband at night, carried in a litter by mules or oxen, as Suda writes; and the litter was made in such a way that the bride sat in the middle, the bridegroom on one side, and on the other the most honoured and dearest friend or relative he had, from which perhaps there has come today among us the custom for the bridegroom to find one of his dearest friends who, attending the wedding with him, is afterwards called “best man”.

<aside>Plutarch</aside>
<aside>Torches before brides</aside>

And before them, as can be gathered from Plutarch in his Questions, five boys carried as many lit torches, of pitch pine or of hawthorn; which torches, besides the service they performed in driving away the darkness of night, also gave by their light a sign and good omen of the offspring that was expected of that marriage, since to beget is nothing other than to bring forth into light.

<aside>Even and odd number</aside>

Nor could there be more than five, because according to some it was believed that a woman could bear up to five children at one birth, and no more. But others, considering the matter more subtly, have said that the ancients used the odd number at weddings as a sign of peace and union, because it cannot be divided into two equal parts without there always remaining one in the middle, which can afterwards join them together again, as common to both; so it was believed that the odd number is pleasing to the gods of Heaven, authors of peace and quiet, and the even to those of the Underworld, from whom come discord and disunion, just as the even number can be disunited by making two equal parts of it, with nothing remaining in the middle to reunite them.

And they took five, because this is the first number that arises from the union of the first two numbers, even and odd, which are three and two; for one is not a number, but a principle from which counting begins. And they likewise called upon five gods, and worshipped them with devout prayers. These were Jupiter and Juno adult, that is, no longer children; Venus, Suada and Diana.

<aside>Fire and water presented to the bride</aside>

Besides this, the ancients set before the new bride fire and water, either to show her that just as fire by itself can produce nothing, nor nourish it, having no moisture at all, and water still less, being wholly cold (for which reason, for the generation of animals and of all the other things produced by nature, heat and moisture must be joined together), so, to preserve the human race, it is necessary that man and woman be joined together; or to give her to understand, by the fire, which purges and separates the pure from the impure, and by the water, which washes out stains and takes away filth, that she must keep herself chaste, pure and clean, and guard herself against everything that can stain the laws of marriage.

They also made her carry the spindle and the distaff, and pass over a sheep's skin with all its wool the first time she entered her husband's house, and they used many other ceremonies; but let these few suffice for now, to give an idea of how Juno is to be depicted in the form of a bride, since Varro did not say it when he said that there was such an idol of hers on the island of Samos.

<aside>Juno as bride</aside>

But to return to what we said on the report of Pausanias, that Juno in Boeotia was called the bride, let us see the reason, as he gives it, who writes of it. Juno, angry with Jupiter, once left him and went to Euboea, that is Negroponte; and he, who nevertheless wished to placate her and make her return, but did not know in what way, asked advice of Cithaeron, then lord of that place, who suggested to him that he should have a statue made of oak and carry it about covered so that it could not be seen, pretending it to be a young girl whom he had newly made his bride.

So Jupiter did, and the new bride was already being led about, when Juno, who had learned of this and was greatly vexed by it, came out and drew near to the cart where she believed the new bride was hidden, and, full of jealousy and anger, tore the cloths that covered her, and finding it a wooden statue, rejoiced greatly and made peace with Jupiter, and stayed with him as a new bride. Hence the ancients afterwards celebrated some days of festival in memory of this fable, which Plutarch, as Eusebius reports, interprets in this way.

The discord that arose between Juno and Jupiter is nothing other than the unbalancing of the elements, from which comes the destruction of things; just as through their balance, or a certain proportion among them, the same things are born and preserved.

<aside>Oak, very useful</aside>

If Juno, then, that is moist and windy nature, is opposed to Jupiter [?2], that is the hot and dry power, and scorns him, there will be as many rains, flooding the earth, as there once were in the country of Boeotia, which went wholly under the waters; and so, when these afterwards went down and the earth was left uncovered, the fables feigned that Jupiter and Juno were reconciled, and that the veils were torn and the statue of oak was seen. For they say that the first tree that sprang up out of the earth was the oak, which, as Hesiod says, was of double benefit to mortals, since from its branches they gathered the acorns on which they lived before, and from the trunk they made roofs.

<aside>Roses of Juno</aside>
<aside>Milky Way. Tertullian</aside>

For Juno the ancients made wreaths of white lilies, which they called the roses of Juno, because, tinted with her milk, they became white, as the fables relate, saying that Jupiter, while she slept, put Hercules, still a little child, to her breasts, so that, having nourished him with her milk, she should not hate him afterwards. But he, sucking too greedily, made the goddess wake; and recognising him, she at once thrust him away from her, so that the milk, which was still flowing, was for the most part scattered over Heaven and made there that certain white band which is still seen there, which the astrologers call the Milky Way, and the rest fell down to earth upon the lilies, which were left so tinted with white that ever since they have always been born white.

Tertullian writes that in Argos, a city of Greece, there was an idol of Juno encircled with vine branches, and which had under its feet the skin of a lion: as though she wished to have those branches in contempt of Bacchus, and this skin likewise in dishonour of Hercules, because she hated both of them greatly, being stepmother to both, according to the fables.

> Image of Juno of Argos, of Juno the Saviour in Lanuvium, and of Juno, queen of the gods, patroness of the air, stepmother and hater of Bacchus and Hercules, purifier and cleanser of things, which is the proper effect of the air.

<aside>Titus Livy. Marcus Tullius</aside>

In Lanuvium, a city of Latium, Juno Sospita was worshipped, whom we can call the saviour, as the principal numen of that place, according to what Titus Livy relates; and she had her statue there, as Marcus Tullius writes, with the skin of a goat about her body, with a spear in one hand and a small shield in the other.

<aside>Juno of February. Festus</aside>

And Festus, speaking of Juno of February, of why she had this name, says that the Romans sacrificed to her in the month of February, and that the Lupercalia celebrated in this month were consecrated to her, in which the Luperci ran through the city and purified the women, who for this purpose held out their hand to them, and they struck them with that of which Juno's jerkin is made, which is the skins of goats.

<aside>Virgil</aside>
<aside>Vicus Iugarius. Juno Iugalis. Couples bound together</aside>

Besides this, it is found that the ancients sometimes made the statue of Juno also with shears in her hand, as Suda reports, and he gives the reason for it, saying that the air, understood by Juno, purges and cleanses, as shears, cutting the hair, make bodies smooth and clean. And on an ancient medal of the emperor Nerva is seen a matron crowned with rays, who sits on a high throne and holds a sceptre in her left hand and shears in her right. Many judged this to be Juno; nevertheless the letters that are on that medal call her the Fortune of the Roman people. Nor do I remember having seen or read of any other image or statue of Juno, except one made by some who know that the ancients called her the inventor of marriage, and that she had charge of weddings; hence Dido in Virgil, when she has decided to take Aeneas as her husband, sacrifices to certain gods, but before all to Juno:

> She who has charge of the marriage knot.
>
> *(Virgil)*

These have depicted her standing, clothed, with poppy heads in her hand and a yoke at her feet, wishing to show by this how husband and wife must stay joined together, and by the heads the numerous offspring that then follows. Of this I find no mention in any of the ancients, but I do find that in Rome a certain place was called Vicus Iugarius, because Juno is called Iugalis, as if by the favour of her numen man and woman were joined (*si giungessero*) together; she had an altar there, to which the newly married went, and were bound together by the priest with certain knots, giving them thereby to understand that their minds should always be bound thus in one will, as their bodies were then by those knots.

<aside>Marriage</aside>
<aside>Hymenaeus</aside>

Hence it came about that some, perhaps taking the example from this, and from what can be seen in the image of Venus made in fetters, have depicted marriage with a yoke on the neck and with fetters on the feet. Some have held that this was introduced first by Juno, as I have said, some by Venus, and some others by Hymenaeus, who was therefore worshipped as the god of weddings, at which they invoked him with certain solemn prayers, that he might be favourable to them and give them a happy outcome by his numen.

> Image of Juno, inventor or protector of marriage, called Juno Iugalis, and of the yoke and the birds sacred to her, signifying the duty of the married and the succession, or offspring, that comes from a harmonious marriage.

<aside>Gall thrown away</aside>

But we read also that the ancients, showing by many ceremonies the peace and union that ought to be between husband and wife, and desiring for them every good and consolation, in celebrating weddings named nothing except what could give good omen and a sign of happiness. Hence they also often called upon the crow, as is seen in the image of Concord; and sacrificing to Juno Iugalis they cut the gall out of the victim and threw it behind the altar, to show that between husband and wife there must be no bitterness of hatred, nor any anger.

<aside>Tale of Hymenaeus</aside>

And for this reason some hold that Hymenaeus too was invoked, not because he had ordained marriage, but because after many labours and grave dangers he obtained the wedding he desired, with a most happy outcome; and the tale is this. Hymenaeus was a youth in Athens, son of Apollo and of Calliope, one of the nine Muses, so beautiful, and with a face so delicate, that by many he was thought to be a woman, and he fell most ardently in love with a beautiful and most noble girl, and without hoping ever to enjoy her love, because he was, by family, far too inferior in blood and riches to the girl, he went about nourishing himself as best he could on the sight of the beloved, and followed her always and wherever he was allowed and permitted to go, and he often found himself among the other girls so arrayed (his smooth cheek helping him much in this) that he was easily taken for one of them.

While the poor boy was thus deceiving others, but even more himself, it happened that he was carried off with his beloved, and with many other most noble girls of Athens, who had gone together outside the city for the sacrifices of Eleusinian Ceres, by pirates who arrived there suddenly. These, when they were many miles away from Athens, pleased with their booty, put in to land, and withdrawing to a certain place where they felt safe, tired already from the continual sailing, fell asleep. Then Hymenaeus, seizing the occasion to free himself and the abducted girls, killed them all before any of them woke, and having put the girls back in a safe place, returned to the city and promised the Athenians to restore to them their already lost daughters, if they would give him as wife the one he loved so much.

This was readily granted him, since it seemed to everyone that he had well deserved her. And so Hymenaeus had the girl he had so much desired. Having celebrated a solemn and joyful wedding, he lived afterwards with her happily all his life. Since, then, these maidens were recovered by him, and the marriage that had been so desired had a happy outcome, the ancients often repeated his name at weddings for good omen, as if they wished for those who were marrying the happiness of Hymenaeus.

<aside>Thalassius, called upon at weddings</aside>

And this was a custom of the Greeks, just as it was of the Romans to call upon Thalassius at weddings, likewise for good omen. For, as Livy writes, when the Sabine women were carried off by the Romans, a most beautiful girl fell into the hands of a poor soldier, and when asked, he said that he was taking her to Thalassius, because he had already seen that someone had cast an eye on her in order to take her from him. Thalassius was then a commander of great valour, and held in great respect for that, so that, having heard his name, no one dared even to touch the girl; on the contrary, forming a faithful escort for the one who had her, they all went together crying out “To Thalassius, to Thalassius”, who held the beautiful girl very dear, and with a joyful wedding made her his wife, and they afterwards lived happily together always. They called upon Thalassius, then, wishing the newly married the good fortune which that abducted girl had through his name.

<aside>Varro</aside>

Or else it was because Thalassius means a certain basket in which women kept wool and the other things for spinning, and the ancients, according to Varro, by often repeating this word at weddings, wished to remind the bride what her duty was to be, since she was married: which Plutarch also confirms in his Questions, reporting also what I said a little above, that the bride, entering her husband's house the first time, carried with her the distaff and the spindle, and passed over the skin of a sheep, or sat on it, as Festus writes; because from it the wool was got that is then prepared for spinning, and she said these words: “Where you are Gaius, I am Gaia”, which came to show that everything must be common between husband and wife, and that in the house they must be equally masters.

And some hold that the name Gaia was used in this ceremony out of regard for Gaia Caecilia, who was Tanaquil, wife of Tarquinius Priscus, king of the Romans, a wise and virtuous woman, who governed her house excellently. Thus Varro writes, and Pliny repeats it, that at a certain time her spindle and distaff were kept as a thing worthy of reverence; and some add her slippers as well. Hence, they say, came the custom for the bride to carry with her the distaff with wool and the spindle, in order to remember to imitate the virtue of that great woman, who spun and made with her own hand a beautiful royal garment for Servius Tullius, her son-in-law, who succeeded her husband in the kingdom, which was afterwards placed in the temple of Fortune.

<aside>Herculean knot</aside>

The new bride also went girded with a certain narrow band of wool over her shirt, with the Herculean knot, which was untied by the bridegroom on the first night that he was with her, taking from it an omen of being as fortunate in having children as Hercules was, who left seventy.

<aside>Goddess Virginensis</aside>

And to this end she called to her aid the goddess Virginensis, because she was believed to take care that the virginal band, worn by girls all the time they remain virgins, should be happily untied as soon as they are married.

<aside>Mutinus</aside>

And the ancients used, as St Augustine reports from Varro, to bring this goddess, together with some others, into the room where the newly married were to be together the first night, so that with the help of these the bridegroom might more easily gather the desired flower, and it be less defended by the bride, seeing so many gods around her who all encouraged her to it, each according to his office, for the offices were divided among them in this business, in which the general presiding gods seemed to be Venus and Priapus, and Priapus was also given a particular office: and they then called him the god Mutinus, so that he should give strength to the bridegroom to labour vigorously, and put it into the bride's heart to offer no resistance.

<aside>Iugatinus. Subigus. Prema. Partunda</aside>

There were also the god Iugatinus, to join (*giungere*) husband and wife; the god Subigus, who saw to it that the one should subdue and the other let herself be subdued easily; the goddess Prema, who induced the bride to let herself be well pressed (*premere*); and the goddess Partunda, who left no fear at all of the childbirth (*parto*) that was to come. And I believe there were others too, because, as I said at the beginning, the ancients gave particular gods to everything they did, or under various epithets gave to one alone the care of various things, as Martianus Capella, speaking to Juno on this subject, sets down these four epithets, Iterduca, Domiduca, Unxia and Cinxia, which were given to her in the ceremonies of marriages; and says:

> Rightly the young brides ought to call upon you from the heart, that you may take care of them as they go; that you may lead them safe into the longed-for houses of their bridegrooms; that you may bring it about that the anointing (*vngere*) of the doors is done with good omen; and that you may not abandon them when they lay down the virginal girdle (*cinto*).
>
> *(Martianus Capella)*

From this it follows that Juno was also the goddess Virginensis. But leaving so many gods, of whom I have never found the idols, I return to some of those ceremonies that may serve for the image of Hymenaeus.

The ancients used, then, to bind the doors of the house also with certain bands, or threads of wool, smearing the hinges with pig's lard and with he-goat's fat, as a remedy against all the spells that were often cast on newly married couples, if the creaking of the hinges was heard as the doors were opened or shut. For this reason too, as some have said, the bridegroom scattered nuts, so that nothing should be heard except the noise they made falling on the ground, and the clamour of the boys who gathered them, when the bride cried out and lamented at the untying of the band that I mentioned, for some of them cried out so loudly that it sometimes caused the greatest compassion in whoever heard.

<aside>Pliny</aside>

Others have said that the scattering of the nuts showed that a man, in marrying, left all childish things, because children are accustomed to play often with nuts. Varro held that this was done to draw a good omen from Jupiter, to whom the nuts were consecrated. And Pliny likewise interprets it in another way. But of this and of the other ceremonies used at weddings, what I have said of them is enough, to come to draw the god of those, who was, as I said, Hymenaeus.

> Image of Hymenaeus, god of marriage and of weddings, with the images of several boys gathering scattered nuts, signifying the perpetuity and indissolubility of marriage, the virginal blush, and that one who has taken charge of a house must leave childish things.

<aside>Image of Hymenaeus</aside>

He was depicted by the ancients in the form of a handsome youth crowned with various flowers and green marjoram, who held a lit torch in his right hand, and in his left had that veil, red or yellow, whichever it was, with which new brides covered their head and face the first time they went to a husband. And the reason for this, which a little above I promised to give, is this: that the wives of the priests among the ancient Romans used to wear almost always a similar veil; and since to these it was not permitted, as to others, ever to divorce, in covering the bride with that veil they came to show that they wished that marriage never to be dissolved.

<aside>The god Modesty</aside>

But this does not prevent the same veil from showing also the decent shame of the bride, as I have said; which we can say was one and the same thing with Modesty, held in such respect by the ancients that she was worshipped as a god. Hence the Athenians consecrated an altar to her, and among the Lacedaemonians an idol was made of her for this reason, told by Pausanias.

<aside>Icarius. Penelope. Ulysses</aside>

Icarius had married his daughter Penelope to Ulysses, with the intention that he should never take her away from his house, but that they should all live together always; as he then begged him many times. But it availed him nothing, because Ulysses had resolved to withdraw with his wife to his own home; so the good old man turned to entreat his daughter not to leave him; and although she was already on the road to go away with her husband, he did not cease, accompanying her, to beg her to stay with him. Ulysses at last, overcome by the importunity of his father-in-law, turned to his wife and gave her free licence to do what she wished, either to go with him or to stay with her father.

To this she answered nothing, but only, drawing a veil over her head, covered her face with it. It seemed to the father that he understood perfectly well that his daughter's mind was to go with her husband; so, without saying anything more, he let her go, and on the spot where she covered her face he set up an idol to Modesty, that is, to that decent shame which Penelope showed, in being ashamed to contradict her father in order not to leave her husband; and it must have been made in the same fashion, with the face covered. So that, shame showing itself in this way, it may well be said that for this reason the new bride was covered with the veil which, as I said, Hymenaeus carried in his left hand.

<aside>Catullus</aside>

And to return to setting down the rest about him: on his feet he had two yellow socks; these were a kind of shoe that they used in comedies, and women wore them likewise. And the whole design that I have made of him is described by Catullus in this way:

> O of high Helicon
> happy dweller,
> O of heavenly Urania
> joyful and merry son,
> who into the strong arms
> of the longing lover
> with lawful knot
> place the delicate maiden.
> Hymenaeus girds his temples
> with fair and lovely flowers
> of fragrant marjoram
> and, holding in his hand
> the coloured veil,
> moves gladly among us
> the white foot clothed
> and adorned with the fair gilded sock.
> On this joyful day
> he comes with sweet voice,
> singing to the newly married
> merry little songs.
> With auspicious foot he leads
> the festive dances,
> and with happy right hand
> carries forward the shining torch.
>
> *(Catullus)*

Seneca likewise speaks thus of him:

> You who with happy omen drive away
> the night, carrying in your right hand
> the glad and holy torch, come now to us
> all languid and drunken; but first wreathe
> both your temples with fair flowers and roses.
>
> *(Seneca)*

Claudian in a certain Epithalamium described Hymenaeus in this way:

> From his eyes there comes a most sweet splendour,
> that contents anyone who gazes on him.
> And the warm rays of the Sun and that blush
> that touches and tempts every modest soul
> scatter with a fair purple colour
> the white cheeks, to which the first down
> presents itself, accompanied
> by fair curly and golden hair.
>
> *(Claudian)*
