# Jupiter

Rules: books/cartari/_translation/en/RULES.md

<aside>Jupiter</aside>

So great a reputation did Jupiter acquire among the ancients after he had driven his father Saturn out of the kingdom of Heaven (as the fables tell) that he was held by everyone in the greatest reverence and believed to be the greatest of all the other gods. For this reason they built him many temples and made various idols of him, calling him King and Lord of the universe, as though everything were in his power. They also called him Best and Greatest, since out of his goodness he wished to benefit everyone and do good, and was able to do so through his preeminence, which stood above all the others.

And it is said that he was called Jove (*Gioue*) by the Latins from helping (*giouare*), just as among the Greeks he had a name which showed that life came from him to all things. And for this reason the Platonists took him for the soul of the World, and some believed him to be that divine mind which has produced the universe and governs it, and which is commonly called God.

<aside>Iamblichus</aside>

On this, Iamblichus, speaking of the mysteries of Egypt, says:

> For God stands above all things and shines as one separate from them, and alone, wholly within himself, walks over the universe.
>
> *(Iamblichus)*

<aside>Jupiter sits on the Lotus</aside>

The Egyptians placed him seated upon the Lotus, a water tree, wishing thereby to give us to understand that the matter of the world is subject to him, who rules and governs it without touching it, because his government is wholly intellectual, as the Lotus signifies, in which the leaves and fruits are round, because the divine mind turns upon itself and, understanding always in one and the same manner, governs. From this comes that supreme sovereignty which rules the whole and, separate from all the things of the world, causes them all to move, while he himself remains always quiet in himself, at rest and unmoving; and this the Egyptians showed by placing him seated, as I have said.

<aside>Jupiter is everything</aside>

And this is what the ancients understood by that great Jupiter, King of Heaven, who dwelt in the most sublime part of the universe; who, when considered afterwards with regard to the things which all proceed from him, descends lower and often lends his name to the lower causes and to the things themselves.

<aside>Seneca</aside>
<aside>Jupiter is Fate</aside>

Hence Seneca in the *Natural Questions* wrote that the wiser ancients did not believe Jupiter to be such as he is seen on the Capitol and in other temples, with the thunderbolt in his hand, but understood by him a mind and a spirit, guardian and ruler of the universe, who has made this great machine of the world and governs it in his own way, and that therefore every name suited him, so that he could be called Fate, as though all things depended on him and the order of causes, which stand one above another, came entirely from him.

> Image of Jupiter and of Pan signifying the universe: the one the god of the celestial gods, the other the god of shepherds; the former seated, to signify the immutability of God and his providence, and the latter, standing and in motion, the course of the world.

<aside>Providence</aside>
<aside>Nature</aside>

He could be called Providence, because he provided that the world should go on continually in its ordained course. They could call him Nature, because all things were born from him, and through him lived everything that has life.

<aside>World</aside>
<aside>Virgil</aside>

And they could equally call him World, because everything that is seen is he, who sustains himself by his own power; and so he was believed to be in all places and to fill every thing with himself, as Virgil says:

> The universe is full of supreme Jupiter.
>
> *(Virgil)*

And Orpheus likewise said that Jupiter is first and last of all things, was before all the times that ever have been, and will be after all those that shall come, and that he holds the highest part of the world and touches the lowest too, and is wholly in all places. And making an image of him then, since he has already said that all things are in him, earth, water, air and fire, day and night, he depicts him in the form of the whole world, making the head with its golden hair the bright Heaven, adorned with shining stars, from which two horns, likewise golden, are seen to come out, signifying one the East and the other the West; the eyes are the Sun and the Moon; the air is the broad chest and the spacious shoulders, which have two great wings for the speed of the winds, and because God is most swift toward all things; the ample belly is the great Earth, girdled by the waters of the sea; and the feet are the lowest part of the world, which they place at the centre of the Earth.

<aside>Pan</aside>

This image of Jupiter, made by Orpheus in the form of the universe, leads me to set down here that of Pan too, because of the likeness they bear to each other, and because the ancients likewise showed the universe under the form of this god. Moreover, Zeus Lycaeus among them was the same as Pan, as his idol shows, which was wholly naked, except that it had a goatskin around it; and this god had, as Justin writes, a temple in Rome at the foot of the Palatine hill.

<aside>Virgil</aside>

We read of him, then, that he was one of those gods who dwelt in the mountains, the woods and the groves, because not all the gods of the ancients could stay in Heaven, but many had to be on earth. He was worshipped more than the others by shepherds, as though he were their own particular god and had the guardianship of the flocks more than the others, as Virgil said:

> Pan has the care of the flocks and of the shepherds.
>
> *(Virgil)*

<aside>Panic terror</aside>

And because sometimes the flocks seem to take fright in the woods, and one cannot see where the reason for such fright comes from, the ancients said that it came from Pan, and called Panic terror every fear that came suddenly, whose cause they could not tell, either for the reason I have said, or because Pan was believed to be the first to find out how to sound that great conch which the Tritons carry, with which he made so great a noise in the war against the Titans that he put them all to flight, so terrified that they did not know where they were going; as we read in Pausanias, it happened also to the Gauls in the war which they waged under Brennus against the Greeks.

For, having suffered a great rout by day, the following night they were assailed by this Panic terror, and it seemed first to a few and then to the whole camp that they heard a great trampling of horses and saw the enemy coming against them with the greatest force, whereupon they all took up arms, and, not recognising one another at all (so had that mad fright driven them out of their wits), and each thinking that all the others, by dress and language, were Greeks, they began to fight among themselves and to flee, some this way, some that; the Greeks, being warned of this, fell upon them and killed as many as they wished.

This kind of mad fear, which seems to be without cause, was believed to come from Pan, who was worshipped chiefly in Arcadia and held to be the father of all the other more powerful gods; therefore the perpetual fire was kept in his temple, where, they said, there had anciently been an oracle that answered through the mouth of a nymph named Erato.

The Athenians likewise began to hold him in great respect after he appeared to a man sent by them to ask the Lacedaemonians for help against the Persians, and told him that he would be found helping them on the plains of Marathon. But how he afterwards did so we do not read, except that in that battle a man was seen, in face and dress a countryman, who, after killing a great number of Persians with a plough, vanished and was not seen again. And where Pan met the man of whom I spoke above, in the Parthenian wood, a temple was built to him; in which wood we read that there are excellent tortoises, good for making lyres, but the people of the country do not dare to take them, still less do they let strangers take them, because they hold that all of them are consecrated to Pan. And for this reason one of them will be placed at the foot of his image, and the conch too as a sign of Panic terror.

<aside>Silius Italicus</aside>

Silius Italicus describes him with horns, with goat's ears and with a tail, in this way:

> Glad at his festivals, Pan waves
> his little tail, and with sharp pine
> his temples are wreathed, and from the ruddy
> brow two short horns come out, and the ears
> are like a goat's, long and shaggy;
> the bristly beard falls over the chest
> from the hard chin, and this god carries
> always a shepherd's staff in his hand,
> and the flanks are girded by the spotted
> skin of a timid fallow deer, as are the chest and back.
>
> *(Silius Italicus)*

And it goes on to say that he walks over steep crags, however precipitous they may be, and that in running he is swiftest, just as the world likewise revolves with the greatest speed, which is shown in the image of this god, whose name is Greek and, brought into our language, means the universe. And for this reason Servius said that the ancients gave him horns, wishing to show by them in him the rays of the Sun and the horns of the Moon.

<aside>Boccaccio</aside>

And Boccaccio holds that these horns, which come out from the forehead and tend toward Heaven, show the celestial bodies, of which we have knowledge in two ways: one by art, which with astronomical instruments measures the course of the stars and the distances between them; the other by the effects which we see produced by them in the things here below.

The face purple, red and fiery (for that is how they paint it on Pan) signifies that pure fire which stands above all the other elements, on the border of the celestial spheres. The long beard that goes down over the chest shows that the two upper elements, that is air and fire, are masculine in nature and force and send their impressions into the other two, which are feminine in nature. The spotted skin that covers his chest and shoulders represents to us the eighth sphere, all painted with gleaming stars, which likewise covers everything that belongs to the nature of things.

The shepherd's staff that he has in one hand signifies, according to Boccaccio, the government which nature has over all things, which so rules them that it prescribes to them even the determinate end of their operations, excepting, however, rational creatures. And Servius says that, because this staff was curved, it showed the year, which curves back into itself.

<aside>Macrobius</aside>

In his other hand he has the pipe of seven reeds, because Pan was the first to find out how to join several reeds together with wax, and the first also to play on them, as Virgil says. It shows us the heavenly harmony, which has seven sounds and seven different voices, just as there are seven Heavens that produce it. And Macrobius holds that this is also to be understood by Echo, whom the ancients feigned to have been much loved by the god Pan.

<aside>Echo</aside>

The reason for this is given by Alexander of Aphrodisias, who says that it was an error of the common people to believe that Echo was a goddess and loved by Pan, because she was nothing other than that resounding which voices make when scattered over high and hollow places; and Pan was a learned man who sought with the greatest zeal to understand why voices resounded in that way, and, not being able at times to find it out, felt that displeasure which one often feels who cannot enjoy his beloved.

<aside>Ovid</aside>

Then the fables tell, as Ovid reports, that Echo was a nymph in love with Narcissus, a most beautiful youth, and, being unable to enjoy his love, she drove herself in shame into caverns and hollow caves, and there wasted away with distress and grief, so that her body turned into stone and nothing was left of her but the voice, which Lucretius writes that he has heard repeated in certain places six and seven times. And Pausanias relates that in Greece among the Eleans there was a portico where voices were heard repeated by Echo as many as seven times, and more.

<aside>Ausonius</aside>

We read further of her that she was a goddess, the daughter of air and of tongue, and therefore invisible. Hence in Ausonius she rebukes whoever tries to paint her, in an epigram he composed on this, which says as follows:

> Why do you keep seeking, foolish painter,
> to make a picture of me? For I am such
> that no mortal eye has ever seen me,
> and I have neither form, body, nor colour.
> Of air and of tongue at all hours
> I am born, and then I am the mother of a thing that
> means nothing, since nothing is worth
> the voice that, crying, I send forth.
> When I am about to perish, the last accents
> I renew, and with my own the words of others
> I follow, which then go through the air with the winds.
> I dwell in your ears; and as is the custom of one
> who tries what he cannot do,
> let him paint the sound who wants to paint me.
>
> *(Ausonius)*

<aside>Monsignor Barbaro</aside>

This I have not the heart to do, but I shall set down the image that Monsignor Barbaro, Elect of Aquileia, once made of her, in two stanzas, in this way:

> Echo, daughter of the woods and of the valleys,
> naked spirit and voice, wandering and free,
> eternal example of amorous faults,
> who cut off as much of others' words as you hear;
> so may love turn you back to its more joyful dances
> and give you back your stolen form,
> away from these abandoned and lonely valleys,
> resolve my doubts in simple words.
>
> Echo, what is the end of Love? — *Love.*
> Who makes her road less safe? — *Care.*
> Does she live always, or does she die? — *Dies.*
> Must I flee the harsh fate? — *Harsh.*
> Who will put an end to the great sorrow? — *Hours.*
> How am I to conquer her who is forsworn? — *Swears.*
> Does deceit, then, please Love? — *Pleases.*
> What is the end of it, war or peace? — *Peace.*
>
> *(Monsignor Barbaro)*

At this point it seems to me not out of place, but rather something that must give the readers very great pleasure, to put here that Echo which a modern poet of ours elegantly wrote, drawing from her voice answers corresponding to what he asks of her. He says, then:

> Valleys, Rocks, Mountains, Caves, Grasses and Slopes,
> Hills, Woods, Fountains, Birds and Wild beasts,
> Satyrs, Fauns, and you graceful Nymphs,
> hear in pity my suffering.
> Hear how Love leads me to Death,
> bound in a hard and indissoluble knot? — *I hear.*
> A Voice I hear; come, who are you who answer
> my bitter and sorrowful weeping? — *I.*
> Are you a Nymph perhaps? tell me if you are a Nymph,
> you who form the sound of this Voice? — *I am.*
> So you are a Nymph? now tell me your name too,
> that I may know who is moved to weep with me? — *Echo.*
> Now then, since you are Echo, lend me your ear,
> hear me, if hearing is not displeasing to you. — *It pleases.*
> You see how bitterly I weep;
> let pity for my grief move you? — *I grieve?*
> If you grieve for me, do you wish to offer counsel
> to the deep thought in which I am entangled? — *I wish.*
> But what will your reward be, if my torment
> is at least in some part brought to nothing by you? — *Nothing.*
> I thank you. Now then, advise me,
> since you ask for no other reward? — *You ask.*
> You see, Nymph, how Love consumes me,
> so that I run to death and scarcely notice it? — *I see.*
> What do you advise me? what shall I do so that
> I may find pity there where Love calls me? — *Love.*
> I should like to know what this Love is,
> this that burns me wholly and sets me aflame? — *Flame.*
>
> What flame is this, that does not cease
> to consume, if it never lets up on me? — *Slowly.*
> In what place does it nestle? where does it dwell?
> What part is that where it burns in spite of me? — *In the breast.*
> How does it enter, tell me? where does it find the Way
> to overflow within the breast? — *Through the eyes.*
> Does it enter through the eyes? I seem to have heard
> that many fell in love through hearing. — *Rarely.*
> Tell me, what is its food? where does it feed?
> For it seems that without ceasing it grieves me to the heart? — *Heart.*
> If my heart burns, must I still endure
> the yoke where I lost my freedom? — *You must.*
> Do you wish, then, that I remain in my thought
> still constant, though afflicted and weary? — *Still.*
> So many tears I shed, and nothing avails?
> Tell me, might weeping perhaps be in vain? — *In vain.*
> What shall I do, then, so that for my chaste ardour,
> which burns me, an honest reward may be kept? — *Serve.*
> Do you believe that my Love will be pleasing to her,
> and that she will be content with my service? — *Try.*
> Every way I shall try, if you believe that I
> may win some reward from it afterwards? — *Afterwards.*
> What must I be, then, if sometimes
> sorrow should make me a trembling invalid? — *Firm.*
> But what shall I do, if it so consumes me
> that my life goes out of tune in weeping? — *Tune it.*
> How shall I tune it, if Love does not cease
> to shoot at me from the third sphere? — *Hope.*
> So, gentle Nymph, hoping helps
> and restrains the mortal passion? — *Restrains.*
> What will my life be, if without hope
> Love keeps me captive with a cunning hand? — *Short.*
> If the days of my Life are short,
> will they not be joyful at least, though now you make me sad? — *Sad.*
> What shall I hope for? may I perhaps hope
> that one day Love will make me happy? — *It is allowed.*
> I should like to know who will give me hope,
> since your reason invites me to hope? — *Life.*
> Shall I have life, then? shall I have anything else if I
> never let hope fail me? — *Pains.*
> Pains? by hoping, then, what does it help me?
> But who is the cause that I fear pains? — *Fear.*
>
> Is fear the cause? Pray tell me the truth:
> can fear, then, make me a beggar? — *I say.*
> Alas, wretched me, alas, discourteous, wicked fear,
> does this, then, trouble my pleasure? — *Troubles.*
> Can it do me worse? tell me whether worse can
> befall these afflicted and pallid limbs? — *Death.*
> Death? if fear, then, goes beyond the mark,
> does one sometimes die of excess of Love? — *One dies.*
> How shall I drive it off? the soul wastes away,
> for it does not want it, weeps, and despairs? — *Hope.*
> Yet you say that I should hope; do you perhaps believe
> that hope alone is what consoles others? — *Alone.*
> Will it remove all or part of the torment
> that, alas, consumes me and tears my heart apart? — *Part.*
> So hope by itself
> can never make me blessed? — *Never.*
> But besides Love, service and hope,
> what else is needed? tell me everything part by part? — *Art.*
> Who will give me this art? perhaps Love;
> who else could it be? if not Love himself? — *Himself.*
> Does Love, then, teach lovers,
> does Love show the art of true Love? — *Shows.*
> Tell me, I pray, shall I reveal the flame,
> or do you advise me not to reveal it? — *Reveal.*
> To whom must I reveal it? to everyone perhaps?
> Or will it suffice that only someone understand it? — *One.*
> Do you wish it to be known to one friend only,
> and hidden from the others the mortal body [?1]? — *So.*
> Shall we, then, only three know my ardour,
> if you want me to console myself with one alone? — *Only.*
> But tell me what sort he must be
> to whom I confide my secret ardour? — *Faithful.*
> Will faithful friends be found in Love
> who afterwards respect the rank of friend? — *Rarely.*
> How shall I act, then, so as to find him,
> one who is faithful, as is required? — *Seek.*
> And if I find him, how can he help me?
> Perhaps at times he relieves the passion? — *Relieves.*
> Now this that you dictate to me, tell me the true way
> of Love, tell me, I pray, the truth? — *Truth.*
> If this is the true way, I am happy;
> henceforth I do not fear that sorrow will cast me down? — *You err.*
>
> Why do I err? perhaps something else is needed still?
> Because without wings my thought does not fly? — *It needs.*
> Is something else needed still? is this not enough?
> Tell me the truth, do not leave me uncertain? — *Certainly.*
> What is needed, then, tell me, please,
> for the soul to be a partner of joy? — *Fortune.*
> Fortune? is something else needed so that in the end
> desire and hope may not remain in vain in my lot? [?2] — *Fortune.*
> In short, above all, what helps
> so that desire may not be in vain, and fortune? — *Fortune.*
> Now stay in peace, Nymph; I thank you,
> for with your discourse you seem to bring me back to life? — *Live.*

<aside>Lower parts of Pan</aside>

Now I return to Pan, whose lower parts are hairy and rough, with goat's feet, because they represent to us the earth, which is hard and rough and wholly uneven, covered with trees, with countless plants and with abundant grass.

<aside>Pan as the Sun</aside>

Some, wishing the Sun to be understood by this god, Father and Lord of all things (among them Macrobius), say that the horns in him show the shape of the new Moon; the ruddy face, the redness that is seen in the air at the rising and at the setting of the Sun, whose rays, which descend all the way down to the earth, are meant by the long beard; the spotted skin shows the stars that appear when the Sun departs; the staff, the power that he has over things; and the pipe, the harmony of the Heavens, which they hold to have been known from the movement of the Sun.

But whether this or something else was signified by the god Pan (for Plato holds that by him discourse is to be understood, and that he is two-formed, that is man and goat, because sometimes the truth is spoken and sometimes the false; and therefore the upper part shows the truth, which is accompanied by reason and, being light and divine, always tends upward, while the lower part shows the false, which is wholly bestial, hard and rough, and dwells nowhere else than down here among mortals), whatever this god may signify, as I said, he was painted by the ancients in this way: a man from the middle upward, with two horns on his head, with a grimacing face, all ruddy, and with the skin of a panther or a leopard girding his chest and shoulders; with one hand he holds a shepherd's staff and with the other a pipe of seven reeds; from the middle downward he is then a goat, with a goat's thighs, legs and feet.

<aside>Virgil</aside>

In the same way Faunus, Silvanus and the Satyrs were also depicted, who for that reason seem to be of one and the same nature: all have a certain small, short tail, and to all the ancients gave wreaths of lilies and reeds, and we read that sometimes they were crowned also with poplar and fennel. Hence Virgil in the last Eclogue shows Silvanus with his head roughly adorned with flowering ferulas and great lilies.

<aside>Silvanus</aside>

And in another place he gives him a tender plant of cypress to carry in his hand, because, as Servius explains there, Cyparissus, a most beautiful youth greatly loved by him, was changed into this tree. Silvanus was held by the ancients to be a god not only of the woods but of the fields too, and to have the care of their cultivation, to which they urged him with a certain ceremony when women were in childbed, so that, occupied with that, he should not go at night to trouble them. For he was believed to be that heavy, weighty thing which one who sleeps seems sometimes to feel coming upon him.

<aside>St Augustine</aside>
<aside>Intercidona</aside>
<aside>Pilumnus</aside>
<aside>Deverra</aside>

So that Silvanus should not go to molest women in childbed, the ancients (as Varro writes, and St Augustine reports in the *City of God*) used to send three young men around the house, who, on reaching the door, struck the ground there: one with an axe, calling on Intercidona, god of the cutting of trees; another with a pestle (because without this the iron could not be properly cleaned), and he called on the god Pilumnus, who had the care of pounding; and the third swept there, because by sweeping the grain is gathered together, and he called on Deverra, goddess of sweeping, so that Silvanus should go away with these three gods and not enter the house where the woman in childbed was.

<aside>Satyrs</aside>
<aside>Philostratus</aside>
<aside>Plutarch</aside>

Of the Satyrs Lucian writes that they have pointed ears, like those of goats, and are bald, with two little horns on the head; and Philostratus adds that they have a red face of human appearance and goat's feet. Hence they are exceedingly swift, as Pliny reports, and are found in the mountains of India; but because of their swiftness it is not possible to catch them except when old or sick, as Plutarch relates, one was brought to Sulla when he was returning from the war waged against Mithridates.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

Pausanias writes that it was reported to him by a man who had once been driven by the wind to certain deserted islands in the Ocean Sea, called the Satyrides, that wild men lived there, all reddish, with a tail little smaller than a horse's, who ran down to the shore as soon as they saw any ship, and, if there were women on board, hurled themselves upon them with the greatest fury in the world, using them in every way: which agrees very well with what we read of the nature of the Satyrs.

<aside>St Jerome</aside>
<aside>Satyr seen</aside>

And the blessed Jerome relates in the life of St Anthony that in the deserts of Egypt this holy man saw a little man who had horns on his forehead and a hooked nose, and from the chin down, in the thighs and feet, was like goats; and having made the sign of the Cross, he asked him who he was, and he replied that he was mortal, an inhabitant of the woods, and one of those to whom deceived paganism paid divine honours, calling them Fauns and Satyrs. And these never went to Heaven, but always stayed on earth together with the nymphs and other woodland gods, exactly as Jupiter says, who wishes them to stay there, when in Ovid he declares to the council of the gods that he wants to ruin the world with the flood. And they were called Demigods because, although they were believed able to help and to harm, and to know many of the things to come as well, they nevertheless died.

<aside>Herodotus</aside>
<aside>Mysteries kept hidden</aside>

But returning to Pan, Herodotus writes that he was one of the eight principal gods of Egypt; for, as I said before, the Egyptians believed that the first gods were twelve, but then said that there had been another eight before those, and of these Pan was one, as I have said. His idol was like the one by which the Greeks depicted him, not because they did not believe him similar to the other gods. But why they depicted him so, Herodotus adds, he would rather keep silent than say; from which one sees how careful they were then not to reveal the mysteries of their religion.

<aside>Goatherds highly esteemed</aside>
<aside>Goat revered</aside>

And he continues that those peoples held goats and billy goats in great veneration, and that goatherds were held in the greatest respect, but one in particular above all the others, at whose death the country went into the deepest mourning; and this was all out of the reverence they bore to the god Pan. But in Greece honour was paid to the goat for another reason, as Pausanias relates, saying that at the appearance of the celestial Goat, which is certain stars that, as Ovid says, begin to show themselves at the Kalends of May, it was usual for some great evil to fall almost always upon the vineyards; and so certain people of Corinth resolved to make a fine goat of metal and set it up in the square. They then paid it many honours, and at certain times they worshipped it almost entire, so that the one in Heaven should do no harm to the vineyards.

<aside>Eusebius</aside>
<aside>Satyrs companions of Bacchus</aside>

Eusebius, writing of the animals that were worshipped in Egypt, after speaking of the genital members that were likewise worshipped there, because human generation is preserved through them, adds that for this reason the Pans and the Satyrs were held in great reverence, as though they too contributed much to the increase of the human race, as appears from their idols set up in the temples in the form of a billy goat, with the member always erect, because they say that this animal is always ready for coition; and they were believed to be lustful beyond measure; hence they were given as companions to Bacchus, because wine heats the natural power and inflames a man to lust.

<aside>Lasciviousness</aside>
<aside>Philoxenus of Eretria</aside>
<aside>Silenus</aside>

So Philoxenus of Eretria, wishing once to depict Lasciviousness, as Pliny writes, depicted three Satyrs who, with vessels in their hands, drank deeply and seemed to invite one another to drink. Similar to this, it seems to me, is what Pausanias writes of Silenus, who likewise belongs to the number of the woodland gods, and it is that in his temple in Greece among the Eleans there was an idol to which Drunkenness offered a vessel of wine.

<aside>Pine given to Pan</aside>

Porphyry holds that the Greeks, imitating the Egyptians, did not worship beasts as they did, but composed the idols of the gods from beast and man, and that for this reason Jupiter sometimes had the horns of a ram, and Bacchus of a bull; and that Pan was made of man and of goat. To him the ancients gave the pine, putting it at times into his hand and at times making wreaths of it for him. The reason, the fables say, is that a girl called Pitys, greatly loved by him, was changed into this tree. As they say also of Syrinx, who became a reed, and he, who had loved her before, made himself the pipe from it afterwards and out of love for her always carried it.

<aside>Porphyry</aside>
<aside>Suda</aside>

Now I return to Jupiter, reputed, as I said, the greatest of all the gods among the ancients, and for that reason having the government of the universe. According to the way in which Porphyry, Eusebius, Suda and others besides have described it, his image was placed seated, to show that that power which rules the world and preserves it is stable and firm and never changes. The upper parts were nude and open, to give us to understand that God manifests himself to the divine intelligences; and those below were covered and clothed, because we cannot see him while we inhabit this lower World.

He held a sceptre in his left hand, because they say that on this side of the body is the principal member, that is the heart, from which come the spirits that then spread through the whole body. And so the World has life from God, who as King dispenses it and governs it in his own way. With the right hand he held out now an eagle and now a small image of Victory, showing in that way that Jupiter is as much superior to all the people of Heaven as the eagle is to all the birds, and that he has all things so subject to him as if he had acquired them by right of victory and governed them in his own way. Hence it comes about that for the most part men cannot understand the cause of the changes in these things, nor of the good and the evil that alternate so often among mortals.

<aside>Homer</aside>

For this reason Homer imagined that Jupiter always has before him two great vessels like casks, one full of good, the other of evil, which he turns and turns again at his pleasure, and then draws now from one, now from the other what seems to him the World deserves to be sent to it. And another very ancient poet said that Jupiter makes the scale descend now on one side, now on the other, according to whom, among these or those, it pleases him to do good; which was also a fiction of Homer, for in Homer Jupiter, holding the golden scales in his hand, weighs the deeds of the Greeks and the Trojans to see to which he should give victory.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

There was also in Piraeus, the harbour of the Athenians, as Pausanias writes, an idol consecrated to Jupiter, which held a sceptre and a Victory in its hand. And the Egyptians had their sacred things, all full of marvellous mysteries, and kept them secret as far as they could, by means of certain ceremonies and various statues. They likewise put a sceptre in the hand of that god whom they called the Creator, and who therefore, it seems to me, agrees much with the Jupiter of the Greeks. So it is no wonder that I put their idols together; for although they had different names and were not made in one and the same way, nevertheless I believe one may say that they signified one and the same thing, or things differing little from one another.

The Creator of the Egyptians was, then, represented in the form of a man, of an azure colour, who held a circle in one hand and in the other a royal rod, and on top of his head had a feather, which showed that the Creator of things, who is King, as the sceptre shows, can hardly be found, because it lies in his hand to give life to the universe, which he does while, understanding in himself, he revolves, and this the circle signifies that he holds in his hand.

From his mouth he sends out an egg, from which is born that god whom they call Vulcan. The egg signifies the World, and Vulcan that natural heat which in it gives life to things. Although in Egypt they showed the world with another idol as well, which was of a man with feet twisted together and tied; he had about him a garment that covered him down to the feet, all varied and of different colours; and he bore on his head a great golden ball. These things signified that the World is round and never changes place, and that the nature of the stars is changeable.

> Image of the Creator God of the Egyptians and of Jupiter, god of the gods of the ancient heathens, which denote the nature of God and his providence, goodness, communication and governance of all things.

<aside>Universe depicted</aside>
<aside>Serpents held to be of divine nature</aside>

All this is said by Porphyry, as Eusebius reports, who writes also that the Universe was painted by the Egyptians in this way. They drew two circles one above the other, and crossed them with a serpent that had the head of a hawk. The circles showed the size and shape of the World, and the serpent the good Daemon, preserver of everything, who comprehends the universe by his power, that is, that spirit which gives it life and nourishes it; for the Phoenicians and the Egyptians held that serpents are of divine nature, seeing that these, not with the help of external limbs, as other animals do, but moved only by their spirit and liveliness, go very swiftly and with marvellous quickness twist and retwist the body in various ways; besides which they live a very long time, because they lay aside old age together with the skin which they change, and so, made young again, they seem unable ever to die of themselves, unless perhaps they are killed. And they added the head of the hawk as well, for its quickness and great agility.

<aside>Martianus Capella</aside>
<aside>Image of Jupiter</aside>

Martianus Capella, when in the wedding of Mercury and Philologia he imagines that Jupiter calls all the other gods to a council, describes him thus. On his head he has a royal crown, all resplendent and flaming; a shining veil, once woven by the hand of Pallas, covers the nape of his neck; he is dressed all in white, except that above he has a mantle, which seems of glass, painted with sparkling stars; in his right hand he holds two round balls, one of gold, the other of gold and silver; and in the left a lyre with nine strings; his shoes are of green emerald, and he sits upon a cloth made and woven of peacock feathers, and with his feet he treads on a trident.

> Image of Jupiter, god of the gods of the ancients, signifying the power and providence of God, and that he is the maker and preserver of all things, and that from him comes the harmony of the celestial spheres.

Statues of Jupiter were also made in such a way that they not only signified who he was and what he could do, but also gave to understand what men ought to do among themselves, and chiefly Kings and Princes toward their subjects: for these (as I remember having said at another time) are on earth as it were the image of God, and therefore ought, as far as they are able, to represent likewise the providence, justice and goodness of God.

<aside>Plutarch</aside>
<aside>Jupiter without ears</aside>
<aside>Jupiter with four ears</aside>

Plutarch writes, then, that in Crete there was once an idol of Jupiter which had no ears, to show that he who is over others and has to govern them ought not to listen to what is said to him, neither this more than that, nor that more than this, but to stand so firm and steady that he never departs from the straight path because of other people's words. And on the contrary the Lacedaemonians depicted him with four ears, as though Jupiter heard everything and understood everything, or else because he must have two ears to judge, in order to hear one party, and two for the other: which likewise refers to the prudence of the King and the Prince, who has to hear and understand everyone, and all that his peoples do.

<aside>Jupiter with three eyes</aside>

And perhaps the same thing was meant to be shown by the one who once depicted Jupiter with three eyes, as though he saw everything and nothing were hidden from him: as nothing ought to be hidden either from him who has the care and government of cities. Hence it came about that the ancients said that justice sees everything, as appears in her image.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>
<aside>Aeschylus</aside>

But Pausanias gives another reason for it, writing that among the Argives, in the temple of Minerva, there was an idol of Jupiter which had two eyes, as one sees men have, and another in the middle of its forehead; and he says one may think that this signified that Jupiter has three kingdoms to watch over. One is that of Heaven, because everyone commonly reputed him King of Heaven; another is that of Hell, that is of the Earth, because the Earth, in respect of Heaven, is Hell, and Homer for this reason calls him infernal Jupiter; the third is that of the sea, because Aeschylus calls him King of the Sea; and Martianus Capella (as I have said above) puts the trident under his feet; and Orpheus, in a certain hymn, prays Justice to take care of all the living, who are nourished by mother Earth and by marine Jupiter.

The three eyes in Jupiter show, then, according to Pausanias, that those three kingdoms of the universe are subject to him, which the fables say he divided with his other two brothers, the kingdom of the Sea falling to Neptune and that of Hell to Pluto. That in the statues of the gods the ancients showed by the eye what the office of the ruler was is seen, Plutarch again says, from what the Egyptians did, who among their sacred paintings, when they wished to represent the King, drew a sceptre with an eye at the top, as I have already said that they painted the Sun too, and they depicted Jupiter likewise with the same figure, wishing by this to be understood that, as the King can do much, because the sceptre is a sign of superiority and of the power one has over others, so he must be vigilant in the government of his peoples, always showing himself just in all his affairs.

<aside>Suda</aside>
<aside>Aristotle</aside>

We read also that beside the statue of Jupiter the ancients used to place that of Justice, as though the King never did, or never ought to do, anything that was not accompanied by Justice. Hence the ancients also used, as Suda reports, to put a stork at the top of sceptres and a hippopotamus at the butt, wishing in this way to show that the King must be pious and just, and must crush those who with violence and unjustly do harm to others. For we read, and Aristotle confirms it, that the stork feeds its father and mother after they have grown old, in the same way that it was once fed and reared by them, a most pious and most just act; and the hippopotamus is so impious and unjust, as Plutarch writes, that it does violence to its father and kills him, and afterwards consorts with its mother.

<aside>Plutarch</aside>

Besides this we read in the same Plutarch that in Thebes there were some statues without hands, which represented the judges and the administrators of justice, because these must be without hands, that is, they must in no way accept reward or gifts, on account of which they would then have to wrong someone, ruling in favour of one who has no right. And among these there was another without eyes, which represented the ruler who is over the judges, because he must be free from every passion, both hatred and love, considering only in himself what is just, without having regard more to this man than to that in having justice administered, as Kings and Princes are bound to do, as well as officials and magistrates, not only by the law of nature but by their own oath as well.

And by doing otherwise, both the one and the other must expect to be punished by Jupiter, the chastiser of perjury, as the ancients showed in his statues too: for we read that among the Eleans, a people of Greece, there was one which was very frightening and greatly feared by treacherous men and perjurers. It held the thunderbolt in both hands, as though it stood ready to punish the perjurer.

<aside>Water of Jupiter of perjury</aside>

Aristotle also tells of a certain water, writing of the marvellous things of the world, that it was in Cappadocia near Tyana, the chief city of that country, and that at its spring it was very cold but there seemed to boil. If someone was brought to it about whom there was doubt whether he had sworn falsely, then, if he had spoken the truth, it showed itself calm and went on with a slow and pleasant course; but if the man had sworn a lie, it showed itself so angry against him that, swelling up, it flung itself at his feet, his hands and even his face, as though it wished to punish him for his perjury, and it never left him until he had openly confessed his sin and, weeping, begged pardon for it, or (if he stayed obstinate) became dropsical there and vomited from his mouth a great quantity of blood, all corrupt and spoiled; and so the Greeks called this the water of Jupiter of perjury.

> Image of Fidelity signified by the god Dius Fidius, worshipped in antiquity. Truth is represented here as Mother, Honour as Father, Love as the bond.

And among the Corinthians, Pausanias writes, there was in the temple of Neptune a secret chamber with an entrance that ran underground, where they said Portunus dwelt, and whoever had sworn falsely there, whoever he might be, could not escape being punished at once. And the Eleans likewise went to swear at the altar of Sosipolis, their god, with great reverence.

<aside>Ceremony of swearing</aside>

Pausanias does not relate the ceremony they used there; but he does tell, in another place, the one they performed at the much-celebrated Olympic games, where people gathered from every side, some to run on foot, some to race horses, some to wrestle, and some for other things; for whoever won there was highly esteemed, so that great care had to be taken that no deceit should be practised there. And for this reason not only those who went to take part in any of those games, but their fathers too, their brothers and the trainers who had exercised them, all of whom went to accompany them, swore with certain solemn words over the testicles of a pig, which for this purpose were solemnly cut off there at that moment, that they would commit no fraud.

And the competitors swore besides that they had trained for ten continuous months in that kind of contest for which they had come. And those who were to judge the victory swore likewise that they would take no gift [?3] from the competitors or from their people, that they would not favour one more than another in any way, and that they would not reveal why they approved or rejected this one more than that one.

And because this was almost in the form of a sacrifice, and in sacrifices it was the custom to eat the flesh of the sacrificed animals, Pausanias added that he does not know what was done with this pig, on whose testicles the solemn oath had been made, but that he knows well that the ancient religion forbade eating the flesh of the victim over which an oath had been solemnly sworn, as is seen in Homer, when he says that the priest threw into the sea the pig on whose testicles Agamemnon swore that he had not touched Briseis. And almost similar was the ceremony the Romans used in making truces, for they swore and made certain imprecations over a pig which they had there, in the presence of the priests appointed for the purpose.

<aside>Jupiter Horcius</aside>
<aside>Dius Fidius</aside>

But leaving the ceremonies, let us return to the god who guards the oath, called by the Greeks Jupiter Horcius and represented in a statue that held the thunderbolt in both hands. He was depicted by the Romans in another way and also named otherwise, although the numen was the same, as some have said of Jupiter Horcius and of the Roman god Dius Fidius, because just as the former watched over the oath, that it should be true and just, so the latter presided over the keeping of faith, and for this reason he was worshipped, and he is found among the antiquities of Rome made in this way.

> Image of Jupiter hurling thunderbolts against perjurers, called Jupiter Horcius, guardian of the oath and severe chastiser and destroyer of those who swear falsely and of those who were ready to swear.

> Image of the god Dius Fidius, guardian of faith and fidelity; of Jupiter called Vejovis, that is harmful and punishing, in the dress of Bacchus, to whom his numen is attributed; and of the Goat Amalthea, who gave milk to Jupiter, from one of whose horns the horn of plenty, or cornucopia, was made.

It is a piece of marble carved in the manner of a window, on which are sculpted three figures from the middle upward, of which the one on the right side is a man in peaceful dress, and beside him are letters saying HONOR; the other, on the left side, is a woman in the same dress, with a laurel wreath on her head, and with letters saying VERITAS. These two figures give each other the right hand, and between them is the third, a boy with a beautiful and honest face, over whose head are carved those two words DIVS FIDIVS.

<aside>Vejovis</aside>
<aside>Gellius</aside>
<aside>Horn of plenty</aside>

And as for Jupiter punishing the perjurer, as I have said, it comes to my mind that he was not always worshipped in order that he should help (*giouasse*), but also sometimes in order that he should not harm; and then they called him Vejovis, as though he could only harm. They showed this too in his statue, for they depicted it, as we read in Gellius and as Alexander ab Alexandro reports, in the form of a boy with horns on his head and arrows in his hand, as if about to strike, and beside him was a goat.

For the fables said that his mother, to save him from the voracious throat of Saturn, had given him into the keeping of two nymphs in Crete, one named Amalthea and the other Melissa, or else Aega and Helice, and these nursed him with honey and the milk of a goat of theirs which they loved dearly. It happened one day that this goat unluckily broke a horn against a tree, to the very great displeasure of the nymphs, who were beyond measure grieved; and, being unable to do anything else, they filled it with various flowers and fruits and, having adorned it all with beautiful leaves, presented it to Jupiter, who held it very dear and wished that, in honour of his nurse, it should always be a sign of abundance; so we call it the horn of plenty, and sometimes also the horn of Amalthea, of which Pherecydes said, as Apollodorus reports, that its virtue is such that it gives abundantly all that a man can desire for food and drink.

We read also that this horn was not the goat's but an ox's, and that ox into which Achelous changed himself when he once fought with Hercules for Deianira, who had been promised by her father to both; for Hercules, as the fables say, broke it off and threw it away; but the Naiads, nymphs of the rivers, picked it up and, having filled it with various flowers and fruits and adorned it with green leaves, consecrated it to Copia, which is understood as the goddess of abundance, and for this reason it was called the Horn of Copia and of Plenty.

<aside>The cornucopia and its interpretation</aside>

This (leaving aside the histories that lie beneath this fable) some say shows the power of fortune, because many animals have all their strength in their horns and often wound with them; and fortune has abundance as her minister, because she is exceedingly rich, and it lies as it were in her hand to give and to take away riches and temporal goods.

> Image of Jupiter surrounded by the three Graces and the three Horae, whose throne is upheld by images of Victory, with a crowned Victory in one hand and the sceptre with the eagle in the other, signifying the absolute dominion of God and that all things are subject to him.

The abundance of flowers and fruits therefore lies in the horn of plenty (whether it was a goat's or an ox's), because riches and the other worldly goods seem to be in the power of fortune, and to go and come as it pleases her. It might also be said that the horn of plenty came from the goat that gave milk to Jupiter, because all goods were believed to come from him, as I have already said. Hence he was given the same power that the Sun has, and for this reason they wished him to have arrows in his hand in the statue that I drew a little while ago.

<aside>Jupiter with the ornaments of Bacchus</aside>
<aside>Eagle of Jupiter</aside>

And some gave him likewise the numen of Bacchus, making an idol of him with the ornaments of Bacchus, as Pausanias relates that Polykleitos made one in Arcadia, which had buskins on its feet and with one hand held a drinking vessel and with the other a thyrsus, at the top of which was an eagle. And this one too had to be young, as Bacchus is depicted; as was also the Jupiter worshipped at Terracina, to whom they gave a surname that means without a razor, because he was beardless and had no need of such a knife. Few are the statues of Jupiter to which an eagle is not added in some way, as his own bird.

<aside>Eagle queen of birds</aside>

And for this reason the chariot of Jupiter is always drawn by eagles, either because, as Lactantius reports, he took a good omen of victory from the eagle that once appeared to him while he was going to a certain war (and some say it was against Saturn), from which he returned victorious; and so it was afterwards feigned that in the war against the Giants the eagle supplied Jupiter with arms, and for this reason they often paint it with him carrying the thunderbolt in its talons; or else because we read that of all birds the eagle alone is safe from the bolt of Heaven, and that it alone likewise fixes its eyes on the Sun; so that with reason it is called the queen of birds and given to Jupiter, likewise King of the gods.

<aside>Jupiter on the throne</aside>

We find Jupiter also (as Phidias made him for the Eleans, and Pausanias describes him) of gold and ivory, seated on a fine royal throne, with a crown on his head made of olive leaves. In his right hand he has a Victory, likewise crowned, and in the left a sceptre made of various metals, which has an eagle at the top; the mantle he wears is of gold, worked with various animals and with flowers of every sort, but for the most part lilies, and the shoes likewise are gilded.

<aside>Jupiter Custos</aside>
<aside>Lucian</aside>

On the throne, which is all gleaming with gold and precious stones, made of ivory and ebony, many animals are carved, besides the three Graces, who are on one side above the head of the idol, and three Horae on the other, and four images of Victory support it in place of feet. Jupiter is likewise seated on a high throne on an ancient medal of Nero, and has the thunderbolt in his right hand and a spear in his left, with letters saying Jupiter Custos. And Lucian, writing of the Syrian Goddess, says that in the temple of this goddess there was an idol of Jupiter placed seated on two bulls.

> Image of Jupiter with the Thunderbolt and with the Spear, to show the stability of the government of divine Providence and at the same time its prompt punishment of the evil deeds of the wicked.

<aside>Jupiter Stator</aside>

But on the other hand, on some medals likewise ancient, of Antoninus Pius and of Gordian, Jupiter stands nude, on his feet, and has the spear in his right hand and the thunderbolt in his left, with letters saying Jupiter Stator. He was so called in a certain temple built for him by Romulus, because at his prayers he stopped the Roman soldiers and, making them turn about, made them stand firm, when once, fighting against the Sabines, they had taken to flight.

<aside>Jupiter Conservator</aside>

Not very unlike this is Jupiter Conservator, who is seen on the ancient medals of Diocletian, who likewise stands upright and has in his right hand two arrows, so that they can be taken also for two thunderbolts, and a spear in his left. And on another medal of the same Diocletian he is called Jupiter Conservator of the universe, and he holds the spear with the left hand and with the right offers a small image of Victory.

<aside>Thunderbolt of Summanus</aside>
<aside>Thunderbolt given to several gods</aside>
<aside>Minerva hurls the thunderbolt</aside>

No other emblem seems to be so proper to Jupiter as the thunderbolt, although the Romans gave it, as Pliny writes, to the god Summanus as well, who was the same as Pluto, but only the one that came by night, because the thunderbolt of the day was Jupiter's. The Etruscans, most ancient observers of these things, maintained that Vulcan and Minerva likewise hurl the thunderbolt, with which, we read, she once burned the fleet of the Greeks.

<aside>Virgil</aside>

Hence in Virgil Juno, indignant within herself at being unable to do the harm that she wished to Aeneas and the other Trojans when, after the ruin of Troy, they were going to Italy, says thus:

> Pallas was able to take vengeance
> on the Greeks and burn their ships,
> hurling upon them with her own hand
> from the lofty clouds the thunderbolt of Jupiter:
> And I, &c.
>
> *(Virgil)*

<aside>Thunderbolts of three colours</aside>
<aside>Acron</aside>

And they said that the thunderbolts hurled by the other gods (for so we shall interpret, for now, what they called Manubiae) were white or black; but red was the one that came from the hand of Jupiter, as Acron reports, where Horace says that the supreme father with his burning right hand has touched the sacred towers.

<aside>Thunderbolt of three kinds</aside>
<aside>Marvels of the thunderbolt</aside>
<aside>Marcia</aside>

From this come the three kinds of thunderbolt set down by Aristotle, of which one is so clear and penetrating that it works the miracles that are read of as too great, for instance that, passing through, it drinks all the wine of a cask without leaving any sign of having touched the cask; that it melts silver and any other metal it finds in chests without at all harming the chests; that in Marcia, a Roman woman, it destroyed the child she still bore in her womb and did her no harm; that it kills people, and it is not seen to have touched the clothes they have on, and other similar things: and this kind of thunderbolt comes from Minerva, who was born from the head of Jupiter, and is therefore the most purified and subtlest part of fire, and it will be the white one. The other burns whatever it finds, and let this be the red, sent by the hand of Jupiter. The third, which has more of the moist and the coarse, does not burn but only stains, and for that reason they called it black and gave it to Vulcan, minister of this our fire, all smoky.

> Image of Jupiter hurling thunderbolts and bringing rains and storm clouds, and image of Zeus Labrandeus of the Lydians, signifying that God is prepared to punish evildoers, and divine providence.

For these reasons the poets called the thunderbolt three-forked, as one that strikes in three ways, and it is likewise painted with three points, and there were three Cyclopes who forged it, as is told in the image of Vulcan; to whom, however, I do not find that the thunderbolt was ever given, either in statue or in painting, and still less to Minerva; although what I have written of it is read, to show its nature and its various effects; but to Jupiter alone they have given it, sometimes in his hand and sometimes at his feet; now the eagle brings it to him close by with its beak, now with its claws, and in various other ways it has been carved and painted.

<aside>Seneca</aside>

Seneca says that giving Jupiter the thunderbolt, with which he often frightens the World, was a fiction of the ancients to curb the rashness of arrogant ignorant men, who would have given themselves licentiously to every sort of wickedness if they had feared no one who exceeded all human strength. To frighten, therefore, those who could do good only out of fear, it was said that Jupiter, supreme judge of human actions, stood over them with his right hand armed with the thunderbolt.

Nor did he always hurl it of his own will alone: but, as I have said already, often with the counsel of the other gods; and it was then most grave, and a bringer of many ills, just as it was light, and showed that the anger of Jupiter could easily be appeased, when the heavenly counsel did not intervene.

<aside>Moral lesson</aside>

From this Seneca draws a very fine moral lesson, saying that, just as Jupiter, supreme King of the gods, benefits mortals and sends them good without asking the counsel of the other gods, so among us Kings and other lords ought, before doing harm to others, whether as punishment or for any other reason whatever, to think it over carefully and take good counsel, remembering that Jupiter does not trust his own judgment alone when he has to send some grave evil to the world, and that it was said that of the thunderbolts sent by Jupiter some were heavy and destructive and some light and of little harm for no other reason than to give to understand to him whose business it is to punish human errors that he should not strike all in the same way, nor show himself equally terrible to everyone.

<aside>Aegis carried by Jupiter</aside>
<aside>Virgil</aside>

We read also that Jupiter carried on his left arm the skin of the goat that nursed him when he was still a baby, called the aegis, and that by shaking it he made the rains, just as with his right he hurled the thunderbolt, as Servius notes on Virgil, where he says that the Arcadians believed they had seen, from the very beginning, Jupiter himself around the Tarpeian Rock:

> When he often shakes the black aegis,
> and with his right hand stirs the dark storm clouds.
>
> *(Virgil)*

<aside>Diphthera, book of Jupiter</aside>

And that on the same skin, also called Diphthera, he wrote everything that was done throughout the universe, so as not to forget anything, when he wished to review the account of human actions. Hence the ancients used to say as a proverb that Jupiter had indeed looked once into the Diphthera, when they saw some wicked man, after having been happy for a time, punished at last and chastised for his wicked deeds.

<aside>Plutarch</aside>

Moreover, Jupiter was depicted without a thunderbolt too, as we read that there was an idol of him in Caria, a region of Asia Minor, which had neither thunderbolt nor sceptre nor anything else of what has been described so far, but only an axe, and the reason for it is given. Plutarch relates that Hercules, having killed Hippolyta, Queen of the Amazons, took the axe which she carried among her other arms, and gave it to his Omphale, who was a Lydian, and therefore the Kings of Lydia afterwards used to carry it, and guarded it as a sacred thing.

<aside>Candaules</aside>
<aside>Zeus Labrandeus</aside>

It passed through the hands of many Kings to Candaules, who then did not deign to carry it but had it carried by one who was always with him, who together with Candaules was killed by Gyges, the winner of the war that he had previously begun against him, and among the other spoils which he brought back to Caria was the axe too, which he then put in the hand of an idol of Jupiter made there for this purpose, which was called Labrandeus, because those of Lydia call the axe *labra*.

<aside>Inventors of the equipment of war</aside>

But Lactantius holds that he was so called from one named Labrandeus who brought help to Jupiter and aided him in a very great war. To this idol, Aelian says, a knife was also hung, called Carian, and it was greatly revered, because they say that the Carians were the first to make those things which serve for war: to fight for reward, to fit shields so that they could be carried on the arm, and to put crests on helmets.

<aside>Jupiter giving birth</aside>

And because painters often show the fables, painting them as well as the poets have invented them in writing, a pupil of Apelles, having once heard, or perhaps read, that Jupiter gave birth to Bacchus, painted him, as Pliny writes, with certain ornaments which the women of Lydia wore on their heads, in the midst of some women who were helping him to give birth, and he, like a woman who feels great pain in labour, seemed to be lamenting, and there were many goddesses there, who were making the greatest hubbub in the world.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

I do not tell of Bacchus, how Jupiter carried him for a time attached to his side, until the hour of mature birth came, because these fables from Ovid's *Metamorphoses* are by now so common that everyone knows them. The ancient sculptors likewise have often taken from the poets the example for the statues they have made. Hence Pausanias writes that certain Leontines, a people of Greece, at their own private expense made a Jupiter seven cubits high, which had an eagle in its left hand, and carried a dart with its right, because they had already seen him so described by some poets.

<aside>Strabo</aside>
<aside>Homer</aside>

Strabo tells of the temple of Olympian Jupiter, which on account of the oracle that was there was once celebrated for a time in such a way that from every part of Greece people flocked there to bring many rich gifts, as Cypselus, tyrant of Corinth, did, who offered there an idol of Jupiter all of solid gold; and he says that in it there was a statue of Jupiter too, made of ivory by Phidias the Athenian, so great that although the temple was very large, it was nevertheless small for the greatness of the statue. For this reason the artist seemed to have ill observed the proportion of the place, because he made it so that, seated, it touched the high roof with its head, so that if it had stood up it would have had to be broken through, since it was much taller than the temple. But it was not for this less praised than its beauty deserved, for Quintilian writes that this seemed to add something, I know not what, to religion and to the reverence that was borne to Jupiter, so well did it represent the divine majesty, of which Phidias (as he said to Pandaenus his nephew, who asked him) took the example from Homer, where he says thus:

> With a grave and reverend nod
> the son of Saturn showed his will.
> Moving his head, which, sprinkled with ambrosia,
> made the universe move together.
>
> *(Homer)*

The painters too have sometimes invented something of their own, as Apelles did when he was accused of the conspiracy, as can be seen in the image of Calumny. And Pliny writes that Nealces, a painter of great genius, had painted a naval war between the Egyptians and the Persians, and, not being able by the painting of the places alone to show that it had been fought on the Nile, as he wished it to be understood, he thought of showing this in the following way. He painted an ass drinking on the bank, and a crocodile lying in wait to do it harm, because the crocodile is an animal proper to Egypt, and in Persia there is a great abundance of asses.

<aside>Sicyonians</aside>

For which reasons I wish to say that it was perhaps an invention of the painters too, or of the sculptors, to make the images of the gods without any form of man or of other animal, as it is read of Venus that she had one in Paphos; the Sun likewise was so represented among the Phoenicians; and the Sicyonians, a people of the Morea, had a Jupiter made in the shape of a pyramid, as Pausanias writes.

> Image of the oracle of Jupiter Ammon of the Troglodytes, signifying the obscurity and baseness of worldly things, and that one must look, and raise oneself with the keenness of the intellect, to the height of the divine.

This, I believe, is meant to signify the same thing that the statue of the same Jupiter (of which I have already spoken) also signifies, nude from the middle upward and clothed in the rest. For the base of these images represents to us the gloom of the darkness through which we walk in this world, so that, keeping our mind applied to human things, we cannot have any knowledge of the divine, into which we must look with the keenness of the mind, shown by the sharp top of the pyramid. And our mind can do this when it cuts away all the effects of the body and grows subtle so as to penetrate the Heavens; or when it lays down the corporeal mass and, all unburdened and light, flies back to enjoy the blessed vision of eternal things.

<aside>Quintus Curtius</aside>
<aside>Jupiter Ammon</aside>
<aside>Fountain of the Sun</aside>
<aside>Image in the form of a navel</aside>

And therefore, whether this or something else was the cause, Quintus Curtius writes that among the Troglodytes in Egypt, where there was a grove consecrated to the god Ammon, who was Jupiter, in the middle of which rose a spring called the water of the Sun (which, as Pomponius Mela also reports, at the beginning of the day was lukewarm, at midday cold; towards evening it grew a little warm, and at midnight was so hot that it boiled, and towards day it gradually grew lukewarm), a certain thing was worshipped which was not such as the idols of the gods are usually made, but in the form of a navel composed of emeralds and other gems, wide below and round, tapering towards the top. When they wished to learn something from it, the priests carried it, wrapped up, on a little gilded boat, to which many silver cups were attached all around, and women and girls went within it, singing certain unmetrical verses, by which they thought to make Jupiter give them afterwards certain answers about what they wished to know.

<aside>Jupiter in the form of a Ram</aside>

Under the image of a ram this Jupiter Ammon was also worshipped, and some say the cause of it was this: when Bacchus was once walking through the deserts of Libya, he was on the point of perishing of thirst with all his army, if, after pious prayers to the Father, a ram had not come, which, always going before him, led him where he found enough to water the whole army; and believing that Jupiter had come in that animal to show him the desired waters, he set up an altar there and made his idol in the form of a ram.

<aside>Herodotus</aside>

Ovid, following the fables, holds that this was because, when the gods of Heaven fled from the fury of the Giants into Egypt, Jupiter, for his greater safety, changed himself there into a ram. And Herodotus, giving the reason why the Thebans in Egypt were forbidden to sacrifice sheep, writes that Jupiter, not wishing to be seen by Hercules, who greatly desired it and begged him for it every day, and being no longer able to resist such affectionate entreaties, showed himself to him dressed in a ram's skin; and that from this the Egyptians then took the idol of Jupiter in the form of a ram.

> Image of Jupiter Ammon of the Arcadians and of the Egyptians, and of the oak and the ram sacred to him, signifying that God is the author of life and the sustainer of the living, and the doubtful answers of the said oracle of Ammon.

<aside>Ram revered</aside>

This animal is greatly revered among them, and they never kill it for sacrifice, except on the day of the festival of Jupiter, on which every year they cut off the head of a ram and flay it, clothing the idol of Jupiter in that skin, to which they then bring that of Hercules, so that he may see it; and finally all who are there go to beat the flayed ram and, having put it in a sacred urn, bury it with the greatest reverence. This Jupiter Ammon was not only in Egypt, but in Greece too, and among the Arcadians (as Pausanias recounts) he was made in a square form in the manner of the Herms, statues of Mercury, and had on his head the horns of a ram, and some also gave him the whole head of a ram, and this because his answers were as doubtful as is the head of a ram wrapped in that skin of its own.

<aside>Alexander ab Alexandro</aside>

Besides this we find, as Alexander ab Alexandro reports, that the Celts, a people of Gaul, set up as the image and statue of Jupiter a very tall oak, and worshipped it as Jupiter, perhaps because they knew that among trees the oak was consecrated to Jupiter, as the one from whose fruit men lived in the earliest times, and that it was for him to feed and nourish those whom he was believed to have produced into the world and over whom he had the universal government. For this reason the ancients crowned with oak almost all the statues of Jupiter, as though the oak were a sign of life, which was believed to be given by him to mortals. Hence the Romans used to give a crown of oak to one who in war had defended a Roman citizen from death, wishing to give him, who had been the cause of another's living, the badge of life.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

But of olive too they sometimes made wreaths for Jupiter, because it is always green and very useful to mortals, and its leaves seem almost the colour of Heaven, although it is held rather to be the tree of Pallas, or Minerva, who is the same, as can be seen in her image. And Pausanias writes that in a certain part of Greece there was an idol of Jupiter which held a bird in one hand and in the other the thunderbolt, and had on its head a fine wreath of various spring flowers.

<aside>Martianus Capella</aside>
<aside>Servius</aside>

Jupiter also often had the crown of a King, according to the way Martianus Capella describes him above. For, as Pallas depicted him when contending with Arachne in Ovid, the image of Jupiter is regal, since he was believed to be King of the gods, of men and of the universe. And Servius, on the tenth Eclogue of Virgil, says that the proper insignia of Jupiter, which those who celebrated a triumph used to wear, were the sceptre and the toga palmata, which was a large, ample garment of purple, in which some have said that the palm was woven on the inside, and others that it was painted with large golden bosses. The sceptre was of ivory with an eagle on top, and this is taken from Juvenal in Satire X and from Prudentius in the Hymn of St Romanus the Martyr. The portrait is seen on the ancient medals of the Emperor Probus and on some consular ones, as they call them.

<aside>Pliny</aside>

That the face was painted red on him was because, as Pliny also writes, the Romans on every festival used to tint the face of Jupiter with red lead, and it was one of the principal things the censors did, to give Jupiter out to be coloured with red lead. And those who celebrated a triumph likewise made themselves all red with red lead; from this women took the custom, which has since come down to our times, of making themselves coloured and red, thinking they become more beautiful, whereas many often make themselves frightening to look at. And in Ethiopia likewise the great men used to paint not only the face but the whole body with red lead, and gave the same colour to all the idols of their gods.

<aside>Victims of Jupiter</aside>
<aside>Mad ceremony</aside>
<aside>Axe summoned to judgment</aside>

There were then victims of Jupiter, sacrificed to him by the Romans for various reasons, at various times, and under various surnames: the goat, the two-year-old ewe lamb, and a white bull with gilded horns; although they also sometimes sacrificed without a victim, with spelt, salt and incense. Among the Athenians an ox was sacrificed to him, with a very ridiculous ceremony, and it was as Pausanias recounts. They put a little spelt and wheat mixed together on the altar of Jupiter, and the ox destined for the sacrifice, approaching it, went to eat it; then one of the priests, called by the Greeks, from the office he held, Bouphonos, which in our language means striker of the ox, having dealt that beast a blow on the head with an axe, at once ran away, leaving the axe there, which was then summoned to judgment by those who were round about, as though they had not seen anyone else wound the ox but the axe.

<aside>Suda</aside>

This custom, as Suda writes, came from what once happened at a certain festival of Jupiter, at which an ox ate the flat cakes that were ready for the sacrifice; at which one who was present there, annoyed, thinking that beast had been too presumptuous, seized an axe and killed it, and ran away. The axe that remained was summoned to judgment, and the judges, having heard the arguments of the parties, acquitted it; and it was afterwards observed to do the same every year. And it is no great wonder that an axe was summoned to judgment among the Athenians, because among the first laws that were given them by Draco was that even inanimate things, as Pausanias and Suda report, when the person who had done the harm was not found, should be condemned in court, banished, and thrown outside the city according to their deserts.

Hence in the same authors one and the same tale is read, although the names differ, because Pausanias writes of Theagenes, and Suda of Nicon. This man (whatever name he had) was so valiant that from the victories won in various places he had brought back more than four hundred crowns, and a fine statue was therefore erected to him, to which, after he was dead, one who had always been envious of his honours used to go at night and beat it well with a whip; and he was as pleased with it as if he had offended Theagenes or Nicon still alive.

It happened that the statue fell suddenly upon the one who was beating it, and killed him, whereupon his sons summoned it to judgment, and said so much against it that they had it condemned as guilty of their father's death, and it was therefore thrown into the sea. For this reason, soon afterwards, a great sterility came, which ruined the whole country; which was remedied by the advice of the oracle by restoring to its place the statue thrown into the sea and afterwards found by some fishermen; and divine honours were then also given to it, and it was worshipped as a saving numen.

<aside>Various transformations of Jupiter</aside>

The many fables too that are read of Jupiter give occasion to represent him in many ways; for they relate that he often changed himself into various forms to enjoy his loves: as when he changed into a white bull to carry off Europa, into an eagle to ravish Ganymede, and also to have Asteria; into a golden rain to reach Danaë; into a swan to be with Leda; into fire to deceive Aegina; into Amphitryon to lie with Alcmene; into Diana to enjoy Callisto, and into many other shapes, both bestial and human; of which I shall say nothing more, because I do not find that the ancients ever took an example from these to make any image of Jupiter.
