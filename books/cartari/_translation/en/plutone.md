# Pluto

Source: books/cartari/_text/TEXT_reading.md, scans 0272–0302
Rules: books/cartari/_translation/en/RULES.md

---

Although in the division of the universe that the sons of Saturn made among themselves, the kingdom of Heaven fell to one, that of the Waters to another, and that of the underworld to the third, according to the fables (which is to say, as the histories tell it, that Jupiter had the regions of the East, Pluto those of the West, and Neptune the islands of the sea), nevertheless it seems that each of them has business everywhere. So in Virgil Neptune threatens the Winds because, without understanding his will, they have dared to disturb the sky and the earth; and Jupiter often puts the affairs of the underworld in order, and Pluto likewise raises his power as far as the sky; hence it is said that Jupiter has the thunderbolt with three points, and Neptune the trident.

This god's image we shall represent sometimes as equal in power to the Sun, and sometimes as like the earth, but he will in any case be the king of the underworld, as though his power were stronger there than anywhere else, where he governed the souls already departed from the bodies of mortals.

<aside>Judges of the underworld</aside>
<aside>Plato</aside>

And so that each of these souls might be given a place and a punishment according to its merits, he had three judges appointed for the purpose: Aeacus one, Rhadamanthus another, and Minos the third, who, as has been said elsewhere, were sons of Jupiter, one from Europe and two from Asia. Of these I shall first say what is read about them in Plato, and afterwards I shall come to the image of Pluto, because it seems to me that this must be a very fine and pleasing matter, from which one can see how these three are to be depicted, and in which one also learns what judges ought to be. Thus, then, Plato said.

Once, in the time of Saturn, there was a law of this kind, which even today is among the gods and always was: that all those men who in life had been just and good should, on dying, go to the Isles of the Blessed, and, conversely, that whoever had done evil in life should after death be deservedly punished in a place appointed for the purpose. And in the time of Saturn, and when Jupiter began to reign, men were likewise judged while still alive, and by judges who were also still alive, on the very day on which they were to die; so it happened that many were unjustly judged.

<aside>Why the judges were false</aside>

Learning of this from Pluto and from those who had charge of the Isles of the Blessed, since many were coming to them without deserving it, Jupiter said: “I shall see to this disorder well, since I know that its cause is that men are now judged before they die, and while still clothed in the mortal body, where there are some who speak well of them and others ill. And so many impious and wicked souls dare to present themselves to the judges as good, because they cover their wickedness with beauty of body, nobility of family and splendour of wealth; nor do they lack witnesses to say that throughout their whole life they were always good and just. So the judges, likewise clothed in earthly limbs, which are like a dark veil around the soul, cannot but marvel at their goodness, and judge them for that reason worthy of every good.

<aside>A good order for judging souls</aside>

“We must therefore first bring it about that men do not know when they are to die, as they do now (and so Prometheus was commanded to do this). Then, stripped of all earthly things and already dead, let them go before the judges, who are likewise to be naked and dead, so that with the soul alone they see only souls, naked and open. And so it will easily turn out that the judgment passed on them is just.

“For this reason I wish, as I have already resolved within myself, that my sons — two born in Asia, that is Minos and Rhadamanthus, and one from Europe, who is Aeacus — after they are dead, standing in a certain meadow (this was called the field of truth) where the road divides in two, one branch of which goes to the underworld and the other to the Isles of the Blessed, shall be the judges of the souls of mortals; and Rhadamanthus will judge all the Asiatics, and Aeacus those who come from Europe, and if any doubt arises at any time, it will fall to Minos to decide it, so that the souls may be sent to the places they deserve without any deception.”

<aside>Rhadamanthus</aside>
<aside>Aeacus</aside>
<aside>Minos</aside>

This was the order laid down by Jupiter, so that souls would be justly judged. For this reason Rhadamanthus and Aeacus, when they judge, each hold a rod in hand; and Minos sits apart from them, alone, and deliberates, he too holding in his hand a golden sceptre. So Ulysses says in Homer that he saw him in the underworld giving judgment to the dead. The souls of the dead bear on themselves, marked and impressed, all the passions they had and all they did while they were joined to bodies. So that the just judges, when they see them before them, do not ask or wish to know who they were, but look at what they did while they were in the world, and according to that judge them and send them to the place they deserve, either of punishments or of pleasures.

<aside>Dante</aside>

Next Plato says which souls for the most part go to the place of the damned and which to that of the Blessed, but I shall not relate it, since what I have said is enough for me to sketch a little picture of the three judges of the underworld. Of these, Dante seems to have represented Minos in the form of a beast, for in his *Inferno* he puts him with a tail and shows him snarling, just as dogs do, when he says:

> There stands Minos, horribly, and snarls;
> he examines the sins at the entrance,
> judges and sends according as he coils.
> I say that when the ill-born soul
> comes before him, it confesses all,
> and that connoisseur of sins
> sees what place in Hell is fitting for it;
> he girds himself with his tail as many times
> as the grades he wishes it to be sent down.
>
> *(Dante)*

<aside>What Minos signifies</aside>

And some take it that by him is meant the remorse that each person feels in his soul for his own errors, which continually torments him and accuses him, if not before others, then before his own conscience, and shows him the punishment and the pains that the sins committed make him deserve.

Hence it comes about that there are, as I said, three judges in the underworld, by which has been understood this world of ours, where Pluto reigns, who was so named from riches among the Greeks, since by him they understood the earth, from which mortals draw everything that is now most prized. And the Latins called him Dis for the same reason, that is, because riches come from him, which in Latin are called by a very similar word; or, as Quintilian holds, he was so called by the opposite sense, as if he could not be rich, since the dead are believed to be deprived of all riches.

<aside>Why Pluto is king of the dead</aside>
<aside>Claudian</aside>

But let us leave these expositions aside, and also what is said on the matter, namely that Pluto was god, or king, of the dead because he invented funeral rites and everything that is done around the dead, and let us draw his portrait according to the fables, which represent him in the underworld, seated like a king on a high throne. And Claudian describes him so, when he relates that he sends Mercury to Jupiter to ask him for a wife, as the Parcae had begged him to do.

> On the dread throne of the underworld
> Dis sat with majesty, all
> fearsome, his head girt with black mist;
> in his hand he held the rusty sceptre.
>
> *(Claudian)*

<aside>Pluto's colour</aside>
<aside>Pluto's crown</aside>

Martianus Capella likewise gives him the crown, as to a king, when he describes him together with his brother Neptune, saying that he is of dusky colour, and has on his head a crown of black ebony dyed with the darkness of shadowy night.

<aside>Pluto's sceptre</aside>
<aside>Pluto taken for the Sun</aside>

The sceptre that he holds in his hand likewise shows him to be a king, and it is small, because it shows the kingdom of this lower world; for so Porphyry explains it, as Eusebius reports, and he understands the Sun under the name of Pluto, called king of the underworld because in the winter-time it shows itself little to us; but it stays for the most part with those peoples who are in the lower part of the world, if indeed it is true that we are in the upper one, for they have understood it otherwise, as Servius reports that Tiberianus wrote that a letter had once come from the Antipodes, carried by the wind, which began thus: “We who are above greet you who are below.” And Aristotle likewise shows with reason that we are the ones below.

<aside>Proserpina</aside>

But this serves our purpose not at all; it is enough that Pluto, if the Sun is understood by him, is believed to be underground for all the time that it does not appear above our horizon, and keeps with him the abducted Proserpina, who shows the power of the seed, because it then lies enclosed in the womb of the earth.

He has a helmet, as Homer, Plato and Hyginus said, because the summit of the Sun is hidden from us. And according to the fables the helmet of Pluto, or of Orcus (for Pluto was also called Orcus), made invisible whoever wore it, so that while he saw others, he himself was not seen at all. And they say that Perseus had it when he cut off Medusa's head, and that with it he hid from her sisters, who were right behind him and would have treated him badly had it not been for the helmet of Orcus, given to him by Minerva, who in Homer likewise made use of it so as not to be seen by Mars while fighting against the Trojans.

<aside>Fulgentius</aside>

The dog Cerberus with three heads, who is at his feet, as Fulgentius also writes, who calls Pluto governor and guardian of the earth and depicts him surrounded by dark shadows with a sceptre in his hand, signifies that envy is born in mortals in three ways, namely by nature, by chance or by accident; or else, as others hold, that three things are needed by the seed if it is to produce fruit: first, that it be scattered on the earth, then that it be covered there, and lastly that it sprout.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>
<aside>Key in Pluto's hand</aside>

Pindar imagines that Pluto has a rod in his hand, and says that with it he leads souls into the underworld. And some gave him a key, as though he thus keeps the kingdom of the underworld locked so that the souls that have once gone down there can never leave it again. Hence we read in Pausanias that in the temple of Juno in a certain part of Greece a tablet was set up, on which many things were carved, and among others Pluto and Proserpina with two nymphs, of whom one held a ball in her hand, the other a key, because (Pausanias himself adds) the key is the emblem of Pluto, since he keeps the house of the underworld locked so that no one can leave it from there.

<aside>Seneca</aside>

This gave occasion to the fables to feign that Cerberus stands at the door of the underworld and barks only at one who tries to depart, terrifying the lost souls there, as Seneca says, describing him in this way:

> The dreadful dog, who keeps guard
> over the lost kingdom, and with three mouths
> makes it resound with a horrible voice,
> giving grave fear to the sad shades,
> has his head and neck girt with serpents,
> and his tail is a fierce dragon, which
> hisses, writhes, and thrashes all over.
>
> *(Seneca)*

<aside>Apollodorus</aside>
<aside>Dante</aside>

Apollodorus also describes him so, except that he says in addition that the hairs on his back are all little serpents. And Dante says this of the same:

> Cerberus, a cruel and strange beast,
> with three throats barks doglike
> over the people who are submerged here.
> His eyes are vermilion, his beard greasy and black,
> his belly wide, and his hands clawed.
> He claws the spirits, swallows them, and quarters them.
>
> *(Dante)*

<aside>Hesiod</aside>
<aside>Eurynomus</aside>

Hesiod represented him with a hundred heads, and says that he was Pluto's doorkeeper, and that he fawned on all who entered the underworld, but leaped at once on anyone who wished to leave it, and devoured him. This suits his name very well, because, deriving it from the Greek, Cerberus means that he devours flesh. And for this reason some have said that by him is understood the earth, which devours dead bodies. And a similar creature existed among the gods of the underworld at Delphi, called Eurynomus by those peoples, who was believed to eat the flesh of the dead so as to leave the bones quite bare, as Pausanias relates, who describes him as all blackish, of the colour of flies, sitting on a vulture's skin and showing his teeth. Some have also held that by Cerberus is meant this body of ours, which shows itself agreeable to one who enters the underworld, that is, gives himself to vices and lascivious pleasures, and then cries out at one who wants to leave it, that is, to leave these and give himself to virtue.

> Images of Pluto, god of the underworld, of Proserpina his wife, of Eurynomus devourer of the flesh of the dead, and of Cerberus, the three-throated dog, guardian of the underworld. Pluto is taken for the Sun in the time of winter, in which the power of the earth stays contracted in itself; and Proserpina is understood as the earth; the dog as the three things necessary to the seed: to be born, to grow, and to be perfected.

<aside>What it means to descend to the underworld</aside>

And so perhaps Virgil understood it, when he depicted this beast as rising up against Aeneas going into the underworld; which, although it seems contrary to what Hesiod and the others wrote of it, saying that it shows itself agreeable at the entrance to whoever goes in, is nevertheless not so. For one must notice that all those who have gone into the underworld did not go there for one and the same reason, nor to one and the same end, and so different outcomes also befell them. For whoever goes into the underworld (which now means nothing other than to descend among the lost throng of the vices), in order to remain always among vicious pleasures, finds Cerberus agreeable at the entrance, because this body is silent and enjoys itself, satisfying its lascivious and disordered appetites, but it cries out afterwards, when it sees that the man wants to turn back and leave them in order to follow reason. So whoever makes this journey in order to arrive at the consideration of the vices, so that he may know how he is to flee them and thus become freer for virtuous works, as Aeneas did, finds Cerberus rising against him, which is to say that the sensual appetite cries out, because it sees that it cannot enjoy those pleasures which it most desires.

And for this reason it was also feigned that Hercules went into the underworld and drew Cerberus out of it bound, as a figure of the prudent man, who binds and restrains these senses of the body so that he easily draws them after him out of the hell of the vices and guides them by the light of virtue. And that Pirithous, on the contrary, having gone to take away Pluto's wife in order to satisfy his lascivious appetite, was left dead there by Cerberus, because whoever plunges entirely into foul and vicious pleasures does not afterwards return to virtuous action, but dies among them.

<aside>Hecataeus</aside>

Hecataeus wrote, as Pausanias reports, that there was no dog at all in the underworld, but that this was feigned because in a certain cavern, through which it was believed that one could descend into the underworld, there was a terrible serpent that killed at once whoever approached it, and that this was the beast that Hercules brought out of the underworld to Eurystheus; Homer gave it the name of dog only, but others after him called it Cerberus, and feigned that it had three heads. Of this, and of many other things that remain to be said about this beast, I say no more for now, because it will be more fitting to put them afterwards in a certain writing on the soul that I have already planned.

<aside>Seneca</aside>

But I return to Pluto, of whom Seneca draws a portrait in this way, saying in the tragedy *Hercules Furens*:

> With terrible and cruel majesty
> sits Pluto, severe and gloomy of brow;
> yet not so much that he does not show himself
> still in part like his brothers,
> and born of the heavenly seed. His face
> seems to be that of Jupiter at the time when he
> unfurls the burning thunderbolt; and the dark
> kingdom has nothing that is more fearsome
> than he, since before his fearsome look
> trembles that which brings terror to others.
>
> *(Seneca)*

<aside>Pluto's chariot</aside>

To him the ancients gave a chariot drawn by four very fierce black horses that breathed fire, named Orphnaeus, Aethon, Nycteus and Alastor, for that many are given by Claudian, although Boccaccio says that there were only three, and that the chariot likewise had no more than three wheels, whoever devised it wishing to show in this way what the toil and danger are of those who seek to grow rich, and the uncertainty of things to come; because they also took him for the god of riches.

<aside>God of riches</aside>
<aside>Plutus</aside>
<aside>Aristophanes</aside>
<aside>Lucian</aside>

Although the Greeks had yet another god of riches, who in fact had almost the same name as this one, since they called him Plutus, he was nevertheless different from him, at least in image: for Aristophanes describes him as a blind man, and says that Jupiter put out his eyes so that he should not be able to recognise good men, learned and modest, because from childhood he showed that he loved them so much that he went about saying everywhere that he wanted to be with them always. Lucian likewise represents him as not only blind but also lame, and says that he goes with a litter [?1], sometimes being quite free and swift in walking; for it is said that in giving riches to the wicked he is quick and swift, but that when he brings them to the good he goes with slow and lagging steps, which is also characteristic of fortune.

And so Pausanias writes that it was a shrewd idea of him who among the Thebans placed the god Plutus in the hands of Fortune, as though she were his mother and nurse. And he adds then that Cephisodotus, an excellent sculptor, acted no less shrewdly, who made for the Athenians a statue of Peace, and placed in her lap the god Plutus, because peace preserves riches, and wars dissipate them.

<aside>Stobaeus</aside>

In Stobaeus, in the comparison which this Plutus and Virtue make of themselves with one another, he boasts of leading the desires of men to the desired end; and of his birth Hesiod says that when a certain Iasion was loved by the goddess Ceres, Plutus was born from their union, who was afterwards completely happy in all his affairs, and used to apply this happiness of his to others as well. Interpreting this, some say that from the earth, understood as Ceres, joined with Iasion, which means the farmer, is born this Plutus, which is interpreted as riches, since it is indeed from the fertility of the soil, which comes about through cultivating it well, that man acquires riches and goods.

<aside>Philostratus</aside>

Plutarch writes that among the Lacedaemonians the god Plutus was blind and always lay down. And the people of Rhodes had him seeing, and with wings, and gilded, as is gathered from Philostratus, who says that Plutus stood guarding the citadel of that city, painted with wings, as one who had descended from the clouds; gilded because gold was the matter in which he first appeared, and with eyes, because he came from divine providence.

<aside>Rained gold [?2]</aside>

For he says that at the birth of Minerva gold rained on the Rhodians, and this is read in Claudian too, where he praises Stilicho. This was, according to the same Philostratus, because the people of Rhodes knew Minerva well and also worshipped her, but not as ought to be done, since they sacrificed to her without fire, and so Jupiter granted them the rain of gold. But to the people of Athens the goddess was given as to wiser people, who in their sacrifices used fire.

<aside>Varro</aside>

Then to Pluto, the god of the underworld, the cypress was given, and of its boughs and leaves the ancients made him wreaths, as of a sad and mournful tree, which was used at funerals, whether because once it is cut it does not sprout again, or because, as Varro says, they surrounded with its boughs the fire that burned the dead bodies, so that the heavy smell of the burning bodies should not offend those who stood around; it being the custom of the ancients that relatives and friends went to accompany the dead man as far as the place prepared for burning him, where they then all placed themselves around, and with certain plaintive cries answered a woman who, hired for a fee for this, wept, cried out and lamented as much as she could, and at times also said something good of the dead man; nor did they leave until the ashes were gathered and laid away, the woman having then left off weeping and said the last words, which meant as much as to say: “Now you may go.”

<aside>Narcissus, flower</aside>

Pluto was also at times wreathed with maidenhair, a herb that is commonly called Venus's hair. And there have been some, too, who put Narcissus around his head, likewise making a wreath of it, because this flower was believed to be pleasing to the dead, perhaps on account of the unhappy end of the youth who had been changed into it; for this reason they also made wreaths of it, as Cornutus says, for the infernal Furies.

These Furies were servants and ministers of Pluto, and often came to punish mortals for their impious and wicked deeds, or drew them on to do others; and there were three, whose names are Alecto, Tisiphone and Megaera. They were worshipped by the ancients more so that they should not do harm than so that they might do any good, as the Averrunci were worshipped too, so that they should remove and drive away all evil, and for this alone, says Pausanias, the Greeks too sacrificed to them. And the name itself shows precisely the power of the god Averruncus, because *auerruncare* among the Latins was the same as to remove and drive away.

So the Furies had temples and altars, like the other gods, and among the Greeks the Athenians called them the Severe Goddesses, and the Sicyonians called them Eumenides and sacrificed to them every year on a certain day appointed for it some pregnant sheep, and besides the other ceremonies offered them also little wreaths of flowers.

In Achaea too the Furies had a temple with very small wooden idols, into which, if anyone stained with some grave wickedness went, even if only to look, as people do, he at once became frenzied, and it seemed that all the terror of the world entered his heart, and for this reason they let nobody go in, as Pausanias notes. Pausanias, describing Arcadia, also relates that in a certain part of that country there was a temple and a field consecrated to the Maniae, whom he thinks to have been the Furies; because it was said that there Orestes lost his wits and became frenzied after killing his mother, and that not far from there was a certain little hill called the Finger, because a great finger cut in stone was seen there in memory of the fact that Orestes in his frenzy ate a finger of his hand in that place. From there Orestes passed then to another small hill not far off, where he found relief for his madness, and in another temple of the Furies, whom, as he had seen them all black when he began to go mad, so he saw then white, and so he returned at once to his senses. And for this reason the inhabitants of the country afterwards kept the custom of sacrificing to the White Goddesses and to the Graces together.

Cicero writes that the Romans likewise had a certain little grove consecrated to the goddess Furrina, where with solemn ceremonies they worshipped the Furies, whose idols had serpents on their heads instead of hair, for Aeschylus represented them so before all the others, who have followed him since, as Pausanias reports.

<aside>Seneca</aside>

So Seneca imagines that Juno speaks in this way, when she wants to drive Hercules mad:

> Now begin, you servants of Pluto,
> come forth with angry hand
> shaking the impious torches; up, Megaera,
> head and guide of you, who have horrid serpents
> in place of hair, raise
> the mournful torch from the funeral pyre,
> and let her come with it, bringing
> tearful torments and sorrow.
>
> *(Seneca)*

<aside>Dante</aside>

Dante says that, finding himself in the infernal depths, he directed his eyes to a certain tower.

> Where in an instant he saw, erect and sudden,
> three infernal Furies stained with blood,
> who had feminine limbs and bearing,
> and were girt with greenest Hydras;
> little serpents and horned vipers they had for hair,
> with which their fierce temples were bound.
>
> *(Dante)*

<aside>Strabo</aside>

But what they were like afterwards in other respects can be gathered from Strabo, who, writing of the Cassiterides Islands, says that one of them is inhabited by men all of dusky colour, dressed in tunics that reach down to their feet, and girt across the chest, with staffs in hand, exactly like those Furies that tragedies often show on the stage.

And Suda, reporting of Menippus the Cynic (into whose head had entered such a madness as to have himself believed an official of the underworld, and that the gods down there had sent him to see the evil that men did, and then report it to them) that he used the garb of the Furies, describes it in this way, saying: a black robe, long to the ground, not very wide, and girt across very tightly with a thick sash; he had a hat on his head, on which were drawn the twelve signs of the Zodiac, and his shoes were such as the actors of tragedies used, and he carried a thick ash staff in his hand; and he had a beard (which was his own) like a philosopher's, though this had nothing to do with the Furies, as can also be said of the hat. So the black robe, only long and girt across, and the staff that he had in his hand, will be in Menippus, according to Suda, the image of the garb of the Furies, as Strabo too described it.

<aside>Catullus</aside>

When Ariadne was left on the shore of the sea by Theseus, who went away with Phaedra, after the wretched woman had lamented a great deal, turning to pray for vengeance on him who had betrayed her, she called upon the Furies, speaking thus in Catullus:

> You Furies, who are wont to give mortals
> the deserved punishments for their evil deeds,
> whose viperous hair covers
> the sad brow, which bears marked
> in itself the impious fury, and opens and uncovers
> the raging wrath that comes from the breast,
> come here, come here to hear my complaints
> against this wicked, impious, and cruel man.
>
> *(Catullus)*

<aside>Lactantius</aside>
<aside>Why there are three Furies</aside>

As if there were no one else who could punish him better for his impiety. For the very emotions of the soul are those that trouble us more than anything else, when they turn aside from the straight path and become disordered; and the infernal Furies are nothing else in us: for the poets meant those emotions under the name of these. So Lactantius says thus: The poets feigned that the Furies were three, who came to disturb human minds, because there are three passions that drive men to do every evil without having even the slightest regard either for their own reputation, or for the family from which they descend, or for their own life: anger, which seeks vengeance; cupidity, which longs for riches; and lust, which gives itself over as prey to shameful pleasures.

> Images of Alecto, Tisiphone and Megaera, three infernal Furies, punishers of evil and also bringers of it, understood as three passions of the soul, anger, avarice and lust, with the black sheep sacred to them and with the turtle-doves, a sign of sadness.

These passions, though, were given to us by God to help us live well, and for this reason divine providence set certain limits for them, beyond which they no longer help us but harm us, because they change their nature, and from virtues, which they were before, become vices. For the desire to have was added to our soul so that each might see to obtaining what is necessary to life. Lascivious appetite was given to him so that he should use it only to beget children, and thus by continual succession the human offspring should be preserved; and it was ordained that he might be angry when he wished, so that he could better chastise the faults of others and put a curb on those who are in his power and take every liberty to do evil.

So these passions and emotions of our soul, as long as they stay within their nature and go no further than what they were ordained for, give us a quiet and tranquil life; but if they do otherwise, they disturb it all for us and torment us like the infernal Furies.

<aside>Statius</aside>

To the Furies the ancients gave lit torches in their hands, to show the ardours that the passions I have mentioned set in our breast, as will be seen better still in the image of Tisiphone: when she goes to sow hatred and discord between the impious brothers Eteocles and Polynices, Statius, showing the joy that she felt at going to do such a thing, portrays her in this way:

> Nowhere does she go more joyful or more swift,
> nor does she know any road better than this;
> in the place where harm is done to sinful souls [?3]
> there is no pit that is more pleasing to her.
> A thousand horned vipers from her savage brow
> cast a fearsome and evil shadow over her face;
> beneath two brows that jut out and overhang
> she has wicked eyes, hollow, twisted, and sunk in her head.
>
> Her face is tinted a bloody colour,
> like the enchanted Moon among the mists;
> the rest is pale and iron-grey,
> spread with congealed and dark gore.
> From her mouth comes a thick and malignant vapour,
> that not only poisons the grass and darkens the air,
> but scatters among mortals, by a fierce fate,
> hunger, thirst, impiety, horrors, and death.
>
> Nor is the garment that she wears
> different from so strange and fearsome an aspect:
> ripped at the back, she fastens it at the breast
> with buckles; every buckle is a serpent.
> Atropos and Proserpina for their pleasure
> are wont to adorn her so charmingly.
> Her right hand whirls a scourge of Hydras,
> the other plays horribly with fire.
>
> *(Statius)*

And when Juno sends her to take away the wits of Athamas, Ovid describes her with a troubled look, with grey hair mixed with serpents that hang down over her face, dressed in a gown all spattered with blood; in his account she is girt across with serpents twisted together and has in her hand a torch likewise dyed with blood, and fear and terror go with her.

<aside>Stygian Marsh</aside>

So the Furies served not Pluto alone, although they were of his family, but Juno also, and Jupiter likewise: who appear to have had business in the underworld too, so that each of them was often called infernal, and Stygian, from the Stygian Marsh, which encircles the underworld all around, as the poets sing; saying also that the gods always swore by its waters, with the penalty for whichever of them had sworn falsely of being at once deprived of his dignity for a year, of not drinking nectar and not eating ambrosia. And that privilege was given to the Stygian Marsh, that the gods swore by it, in consideration of Victory its daughter, who was with Jupiter in the war against the Giants. But it is also read that this was feigned, because Styx signifies grief and sadness, from which the gods, who enjoy perpetual gladness and joy, are always far; as though they swore by that from which they are wholly alien.

<aside>Platonists</aside>
<aside>River Lethe</aside>
<aside>Acheron</aside>
<aside>Cocytus</aside>
<aside>Phlegethon</aside>

This Marsh encircles the underworld, because nowhere else is greater sadness found, and for this reason there were also the river Lethe, Acheron, Phlegethon, Cocytus and other rivers, which signify weeping, pain, sadness, lament and other similar passions, continually felt by the damned. The Platonists hold that these are to be understood in this way, saying that the soul goes into the underworld when it descends into the mortal body, where it finds the river Lethe, which brings about oblivion, and from this it passes to Acheron, which means deprivation of joy, because the soul, having forgotten the things of Heaven, loses all the joy that it felt from the knowledge of them, and so is wholly sad and mournful and is therefore surrounded by the Stygian Marsh, and often laments over it and weeps, which makes the river Cocytus, whose waters are all tears and weeping; just as Phlegethon has waters of fire and flames, which show the ardour of anger and of the other passions that torment us while we are in the hell of this body, as we have said that the Furies also did; to whom Virgil adds wings, and says that they are always ready before Jupiter, whenever he wishes to send to mortals some great terror of death, war, plague or other gravest evil.

<aside>Aelian</aside>

And Aelian writes that the turtle-doves were consecrated by the ancients to the Furies; nor do I find that any other animal was proper to them, except that in Virgil one of them changes into an owl, or an eagle-owl, whichever it was, when Jupiter sends her to frighten Turnus while he fights with Aeneas.

<aside>Lyssa</aside>

There have been some, then, who add to the three Furies already named a fourth, whom they call Lyssa. In our language this word means rage, and for this reason they hold that it is she who makes mortals rage and lose their wits. So Euripides imagines that Iris, commanded by Juno, leads her to Hercules, so that she may make him furious and raging. She has her head girt with serpents and carries a goad, or a whip, in her hand.

<aside>Harpies</aside>

To the Furies we may add the Harpies, because the ancients believed that the gods likewise sometimes sent these to punish mortals for their wicked deeds; they too lived in the underworld, although Virgil once has them living on the Strophades islands in the Ionian Sea: but whether they lived there or elsewhere does not matter to me in depicting them, and still less to anyone who will want to know what they were like.

<aside>Ariosto</aside>

These, then, had the face of a woman, quite beautiful but thin, and the rest of the body was that of a bird, with great wings and with hooked talons; so Virgil describes them, and Ariosto has imitated him very well in this part, and almost translated him; which is why I leave out Virgil's verses and give only Ariosto's, which speak thus of the Harpies:

> They were seven in one flock; all of them
> had a woman's face, and were pale and deathlike,
> wasted and dried up by long hunger,
> more horrible to see than death itself.
> Their wings were great, misshapen, and ugly,
> their hands rapacious, their claws hooked and twisted.
> Large and foul the belly, and a long tail,
> like a serpent's, that coils and uncoils.
>
> *(Ariosto)*

<aside>Dante</aside>

And Dante likewise, taking their portrait too from Virgil, made a sketch of it, saying in his *Inferno*:

> Here the foul Harpies make their nests,
> who drove the Trojans from the Strophades
> with sad announcement of future harm.
> They have broad wings, human necks and faces,
> feet with talons, and a feathered great belly;
> they make laments upon the strange trees.
>
> *(Dante)*

<aside>Striges</aside>
<aside>Ovid</aside>

Ovid says that the Striges were born of the Harpies, certain large, fearsome, vile birds, extremely greedy for human blood, and he describes them thus:

> They have a great head, and the eyes stand out
> beyond the common use, large and prominent,
> full of foul and cruel horror.
> Their talons are curved and intent on the prey,
> hooked the beak, and hoary in colour
> the feathers, and it seems that everyone fears them.
>
> *(Ovid)*

These flew by night, and, getting into houses where there were tender infants, sucked their sweet blood, and so the poor little things died of it.

<aside>Statius</aside>

In Statius they are born in the underworld, with the face, neck and breast of a woman, and have some little serpents that come down from the head onto the forehead and the face; he says likewise that they go by night into houses to feed on the blood of little children. And to remedy this evil the ancients worshipped that goddess Carna or Cardea, of whom I spoke in the image of Janus.

<aside>Pliny</aside>

Pliny thinks that what was said of the Striges is a fable, and that the ancients used this word only to insult and abuse others, just as even today we call wicked old women and all enchantresses, who are always ready to do harm to others, witches (*streghe*).

<aside>Lamiae</aside>

Some then held that the Lamiae among the Greeks were the same as the Striges among the Latins.

<aside>Philostratus</aside>

But Philostratus in the *Life of Apollonius* says that the Lamiae are spirits, or as we say wicked demons, cruel, lustful beyond measure, and greedy for human flesh.

Suda, and Favorinus too, write that Lamia was a beautiful woman, with whom Jupiter fell in love and by whom he had a son, whom jealous Juno afterwards caused to perish wretchedly, so that the poor mother wept so much that she dissolved entirely, and in vengeance for her own son has gone on ever since doing harm to other children. Others say that the Lamiae were animals that had the aspect of a woman and the feet of a horse.

<aside>Dio</aside>

But the historian Dio describes them in another way, and because he has said more about them than all the others, I want to report everything that he writes about them. It is read in him, then, that in certain deserted places of Libya there are some most cruel wild beasts, which have the face and breast of a woman, so beautiful that better could not be painted, and in their look and in their eyes there is seen such grace and such charm that whoever looks at them judges them all gentle and agreeable. The rest of the body is then covered with very hard scales, and goes turning into a serpent, so that it ends in the head of a terrible and frightful serpent. These beasts have no wings, nor do they speak; and they have no other voice but a hiss, and are so swift that there is no animal that can escape them, and they hunt men in this way.

<aside>Jeremiah</aside>

They show their beautiful breast, as the prophet Jeremiah also said, although in writing it he meant something other than these beasts: “And the Lamiae had their white breasts uncovered.” Whoever sees these breasts becomes so enamoured that he desires to be with the beasts, and, driven by such desire, goes to them as to most beautiful women, who do not move at all, but, as if bashful, often lower their eyes to the ground, and never show their hooked talons except when the one who has come to them is quite close, for then they seize him with those, and do not let him go before the serpent, which is their end and almost a tail, has killed him with venomous bites, and then they devour him.

> Image of the Harpies, Striges and Lamiae, punishers and bringers of evil, and also frightful monsters of Libya, signifying the feigned and artificial beauty and allurements of harlots, and the flatteries of wicked flatterers, which at first bring delight, then harm to the soul and the body, to honour and to life.

<aside>Sphinx</aside>

Of the Lamiae I say no more, but I come to depict the Sphinxes, which are monsters not very unlike them, fabulous in part, and in part real. For Pliny writes that these beasts are in Ethiopia, of dusky coat, with two breasts on the chest, and of monstrous face.

<aside>Albertus Magnus</aside>

And Albertus Magnus, writing of animals, puts them among the apes, and by what he says of them they are almost what we call monkeys.

<aside>Aelian</aside>

But the poets write of them in another way, and from them all the sculptors and painters have since taken the portrait; for these, as Aelian says, depict the Sphinx as half woman and half lion, for so the fable describes her, which is told of Thebes, where she sat on a certain crag, proposing riddles to whoever passed that way, and whoever could not solve them was wretchedly killed and devoured by her. The riddle was what animal it was that used first four feet, then two, and finally three; and they say that when Oedipus had explained it, saying that it was man, who in infancy uses hands and feet in walking and so goes on four, and when grown goes on two only, and at last, when he is weighed down by years, goes on three, using a staff for his support, she, filled with grief, threw herself down from the said crag, and so was deprived of life. The true image of this one, according to the fables, is that she has the face and breast of a woman with great wings, and the rest is a lion's, as can also be gathered from certain verses of Ausonius. It is read in Pliny that in Egypt, where those very great Pyramids were, there was a Sphinx, which the people of the country revered as a wild numen, made of living rock, and so great that the head had a circumference of a hundred and two feet, and a hundred and forty-three in length, and from the belly to the top of the head there were a hundred and sixty-two feet.

<aside>Chimera</aside>

I shall not be silent about the Chimera either, a monster wholly fabulous and feigned by the poets, which, as Homer describes it, and after him Lucretius, had the head of a lion, the belly of a goat, and the tail of a fierce dragon, and cast burning flames from its mouth, as Virgil also says, who puts it at the first entrance of the underworld with some other terrible monsters.

But the truth was that the Chimera was not one beast but a mountain in Lycia, which from its highest peak, in the manner of Mount Etna, sent out living flames, and around it stood many lions; in the middle it had trees and quite pleasant pastures with various plants, and at its foot it was all around full of serpents, so that no one dared to live there. For this Bellerophon found a remedy, sent there by Iobates, so that he should remain dead there in vengeance for the outrage done (as Iobates believed) to Stheneboea his daughter, wife of Proetus. Bellerophon brought it about that the whole mountain was afterwards safely inhabited. For this reason the fables said that the Chimera was killed by Bellerophon.

> Image of the Theban Sphinx, overcome by Oedipus, and of the Lycian Chimera, overcome by Bellerophon, which was a mountain in Lycia, full of fierce animals and deserted; reduced by Bellerophon to cultivation, and made habitable.

<aside>Parcae</aside>

With these monsters would go the designs of many evils, which all belong to the infernal family, but because it will be more convenient to speak of them in some other place, as I have already resolved to do, and it is not a matter that bears much on this place, I leave them, and come to describe the Parcae, who were likewise placed by the ancients among the number of the gods, and like the others had temples and consecrated altars.

<aside>Claudian</aside>

There were as many of them as there were Furies, and they served Pluto likewise, as one of them says in Claudian, when she begs him not to make war on Jupiter; and her words are these:

> Of shadows and of night, O eternal and great
> fierce ruler and judge, for whom always
> we, turning the threads together, toil
> so much to please you in everything,
> on whom depend the last end and the seed,
> you who govern living and dying, who keep
> human bodies eternally equal.
>
> *(Claudian)*

And it is no wonder that the Parcae serve Pluto, because they were believed to spin human life, which lasts either little or much, according as the frail body is by its nature fit to live more or less; and this in man is the matter represented by Pluto. From the changes, then, that matter receives in itself come death and life, which the Parcae make long and short in proportion to that measure. And for this reason the ancients feigned that they were three, and that one had the care of birth, another of life, the third of death. So it is that when all three are together spinning the lives of mortals, one, Clotho the youngest, holds the distaff and draws the thread, the other, Lachesis, of greater age, winds it round the spindle, and the third, Atropos, already old, cuts it.

<aside>Dante</aside>

So Virgil speaks of Dante to someone who marvelled to see him so far beyond Purgatory, meaning that he was not yet dead:

> But because she who spins day and night
> had not yet drawn out for him the distaff
> that Clotho puts on each and compiles.
>
> *(Dante)*

> Images of Clotho, Lachesis and Atropos, called the three Parcae, in whose hands, the ancients said, are the life and death of all; signifying the alterations of life, from which come its length and brevity, understood also as fate and destiny.

Fulgentius says that the Parcae are ready for Pluto's service because their power is only over earthly things, and we have already said that by Pluto too the earth is understood.

The greater part of the writers conclude that the Parcae are so called from *Parco*, a Latin word which in our vernacular means “to spare”, by that figure which they call antiphrasis, that is, one that denotes the contrary of what the word signifies; as though they meant to say that they have this name because they never spare anyone.

<aside>Varro</aside>
<aside>Decima</aside>
<aside>Nona</aside>

But Varro holds, as Gellius reports, that they were named from giving birth (*partorire*), as though the care of it fell to them: hence, he says, the Latins called one Decima, another Nona, because the time of mature birth is almost always in one of these two months, the ninth and the tenth.

<aside>Morta</aside>

But because whoever is born must also die, the third of the Parcae was called Morta, from death (*morte*), with which she was believed to put an end to human life.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

She is portrayed by Pausanias, when he relates the things carved on the chest of Cypselus, in this way. There, he says, was Polynices fallen on his knees, over whom his brother Eteocles was advancing to kill him, and behind him there was a woman with teeth and hooked claws, who looked in appearance crueller than any most cruel wild beast; and this was, as the letters carved there showed, Morta, one of the Parcae, and she meant to signify that Polynices died by destiny, but Eteocles by his own fault and by his own desert.

<aside>Fate</aside>

And because many of the ancient philosophers held that divine providence had once disposed all things, so that they could no longer be changed, as though the causes of these were so ordered together that of themselves they come to produce them, from which arises the force of Fate; some have said that the poets understood the same under the fiction of the Parcae, and that they made them three because everything begins from a beginning and, proceeding through its proper middle, arrives at the destined end; and they were born of Chaos, because in the first separation that was made, the proper causes were assigned to all things.

Others have represented the Parcae as born of Erebus, which was the deep and dark place of the earth, and of Night, wishing, through the darkness [?4] of the father and the mother, to show how hidden the causes of things are.

<aside>Goddess Necessity</aside>
<aside>Garments of the Parcae</aside>

Plato makes them daughters of the goddess Necessity, between whose knees he places that great adamantine spindle that extends from one pole to the other. The Parcae sit beside their mother on a high and raised throne, at equal distances from one another, and sing together with the Sirens, who are above the celestial spheres: Lachesis of the past, Clotho of the present, and Atropos of what is to come. And they also put their hands to the spindle together with the goddess Necessity their mother, in this way: Clotho puts on her right hand, Atropos her left, and Lachesis touches it with both hands, on this side and on that. They are dressed in white garments, and have their heads girt with a crown.

Plato goes on to say that the lots of human life come from Lachesis, and some other things that contain lofty meanings and great mysteries, as I shall explain when I write about the soul, as I have promised at other times to do, for it is not to the purpose now; but it is enough to know that the Parcae were dressed in white, and, crowned like queens, sat and held out, one a single hand and another both, to the spindle that lay between the knees of Necessity their mother. She too was called a goddess, and a temple was dedicated to her and to the goddess Violence, as Pausanias writes, among the Corinthians, where they said that no one was permitted to enter.

> Image of Necessity, and of the adamantine spindle traversing the world, and images of the three Parcae, daughters of Necessity, named Clotho, Atropos and Lachesis, denoting the three times and three states of life, past, present and future; according to the ancients they also denote destiny.

<aside>Catullus</aside>

Some have made wreaths for the Parcae from white Narcissus, and others have girt their heads with a white band, like Catullus, who, representing them with old faces, describes them thus:

> The Parcae have about them a white garment
> that covers and girds their trembling limbs,
> edged with purple, and on their heads
> they have a white band that ties and binds.
> And although they are old, they are nonetheless quick
> with the hand, which always forms the thread
> in various ways, from which human life
> comes and goes to its last departure.
>
> *(Catullus)*

<aside>Homer</aside>

Homer, in the praises that he sings to Mercury, says that the Parcae are three virgin sisters, who have wings and a head sprinkled with the whitest flour.

<aside>Venus among the Parcae</aside>

And in Pausanias it is read that Venus was set by the Greeks as one of the Parcae, and above all by those of Athens, who in a certain temple dedicated to this goddess had an idol of square form, like the Herms that were made for Mercury, with an epigram that named it Heavenly Venus, one of the Parcae, and the oldest of them; nor was there anyone who could say anything else about it.

This brings to my mind what the Romans did: they kept in the temple of Libitina the things that served to carry the dead to burial. Plutarch, giving the reason for this, says that Libitina was Venus, and that in her temple the ornaments of the dead were kept, to remind us of the fragility of human life, the beginning and end of which were in the power of one and the same goddess. For, as we have said another time, Venus was the goddess of generation, and making her the oldest of the Parcae was meant to say precisely that it was she who put an end to human life.

But perhaps we may also say that this showed that the Parcae were believed to be something from Heaven, although they were said to serve Pluto, and I have placed them with him for the reasons I have given.

<aside>The god Moiragetes</aside>

So it is found that in a certain part of Greece there was an altar dedicated to the god Moiragetes, which means head and leader of the Parcae, and Pausanias says that it is to be held as certain that this was a surname of Jupiter, because he alone has the Parcae in his power, and he alone does what the Fates ordain.

<aside>Chancellors of the gods</aside>

From this, perhaps, came the fact that some called them Chancellors of the gods, as though it were their office to learn the will of Jupiter and the decisions of the whole heavenly Senate, and to put them in writing, so that they could then be drawn up in full when the time came to send them to be carried out.

<aside>Fulgentius</aside>

Fulgentius, interpreting the names of these, says that Clotho, which is a Greek name, means in our language summoning (*euocatione*), Lachesis means lot (*sorte*), and Atropos denotes without order (*senza ordine*), as though the first were she who calls us to life, the second shows us the manner that we must use while we live, and the third the condition of death, which is wont to come without order or any law of lot.

<aside>Peter Apian</aside>

I remember having once seen in the book of antiquities collected by Peter Apian the Parcae drawn in this way, as he says they were on a certain lead plate that was once found in Styria in the year 1500. A line is drawn in a circle, and inside it a naked youth sits on a small hillock, covering his face and eyes with both hands, and above his head is written Clotho. At his feet lies a winged boy, also naked, who keeps his right hand on his right knee and rests his left arm on a human skull, which holds a shin-bone crosswise in its mouth; above the boy was written Lachesis, and above the skull Atropos. Then it seemed that, to the right of the boy, not far from him, there was a burning flame, and behind, almost toward the youth who sat, a little tuft of grass with some flowers; and all the rest was dry ground with some stones scattered there in disorder.

> Images of the three Parcae found, according to Peter Apian, in Styria in 1500, interpreted thus: Clotho, summoning, that is the beginning of life; Lachesis, lot, that is the use, and path, or course of life; Atropos, without order, that is the necessity and variety of death, common to all the things of the world.

<aside>Dante</aside>

Now, to put an end to the family of the underworld, let us see how the ferryman was made who stood at the bank of the river Acheron to carry across the souls that, leaving mortal bodies, made their way there from all the world, provided, however, that they died in the wrath of God, as in Dante Virgil says to him in this way:

> My son, the courteous master said,
> those who die in the wrath of God
> all gather here from every country.
>
> *(Dante)*

<aside>Charon</aside>

But the ancients did not make this distinction, for they held that all souls went there after death, although they did not all cross in the same way. This can be gathered from Virgil when Aeneas goes down to the underworld: on arrival, only those souls crossed whose bodies had already been buried; but those that had not yet had burial of the body wandered a hundred years before they could enter the little boat of Charon, which carried them to the other bank, Charon the demon, with eyes of embers.

<aside>Seneca</aside>

Seneca describes him in this way, when in the tragedy *Hercules Furens* Theseus tells Amphitryon what he saw down in the underworld:

> An old man guards that river, horrid and sad
> in look and in dress, and from one
> bank to the other carries the mournful shades
> in a little boat, for the steering of which
> he uses only a long pole.
> His cheeks are hollow and all full of foul
> squalor, and from his old chin hangs
> the dishevelled beard, and the black cloth
> that covers his filthy limbs only in part
> is gathered up by a knot, without order or art.
>
> *(Seneca)*

<aside>Virgil</aside>

And it is to be believed that he took the portrait from Virgil, who a good while before him painted him thus:

> There is the road that through the black air
> leads us straight to Acheron,
> and the Marsh, which grows ever blacker,
> and brings hot sand into Cocytus.
> At the entrance to the horrible river
> Charon stands as ferryman and guide.
> His eyes are of fire, and he is pale in look,
> his beard white, and long down to his chest.
> His robe hangs from his shoulders,
> tied in a knot, laden with filth.
> He attends continually to the steering
> with oar and sail of a light boat,
> which carries the souls, of whom a great multitude goes down
> into the underworld every hour, and nothing else;
> already old, and full of pride and harshness,
> but with a raw and green old age in him.
>
> *(Virgil)*

> Image of Charon, the infernal ferryman on the black river Acheron, with his boat and oar, understood as time that consumes life and destroys all things; by his other effects he also denotes the misery and unhappiness of human life.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

Polygnotus had also painted him thus in certain panels that he made of it in the temple of Apollo among the Phocians, having taken the design from the ancient poets, as Pausanias reports, who says that there was also some water there, which may be believed to have been the river Acheron, because of the ferryman who crossed it, and inside it there was much marsh reed, and some things that seemed rather shadows of fish than real fish.

<aside>Boccaccio</aside>
<aside>Explanation of Charon</aside>

Wishing to explain this image, Boccaccio says that by Charon is meant time, as Servius also understood it. Time is the son of Erebus, which is taken for the secret counsel of the divine mind, by which time and all other things are created; and its mother was Night, for before there was time no light was yet seen, and so it was made in darkness and seemed to be born from darkness. Charon was then placed in the underworld because those who are in Heaven have no need of time, as we mortals do, who inhabit the lowest part of the world; so that, if we look to them, it can rightly be said that we are in hell.

Charon carries mortals from one bank to the other because, once we are born, time carries us to death and makes us cross the river Acheron, which means “without joy” (*senza allegrezza*), exactly as happens to us in running through this life, frail, perishable, and all full of miseries. He is old, but nevertheless robust and fierce, because time does not lose its strength with the years; and he has about him a black and dirty cloth, because as long as we are subject to time we care little for anything but earthly things, which we shall find base and sordid if we wish to compare them with those of Heaven, on which we ought always to keep ourselves intent with all our desire. But this frail husk of the mortal body that we wear about us so covers from us the light of reason that we go almost blind through the hell of this world, guided by sense alone and by a thousand disordered appetites.

<aside>Virgil</aside>

So it is no wonder that we are then always surrounded by endless evils, which present themselves to us as soon as souls descend into the hell of this world of ours and are driven into mortal bodies; for in this way Virgil can be explained when he speaks of the evils that stand at the gates of hell, and his verses, rendered into our language, are these:

> Of the blind realm, fierce and horrible, as much
> as the soul knows that goes down there condemned,
> on the very first entrance Weeping has its seat,
> and wicked Thought, which is bent on Vengeance.
> With deathly face and mournful cloak
> there Sickness halts its step,
> and lies, its breast full of pain,
> with Old Age on one and the same bed.
>
> Close to her dwells Fear
> and languid Hunger, friend of theft,
> Poverty, which cares little for honour,
> Death (horrible shapes) and Toil.
> And that which steals man from himself
> and often restores and nourishes him,
> Sleep, which is kin to Death,
> and the sad Joys of twisted minds.
>
> There opposite was the place of impious War,
> with breast and hands stained with blood:
> like one that turns the earth
> often upside down, so that it weeps and languishes;
> then a roof of iron walls encloses
> the three Furies, who have in their hair more than one serpent;
> or rather, in place of hair, burning with rage,
> a thousand serpents girdle their temples.
>
> With her stays, and never from those parts
> does wicked mad Discord draw back her foot;
> on whose neck a great number
> of venomous snakes is also seen to hang.
> In the middle, moreover, a great elm spreads
> its ancient arms, on which have their seat,
> gathered among the leaves, the foolish Dreams,
> which make a man often yearn even while awake.
>
> *(Virgil)*

> Images of Mercury, messenger of the gods, god of eloquence and of merchants. This denotes that speech is the messenger and revealer of the mind and heart, and the Caduceus is a sign of concord, union and peace, with some animals sacred to him, denoting industry and vigilance in trading and in business.
