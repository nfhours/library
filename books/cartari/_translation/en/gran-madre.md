# The Great Mother

Source: books/cartari/_text/TEXT_reading.md, scans 0215–0243
Rules: books/cartari/_translation/en/RULES.md

---

The ancients believed that the Earth was the first of all the gods, and for that reason they called her the Great Mother and the Mother of the Gods. And since they saw that her nature was varied and her properties many, they gave her many names, and different ones, and worshipped her in various ways and made statues of her. Hence, having already said how the ancients sometimes understood her as Juno and made an image of her, I shall now speak of the other goddesses who were all goddesses signifying the Earth among the ancients.

<aside>Why the earth is called mother. The goddess Levana</aside>
<aside>Cunina. Vagitanus. Paventia</aside>
<aside>Potina. Edusa</aside>

To her alone of all the parts of the universe, Pliny writes, the surname of maternal reverence was deservedly given. For once mortals are born, she receives them, according to the custom of the ancients, which was to place the child, as soon as it had come out of its mother's womb, on the ground, as in the arms of the common mother of all, and then to lift it up again at once. For this reason they had a goddess called Levana, whom they believed to be set over this, so that with her numen she brought it about that the little child just born was happily lifted from the ground. In the same way they had another who had the guardianship of the cradles of those same little children, called by them the goddess Cunina; and Vagitanus was the god of the weeping of children, which in Latin is called *vagire*. The goddess Paventia presided over *pauore*, that is, the fear of the same children, and Rumina over suckling, because the ancients called the breast *ruma*. Potina was the goddess of *potione*, that is, of their drinking; and Edusa of *esca*, that is, of their eating.

Having received mortals, then, as soon as they are born, the Earth, like a loving mother, afterwards nourishes and sustains them as well; and when at last they are abandoned by all, she gathers them into her ample bosom and shuts them up in herself. And not only men and the other animals, but all other things as well seem to have life here among us from the earth, and to be sustained, nourished and preserved by her. For which reasons she was rightly called the Great Mother, and likewise the Mother of the Gods, because the gods of the ancients had been mortals, and had lived for a time on what the earth produces, as all other mortals live on it.

And she was the same as Ops, Cybele, Rhea, Vesta and Ceres, and others besides, who show the different powers of the Earth. Their names I shall explain as I draw their images, as it suits me, and I shall tell their fables, or something else, if it comes to the purpose. For just as painters adorn their pictures with all those ornaments that make them better, so that they may seem more beautiful to those who look at them, so have I tried to do while I draw these images with the pen. For I sometimes explain certain names, sometimes interpret some fable, and of some I tell at times simply, and at times also touch on some history, according as it seems to me to suit better what I have already said or have still to say, thinking that in this way I shall be, if not entertaining to the reader, at least not too tedious, since variety of things is wont to take away a great part of the tedium from readers.

<aside>Ops</aside>
<aside>Homer</aside>

Coming, then, to speak of the Great Mother: she was called Ops by the ancients because this word means help, and there is no one who helps the life of mortals more than the earth; hence Homer calls her the giver of life, because she gives us where we may conveniently dwell, and provides us with the means by which to feed ourselves, and in many other ways benefits us in the manner of a pitying mother.

<aside>Martianus Capella</aside>

And therefore Martianus Capella, describing her, says that she is of great age and has a large body, with which agrees what Pausanias writes, that in a certain part of Greece, near the river Crathis, there was a little temple of the Earth where she was called the goddess with the broad breast. And although she gives birth often, and has many children about her, she nevertheless has a robe all painted with flowers of different colours, and a mantle woven of green grasses, on which there seem to be all those things that mortals most prize, such as precious gems and all the metals; and there was seen on it also a great abundance of all fruits, and a marvellous plenty of all things. Now who is there who would not recognise the Earth in this portrait?

Varro, as St Augustine reports in the book of the *City of God*, holds that she is called Ops because through human labour (*opera*) she becomes better, and the more she is cultivated the more fertile she is; that she is named Proserpina because the crops that are born from her, as they come out of her, go as if creeping (*serpendo*); and that she is called Vesta because she clothes herself (*si veste*) in green grasses.

<aside>Explanation of the image of Ops</aside>

Besides this, Boccaccio too paints her and at the same time explains the whole picture, when he writes of the Genealogy of the Gods. He says that she has on her head a crown made of towers, because the circuit of the earth, like a crown, is all full of cities, castles, villages and other buildings. The robe woven of green grasses and encircled by leafy branches shows the trees, plants and grasses that cover the earth. She has a sceptre in her hand, which signifies that on the earth are all the kingdoms and all human riches, and also shows the power of earthly lords. By the tympana, which she likewise has, is understood the roundness of the earth, divided into two hemispheres, of which one is called the upper hemisphere, and is the one which we inhabit; and the other the lower, where the Antipodes are. She has then a chariot with four wheels, because although she herself stands still and is immovable, the works that are done upon her are varied in a certain order through the four seasons of the year, and go on succeeding one another.

<aside>Nature of the lions</aside>

Lions draw it: either to show what the farmers do when sowing grain, who cover it at once so that greedy birds may not make a prey of it, as the lions do when they walk through dusty places, for, as Solinus writes, they sweep away their footprints with the tail, so that hunters may not by these find out where they go; or because there is no earth, however harsh and hard it may be, that does not become soft and easy for producing when it is cultivated; or else it is to show, by putting the lion, king of the other animals, under the yoke of the goddess Ops, that the lords of the world are likewise subject to the laws of nature, and so have need of the help of the earth just as the others do.

The fables say that the Mother of the Gods, angry with Hippomenes and Atalanta because without respect for her numen they lay together in a wood consecrated to her, made them become lions, and wished that afterwards they should always draw her chariot. The seats that stand around this goddess show that, although all other things move, she nevertheless always stands still; or else, because they are empty, they give us to understand that not only houses but cities too are often emptied, both by pestilence and by wars and by other hardships, or that on the earth there are many uninhabited places.

The priests called Corybantes, who stand upright here and armed, are meant to show that not only the cultivators of the earth, but the priests too and those who are set over cities and kingdoms, ought not to sit or remain in idleness, but that each one must take up his arms, one to cultivate the earth, one to pray to the gods, and one to defend the fatherland.

> Image of the goddess Ops, also called Berecyntia, Mother of the Gods, interpreted as the Earth, and of the animals and trees sacred to her, signifying the fruitful cultivation of the ground, and that everyone, however great, is subject to nature. There is also the image of her priests called Corybantes, who show that everyone ought to exert himself virtuously and not stand idle.

The image of the goddess Ops that Varro gives is of this kind. On a chariot drawn by lions is placed a woman who has her head encircled with towers in the manner of a crown; she holds a sceptre in her hand and is clothed in a mantle all laden with branches, grasses and flowers; around her stand some empty seats, and there are also resounding tympana, and certain priests accompany her with helmets on their heads, shields on their arms, and spears in their hands.

<aside>Isidore. Key given to the Great Mother</aside>

Isidore writes that a key was sometimes given to the image of the Great Mother, to show that the earth in the time of winter shuts itself up and hides within itself the seed sown upon it, which, sprouting, comes forth afterwards in the time of Spring, and then the earth is said to open; so Alexander ab Alexandro also reports.

<aside>The Phrygian goddess</aside>
<aside>Berecyntia. Virgil</aside>

The ancients also made wreaths for this goddess, sometimes of oak, because mortals once lived on the acorns she produced, just as they now live on grain and the other fruits which the same earth produces. And sometimes of pine, because this tree was consecrated to her, whether on account of the great abundance of pines in Phrygia, where she was first worshipped and was therefore also called the Phrygian goddess, as though that country were her own native land, where her sacred rites were first celebrated; hence, from Berecyntus, a mountain of that country, she was likewise called Berecyntia; and Virgil so names her when he compares Rome to her, and to a great extent portrays her, saying,

> As Berecyntia, Mother of the Gods,
> crowned with towers, in her chariot
> goes proud through the cities of Phrygia,
> of her divine offspring, from whom a hundred
> grandsons, all dwellers in Heaven,
> she sees about her, and often embraces them.
>
> *(Virgil)*

<aside>Pine given to the Great Mother. Attis and his tale</aside>

Or else the pine was given to this goddess because Attis, a most beautiful youth, once greatly loved by her, was changed on dying into this tree. The fable that is read about it is that the goddess, enamoured of this youth with a pure and chaste love, took him for herself and gave him the care of her sacred things, on condition that he should always keep himself virgin and chaste, as he promised to do; and he bound himself to it with an oath. But the wretch afterwards did not keep it, for, falling in love with a beautiful nymph, daughter of Sangarius, a river of that country, he forgot the promise made to the goddess, and often enjoyed her love.

<aside>Castrated priests</aside>

At this the goddess was so strongly angered that she at once caused the nymph to die, and drove the youth away from herself and from her service. And he, realising the sin he had committed, fell into such a frenzy that he went like a madman running over the high mountains, crying and howling continually, and like one out of his mind tossed his head this way and that, and with the sharpest stones often tore his tender body; and having cut off with these stones also the member that had so offended the goddess, he flung it far from himself; and he would have killed himself outright, had she not at last, moved to pity for him, made him become a pine; and to show that she still kept the memory of the beloved youth, she wished afterwards to be crowned with the branches of this tree. And she ordained that henceforth her priests should be castrated with the sharp stone in the manner in which the wretched youth had castrated himself, and that in her festivals they should go whirling about in the same way, tossing their heads, wounding their arms and shoulders, and shedding their own blood, as he himself did when he ran frenzied over the high mountains.

<aside>Fables of Attis</aside>

And besides the other names they had, these priests were also called Galli, from a river of Phrygia of this name, of whose waters whoever drank went mad at once, and was then fit to serve the goddess, because he boldly did all the follies that I have described. Pausanias writes that in a certain part of Greece there was a temple dedicated to the goddess and to Attis together; and that some said he was killed by a boar sent for this purpose by Jupiter, who took it ill that he was so familiar with the goddess and so loved by her.

<aside>Agdistis</aside>

And then Pausanias tells another fable of him, which is so much a fable that it seems to me to deserve to be recounted. It is this. From seed spilled on the ground by Jupiter (who, perhaps, was dreaming that he was with some beautiful girl) there was born a Genius, or Daemon, whichever we please to call it, in the form of a man, but having both sexes; and it was named Agdistis. The other gods, frightened at this as at a monstrous thing, at once came about it, cut off its male part and flung it away. From this there grew, soon after, a pomegranate tree, of whose fruits the daughter of the river Sangarius, passing that way, filled her lap in order to eat them; but they vanished almost at once, and she was left pregnant, and in due time bore a beautiful baby, which for shame she hid in a certain wood, where a goat always went to give it milk, so that it did not perish. But when he was grown he was named Attis, and was so beautiful that he seemed rather a divine than a human thing; so that the Genius Agdistis fell most ardently in love with him.

It happened that the beautiful youth, sent by his family, went to Pessinus, the principal city of Phrygia, where the king of the country made him his son-in-law, giving him his daughter as wife; and everything was already ready for celebrating the wedding, when Agdistis, who went after the beloved youth, arrived there.

Agdistis, full of anger and rage at seeing that another was to enjoy the thing so loved by him, at once drove into the heads of Attis and of the king his father-in-law, by his enchantments, or however he did it, such a frenzy that both in a fury cut off their genital member with their own hands. But Agdistis afterwards repented of what he had done, because the love he bore Attis had not yet wholly gone; and he begged Jupiter, and obtained it, that the other parts of the body of the beloved youth should never be able to decay or rot.

<aside>What Attis signifies</aside>

And nothing more have I read about this Attis, except that by him the ancients meant those flowers on which no fruit ever follows, and which produce no seed, as Eusebius reports; and so the fables feigned that he castrated himself, as I have said.

But let us return to the Great Mother, who with solemn ceremonies was brought from Phrygia to Rome by men sent there for the purpose, as the Romans had understood from the verses of the Sibyl that it should be done, and that she had to be received by a chaste hand. Whereupon the ship that carried her stopped at the mouth of the Tiber, where almost all Rome had gone to meet her, nor was it possible to move it from there, though many, many strove to pull it up the waters of the river.

<aside>Claudia the Vestal</aside>

Then Claudia, a Vestal virgin, whose chastity many doubted because she went more elegantly adorned, and conversed and spoke more freely than perhaps befitted her, knelt down on the bank of the river, and stretching out her joined hands toward the goddess, said: “You know, kindly goddess, that I am thought not very chaste; if so, I pray you give a sign: condemned by you, I shall confess myself deserving of death; but if it is otherwise, you, who are chaste and pure, bearing witness to my integrity, follow my chaste hand.”

And having said this, she took hold of a small rope and drew the ship wherever she pleased, the goddess showing that she followed her willingly, to the no small amazement of those who saw it. And afterwards there was no one who dared to think ill of Claudia, of whom I have related this because the deed could serve anyone who wished to paint Chastity; although it may be done in many other ways too, as whoever takes on the labour will be able to gather from many images already drawn, and from those that remain to be drawn.

<aside>Ovid</aside>

The idol of this goddess, brought then from Phrygia, was a great black stone, which was worshipped by those peoples under the name of the Mother of the Gods. When it had arrived where the little river Almo enters the Tiber, it was washed there by one of her priests; and placed then upon a chariot drawn by two cows, it was carried into the city with great rejoicing of the people; so the custom was afterwards observed of carrying it every year with solemn pomp in the same manner, and to the same place, to have it washed by her priests, who washed themselves also, and their knives, as is seen in Ovid, where he says:

> There is a place where the little river Almo
> enters the Tiber, and loses its own name;
> there the old priest, adorned
> with purple, with much reverence
> washes in the waters of that little river
> his kindly goddess with her sacred things.
>
> *(Ovid)*

<aside>Prudentius</aside>
<aside>St Augustine</aside>

And at this ceremony many went before the chariot with bare feet, as Prudentius says, and sang the most indecent things they knew how to say of this goddess and of Attis, her beloved. And so St Augustine, condemning those devilish festivals, says that those mad people were not ashamed to cry out before the Mother of the Gods things that their own mothers would have been ashamed to hear. And Herodian writes that people went crying out in that manner then not only of low and plebeian condition, but many nobles also, and men of consequence, who changed their dress so as not to be recognised, and then went about saying and doing all the most indecent things they knew.

> Idol of the goddess Cybele, who is the earth, and the chariot on which it was conducted in procession, drawn by two cows signifying the fertility of the earth and the usefulness that comes from it to mortals.

<aside>Victims of the Great Mother. Ovid</aside>

Many festivals were also observed, many games held, and many ceremonies celebrated in honour of this goddess; but, since they would serve nothing for our purpose, it is better to leave them aside and say rather that, although some have held that the shedding of their own blood, which her priests did, as I said, was to her in place of a sacrifice, it is nevertheless found that the sow too was sacrificed to her, this animal agreeing much, by the numerous offspring that is born of it, with the fertility of the earth. And Ovid says that when she arrived at Rome an untamed heifer was sacrificed to her, the Romans having perhaps learned from the Egyptians that this animal was conformable to the earth, since the Egyptians, as Macrobius reports, wishing to show the earth by their mysterious signs, depicted an ox, or a cow, whichever it might be.

<aside>Cornelius Tacitus. Earth worshipped by the Germans</aside>

In Cornelius Tacitus we read that some peoples of Germania worshipped Mother Earth, as one who, they thought, took part in all the affairs of mortals; but because they had, as I said already, neither temples nor idols, they performed her sacred ceremonies in a wood with a chariot covered all over with cloths, which no one could touch but the priest, as though he alone knew that the goddess was there; and therefore he went beside it with great reverence, having it drawn by two cows, to lead her as if for a stroll about the country.

Then all the days were merry and joyful, there could be no fighting in any way, all the weapons of iron stood shut up and covered, and the country was then all full of peace and quiet, and in every place where the goddess went she was regarded with great respect. But when she was sated with going about, and no longer wished to converse among mortals, they went to wash in a certain lake the chariot that carried her, the garments that covered her, and herself too, as some believed. And the slaves who did this were swallowed up by the same lake, and were never seen again, which increased the religious awe and made the goddess ever more feared.

Her, as the same Tacitus writes, some other peoples of Germania likewise worshipped, again without having any idol of her; but the emblem of their religion was to carry the image of a boar, and this was to them in place of arms, and they thought that, showing themselves in this way to be worshippers of the goddess, they should be safe from all dangers, and from enemies too.

<aside>Medal of Faustina</aside>

I remember having seen on an ancient medal of Faustina the image of the Great Mother, which agrees well with what I drew and explained above; for it is a woman with her head encircled with towers; she sits, and rests with her right arm on the seat, and with her left hand holds a shield set upon her knee, and at each side she has a lion.

<aside>Cybele</aside>
<aside>Diodorus. Pompeius Festus. Cube</aside>

This goddess was then called Cybele from a certain mountain in Phrygia, of which Diodorus Siculus speaks. There was, he says, in ancient times in Phrygia a king named Meon, who had as wife one called Dindyme. A girl having been born to them, and the mother not wishing to rear her, she laid her on Mount Cybele, where she was nourished with the milk of wild beasts. But a young woman who was pasturing a flock thereabouts having come there, and seeing the girl, took her up all amazed, and carried her with her, naming her with the name of the mountain, and so reared her until, grown up, she turned out to be of singular beauty and marvellous intelligence.

For she not only was the first to find the pipe made of reeds joined together, and the cymbal, but also various remedies for the diseases of flocks and of children, for which she deservedly earned the name of Mother. So says Diodorus; but we, with Pompeius Festus, shall say that she was so called from a certain geometrical figure made exactly like a die, called the Cube, which was also consecrated to her by the ancients, to show the steadiness of the Earth; for let a die be thrown, it always comes to rest, on whatever side it may fall.

<aside>Lucretius</aside>

And the image of Cybele is the same as that of the Great Mother, because she too has her head encircled with towers, as Lucretius says speaking of her:

> They girt her lofty head and adorned it
> with a mural crown, to show
> that she upholds cities, towns and castles.
>
> *(Lucretius)*

<aside>Mural crown, to whom it was given</aside>

This sort of crown was given anciently by the imperator to whoever had first mounted by force upon the walls of the enemy. She has the chariot likewise drawn by lions, which shows, according to some, that the earth hangs in the air and is held up by wheels, because the celestial spheres revolve about it continually, as the lions show, fierce and impetuous animals; for such is the nature of Heaven, which surrounds the air that upholds the earth. Hence in Lucretius too we read thus:

> The ancient Greeks, who wrote of her
> in poetry, made her sit
> upon a chariot, to whose yoke go together
> two fierce lions, which shows
> that in the field of the air the great earth
> hangs, standing by itself.
>
> *(Lucretius)*

<aside>Ovid</aside>

It is also said that the lions signify that there is no fierceness so cruel that maternal pity does not overcome it; and therefore Ovid speaks thus of this goddess:

> Through her let fierceness be believed
> to be conquered and made gentle and humble.
> Hence it comes that humbly are yoked
> the proud lions to her beautiful chariot.
>
> *(Ovid)*

<aside>Aristotle</aside>

Not very unlike this is what Aristotle writes, who, telling of the miraculous things of the world, puts it that on Sipylus, a mountain of Phrygia, there grew a certain small stone, long and round, and whoever found it and carried it into the temple of Cybele became most loving toward his father and mother, and obeyed them with all reverence, even though he had before been their enemy and had struck them with impious hands.

Some thought also, as Diodorus reports, that the lions were given to Cybele because she had been nourished and reared by them on Mount Cybele, as has been said, from which they claim she afterwards took her name; for the ancients tell also of many others who were nourished by beasts, as Aesculapius and Cyrus by dogs, Romulus with his brother by wolves, Telephus by deer, Semiramis by birds, and Jupiter by bees with the help of a goat: which, though it seems to have something of the fabulous, has nevertheless been written as history.

<aside>Plato</aside>

Those who write of natural things hold that the elements have among themselves such a community that one easily changes into another, according as it becomes more rare or more dense. Hence Plato said that among them there was the tenfold proportion. Therefore whoever bears this in mind will not wonder to see the gods of the ancients so entangled together, and that one and the same god often shows different things, and that different names sometimes signify one and the same thing.

Thus, for example, Jupiter, although he shows for the most part the element of fire, shows that of air also at times, and Juno likewise is taken for the air, but not so that she does not show the earth also at times; the Sun is one only, and the Moon likewise, and yet each of them has different names; Water too had many gods, and the Earth also, from which, because of the moisture that continually escapes, vapours rise, which, thickening in the lowest part of the air, make the clouds, from which the rains then descend.

<aside>Rhea</aside>

And for this reason Cornutus holds that the Earth is called Rhea, as though she were the cause that the rain descends; or else that he calls Rhea not the Earth, but, be it what it may, the cause of the rains; and he says that to this goddess were given the tympana, the cymbals, the torches and the lamps, because thunders, lightnings and flashes are wont to go before the rains and often to accompany them.

<aside>Vesta</aside>

Some hold that the tympana signify that the Earth contains within herself the winds, and so Alexander understands it, who says that they are given to Vesta too, who was painted as a woman of virginal aspect, because she is the earth, which sits; as Pliny writes that Scopas, an excellent sculptor, made her, and she was much praised in the Servilian gardens, and that she holds a tympanum in her hand. Cornutus says that the ancients used also to represent her as almost entirely round, and so they made her shoulders narrow and drawn together, and crowned her with white flowers; because the earth too is round, and surrounded all about by the whitest element there is, which is the air.

But it is to be noted that there were two Vestas among the ancients, and by the one, who was the mother of Saturn, they understood the earth, of which I have just spoken; by the other, who was the daughter of the same, fire; that is, that life-giving heat which, spread through the bowels of the earth, gives life to all the things that are born from her. Of this one the ancients made no image, because they believed, as Ovid says, that Vesta was nothing else than the pure flame, and they said therefore that she was always a virgin, quite pure and untouched, just as the flame generates nothing from itself and receives no filth or stain at all.

<aside>Livy</aside>
<aside>Gellius</aside>
<aside>Amata, first Vestal virgin</aside>
<aside>Vestals</aside>

And therefore her sacred things were guarded and handled only by the purest young girls, who for that reason were called the Vestal virgins; and these, as may be gathered from Livy, were introduced and ordained by Numa. Gellius reports that the first who entered the service of Vesta was named Amata, and that therefore all the others after her were likewise called Amatas; and they were taken by the high priest not younger than six years, nor older than ten, and it was necessary that they should have no defect of tongue, nor of eyes, nor of ears, nor of any other part of the body, and that neither father nor mother had ever been slaves, nor had followed any sordid and vile office or trade. At first there were only four, and afterwards six, because the city was first divided into six parts; and it was forbidden to men to go where they lodged, except by night [?1].

They were bound to the service for thirty years, in this way: in the first ten they learned the sacred ceremonies and everything that belonged to their office, which was principally to take care that the lighted flame should never go out, because when this happened it was a very bad omen for the Romans, and the virgin who was to blame for it was punished by the Pontiff with harsh beatings; and that sacred fire was then lighted again not from any other material fire, but from the rays of the Sun, as is done with certain concave mirrors; or else, as Festus writes, they beat and rubbed a certain board so long that it gave out fire, which they gathered in certain vessels of metal and put back in the place of the one that had been extinguished. In the second ten years they performed the office, and in the last ten they taught the newly arrived young girls.

After this time they were at liberty to marry, but very few ever did so, because it seemed that, once married, they always came afterwards to a wretched and unhappy end. During the thirty years that they were in the service they had to be wholly chaste and pure, for the Vestal virgin found unchaste was laid alive on a bier, and carried in the manner in which the dead are carried to burial, and the relatives and friends followed her weeping as far as the walls of the City, where there was a great pit like a chamber under the ground, with a bed and a lighted lamp, and with a little bread, water and milk which they put there, so that it might not seem that a consecrated virgin had been made to die of hunger.

Then, after certain secret prayers had been said there, the Pontiff sent the unhappy young woman down a ladder into the underground pit, with his face turned away, and those who were appointed for this at once cast earth upon her and buried her there, where the poor girl died a miserable death for having violated the chastity she had promised; and the day on which this was done was sad and funereal for the whole City. Every year on a fixed day the same Vestals were wont to kindle the fire anew upon the altar, as is still done among us today with the paschal candles.

It is found then that this goddess is often confused with the other Vesta, who was the Earth, among the ancients, when they write of her nature, her temples, her sacrifices and her other ceremonies. Therefore let it be no wonder if I too, in speaking of the one, sometimes say things which will seem proper to the other, since it seldom happens that one speaks or writes of the natures and powers of the earth, which are like her soul, without understanding her also, that is, her whole body.

<aside>Temple of Vesta</aside>

Ovid said, then, that the temple of Vesta in Rome, which had first been the royal house of Numa, was quite round, to represent the globe of the earth, within which the fire is preserved just as it was preserved inextinguishably in that temple. And Festus writes that Numa consecrated to Vesta a round temple, because he believed her to be the earth, which sustains the life of men; and because she is made like a ball, he wished her temple to have the same figure.

<aside>Landino</aside>

And the temple alone was often her image; so Alexander held that by her was understood the divine spirit, which we cannot reach with the eyes of the body, though we see well the things that are about it; and it was made in this way, as Landino draws it in his commentary on Virgil, where Hector in a dream commends Vesta and the other sacred things to Aeneas. It was great, wide and spacious, and in the middle had an altar with the fire lit, and on either side, as its guard, was a virgin, and on the top of the temple was likewise a virgin, who held a little baby in her arms; because the ancients said that Vesta, shown by the virgin, nursed Jupiter, who is the baby.

> Temple in Rome of the goddess Vesta, Mother of the Gods, and of Vesta, goddess of fire and of virginity, signifying that life-giving heat which gives life to things, or the invisible divine spirit, with the two Vestals who keep guard that the perpetual fire may not go out.

<aside>Vestibule</aside>

Moreover, the ancients consecrated to Vesta that place at the very entrance of houses where they made fire, which was therefore called, as Ovid believed, Vestibule. There they also often ate, inviting the gods to their tables, which they then consecrated and used in place of altars, worshipping the gods who had been invited.

<aside>Lares</aside>
<aside>Hearth (*focolare*)</aside>

Since, then, sacrifice was almost never made without fire, and this was shown by Vesta, those places where the fire was most often lit were deservedly consecrated to her, and these were called properly Lares, because there the Lares too were worshipped, who were certain household gods of the home. Hence it seems that it has come down even to our times to say *focolare*, as though *lare* and *foco*, which is the very place where the fire is lit, were one and the same, although the ancients made of the one the god, and of the other the thing consecrated to the god.

Nor is it to be believed that Vesta was taken for fire in general, and for every kind of fire; for according to the different things that are considered in it, so the ancients made of it different gods; but that she was taken for that which is shut up in the bowels of the earth, which is therefore perpetual and never goes out, and gives life to all the things created there.

<aside>Vesta in all sacrifices</aside>

And in all sacrifices, to whatever god they were made, Vesta was invoked before all the others, as I said also of Janus. The reason for this was (besides that which Ovid gives, that the first entrances of houses, where at the beginning they often sacrificed, were consecrated to her, and besides the fable also which says that she obtained from Jupiter, after the victory against the Titans, perpetual virginity and the first-fruits of all sacrifices) that all created things with which the ancients worshipped the gods have being and life from the heat which produces them and makes them be born, which comes from the fire already spoken of. Nor did there seem to be anything that better represented the purity and the never dying of the gods than the pure and lively flame; and therefore a sacrifice was never made without fire, and without Vesta being invoked at the beginning.

Besides her there were afterwards other particular numina worshipped by the ancients for the particular powers that the earth shows in different parts; for, as Virgil has sung and as the authors on agriculture write, in one place grain does better, in another trees produce better; in one the flowery meadows are more cheerful, and in another more abundant in grassy pastures: from which the goddesses Ceres and Proserpina, and the goddess Bona, Flora, Pales, and others took their names, of whom we shall speak afterwards.

<aside>Ceres</aside>
<aside>Virgil</aside>

Let us now speak of Ceres, who was held to be the first to show mortals, who until then had lived on grasses and acorns, how to sow grain, gather it, grind it and make bread of it. Hence Virgil says:

> Ceres was the first who showed
> mortals how to break the ground
> with hard iron, and how to sow it.
>
> *(Virgil)*

<aside>Ovid</aside>

And Ovid sings likewise of her:

> The first to break with the plough
> the hard clods and to scatter the grain
> over them, whereby mortals
> might feed themselves, was Ceres, who together
> with this showed also the holy laws.
>
> *(Ovid)*

<aside>Laws from Ceres</aside>

And therefore she was so revered and worshipped as a goddess, and was believed to have given laws before all others, because after the use of grain was found, men left, together with the acorns, that first life, all rude and almost beastlike, and gathering together made cities, and lived afterwards as citizens. And for this reason it was also said that the numen of Ceres showed the power of that earth which can be cultivated, and which produces grain abundantly.

<aside>Claudian</aside>

Hence her statue was made in the form of a matron with wreaths of ears of corn on her head, and she held a little bunch of poppies in her hand, because this is a sign of fertility, and two fierce dragons drew her chariot, as Orpheus wrote. Hence Claudian, when he describes her returning from Sicily, where she had hidden her daughter, speaks thus:

> She mounts the chariot and toward her mother's home
> directs the flight of the dragons, whose limbs
> she often strikes, and they through the clouds
> wave, twisted, hissing, and the bridle
> they gently lick, which wet the foam
> of friendly venom makes.
> These keep their proud brow covered
> with lofty crests, and have their back
> all strewn with knots and little discs.
> And their long scales, shining,
> seem to throw out sparks of gold and fire.
>
> *(Claudian)*

<aside>Why serpents are given to Ceres</aside>

The dragons are given either because the crops do not rise too high, but seem to go creeping over the ground; or because the sinuous bodies of serpents show the crooked furrows which the oxen make while they plough the earth; or else it was so feigned because, as Hesiod says, in the island of Salamis there was once a serpent of immense size, which laid waste all that country, and, driven out from there by Eurylochus, passed over to Eleusis (and as if for its safety it had fled to Ceres), and there afterwards stayed always in her temple as her minister and servant.

> Image of Sicilian Ceres, inventor and goddess of the crops, and of her chariot drawn by dragons, signifying the fruitful earth and its cultivation, since the crops do not rise much and seem almost to creep, and denoting also the crooked furrows of the ploughed earth.

<aside>Sicily of Ceres</aside>

And that Ceres signifies the level and broad earth that produces grain, her image shows, says Porphyry, as Eusebius reports, being crowned with ears of corn, and having about her some plants of poppy, which shows fertility. For which reason we read also that Sicily was very dear to her, because it is a very fertile country, and she went to law over it with Vulcan, as to which of them should have possession of it; but the sentence was given in her favour.

<aside>Proserpina carried off by Pluto</aside>

From this came perhaps that one statue of hers, which was very large there, as Cicero says speaking against Verres, held on its right hand a small figure of Victory, and this shows the fertility of that island, and hence the fables feigned that Pluto carried off Proserpina, who is often understood as fertility, because there happened perhaps a time when the Sicilian fields gave a poor harvest. Or else, because Proserpina is taken also at times for that hidden power which the seed has of sprouting, it was feigned that Pluto, understanding by him the Sun, carried her off, and took her to Hell; because the heat of the Sun nourishes, and keeps under the earth all the time of winter, the sown grain; and Ceres goes then seeking her with burning torches in her hands, because in the time of summer, when the rays of the Sun burn most, the farmers go seeking the ripe crops, and gather them.

<aside>Marcus Tullius</aside>

And hence it was that, as Pausanias writes, the statue of Ceres made by Praxiteles, as certain letters carved there showed, in a certain temple of hers in the region of Attica, had lighted torches in her hands. And the priests of this goddess likewise went running with lighted torches when they celebrated the Eleusinian festivals, so called from Eleusis, a city not far from Athens, where they were first ordained; in which certain young girls consecrated to the goddess carried little baskets of flowers for the spring, and of ears of corn for the summer; and of these Marcus Tullius also made mention, speaking against Verres.

<aside>Theodoret of Cyrrhus</aside>

The images of these gods were likewise carried in the same ceremonies, as Eusebius reports: that of the Creator, which the Hierophant carried, who was the principal priest; that of the Sun [?2], carried by him who also carried the lighted torch; he who served at the altar carried that of the Moon, and that of Mercury the herald, or trumpeter of the sacrifices; and Theodoret of Cyrrhus writes that in this solemn procession they carried also, as a thing worthy of great reverence, the female sex, just as they carried the male in the ceremonies of Bacchus.

But Sesostris, a most ancient king of Egypt, as we read in Herodotus, on the contrary used it as a vile thing, worthy of contempt. For in the countries which he subdued with great labour, the peoples defending themselves vigorously, he set up high and fine columns with his own name and that of his fatherland, and how he had conquered that country; but where he found no resistance, or little, he set up the same columns with the same letters, but added to them besides the female nature, wishing in that way to show the cowardice and worthlessness of those peoples.

<aside>Eleusinian mysteries</aside>

The ceremonies and sacred things of Ceres were guarded with such religious awe, and kept so secret, that whenever they were celebrated the priest first cried out: “Let all profane men go away, let all wicked persons withdraw from here!” For no one could enter there but one who was, as we should say, ordained to them, and it was necessary that he should be well purged of all wickedness.

<aside>Nero</aside>

Hence we read of Nero that he never dared to be present at these ceremonies, feeling perhaps that he was too wicked and impious. And Antoninus, in testimony of his goodness, wished to be made one of those who took part in the Eleusinian mysteries. Nor shall I pass over in silence this foolish custom, that whoever was admitted to these mysteries put on, on the day he took the order, a fine new shirt, quite clean, and never took it off afterwards until it was all worn out and torn; some say that they even kept those rags to make swaddling bands of them for children while they lay in the cradle.

Besides this, it could not be known what those mysterious things were that were done and kept there; so closely were they held secret that, although they were carried about at certain times by the purest young girls, they did this in certain small baskets or little hampers, very tightly closed and very well covered, and it seemed a great sin to seek to understand the reason of them, and to know what they were.

<aside>Macrobius</aside>

Hence Macrobius recounts of the philosopher Numenius, who, as too curious an investigator of the sacred mysteries, having divulged these things, saw in a dream the goddesses of Eleusis standing like harlots in a public place, exposed to anyone who might wish to take his pleasure of them; at which, being greatly amazed, and having asked the cause of such shamelessness, he was answered by those goddesses, all wrathful, that this had come about through him, who had taken them by force from the hidden secret places and put them in public, in the hands of the vulgar.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

And Pausanias writes that, having resolved to speak at length of the sacred mysteries of the temple of Eleusis, he saw in a dream a certain image that frightened him from it. And therefore he says nothing else of them, except that before the temple there was a statue of Triptolemus, and a bronze cow wreathed with flowers, with gilded horns, as the victims were when they were about to be sacrificed. And Triptolemus must have been a youth upon a chariot drawn by two serpents, which was the chariot of Ceres; for we read that he was sent by her with her chariot through the world to show how the earth was to be cultivated, the grain sown, the crops gathered, and then used.

<aside>Eleusinian goddesses</aside>

By the Eleusinian goddesses are always understood Ceres and Proserpina, who were also called the great goddesses among the Greeks; and those of Arcadia worshipped them above all the others, keeping in a certain temple of theirs the fire always lit with the greatest religious awe, and they made two statues of them, as Pausanias recounts: that of Ceres was all of marble, and of the other, of Proserpina, the upper part, which made the robe, was of wood; and they were fifteen feet in height.

Before these stood two young girls with robes long to the feet, who carried on their heads baskets of flowers, and at the feet of Ceres was Hercules, no larger than a cubit. There were also two Horae, and there was Pan, who played the pipe, and Apollo the cithara, as being two of the principal gods of Arcadia, according to what was written there; and there were then some nymphs, of whom one, a Naiad, had in her arms Jupiter, a little baby, the others were nymphs of Arcadia, and among them one carried before her a torch (I have already said why the torch was given to Ceres), another held two different vessels of water, one in each hand, and two others likewise carried two hydriae, which poured out water.

<aside>Wedding of Ceres</aside>

By this they perhaps showed that in certain sacrifices called the wedding of Ceres they did not use wine, as they did in those of all the other gods; and from this came the jest of that old woman in Plautus, when she saw that they were going to her house to prepare a wedding banquet and were not bringing wine: “Do you perhaps wish,” she said, “to make this wedding for Ceres? for I do not see that you are bringing wine.”

<aside>Why the victims of Jupiter [?3]</aside>
<aside>Pig given to Ceres</aside>

With Ceres the pig may be placed, because the ancients sacrificed it to her as her own proper victim. And the reason for the victims among the ancients, that is, why one animal rather than another was sacrificed to this god and to that, was, as Servius writes, as much the contrariety as the conformity which the beast was believed to have with the god to whom it was sacrificed. And therefore they say that the pig was given to Ceres, as though it pleased her to see dying before her her enemy, which not only spoils the crops already sprung up, but, turning up the sown fields too with its snout, goes to find the grain even under the ground and devours it. And for the same reason they said that the goat was sacrificed to Bacchus, as an animal greatly harmful to the vines.

Some have also held that the sacrifice of the pig was pleasing to Ceres because of the conformity and likeness that is between them. For she is an earthly numen, since by her is understood the earth, and the pig lies wrapped in the earth more than any other animal; and it is for the most part black, as the earth too by its nature is black and dark. Besides this, this beast shows the fertility of the earth, and so the pregnant sow also was sometimes sacrificed to Ceres; for we read that at a single birth it brings forth at times as many as twenty piglets, and thirty had been brought forth by that sow which appeared to Aeneas on the bank of the Tiber, as Virgil sings.

<aside>Hera</aside>

Another idol of Ceres was also in Arcadia: with her right hand she held a torch, and she laid her left upon another idol of a certain goddess, worshipped by the Arcadians more than by all other peoples, and called by them Hera. She was, as some held, daughter of Neptune and of Ceres, although this name Hera, as Pausanias says, was given in Arcadia to Ceres as well, and Juno also among the Greeks was called Hera. The statue of this one sat and held a sceptre on her knees, and a basket.

<aside>Ceres: Erinys</aside>
<aside>Ceres as a mare</aside>

And also in Arcadia, as the same Pausanias writes, Ceres was called Erinys, which means Fury, and the cause of it was this. While Ceres went seeking her daughter carried off by Pluto, Neptune, having fallen in love with her, made every effort to enjoy her, and she, to get him off her back, thinking to be able to deceive him, changed herself into a mare and thrust herself among certain herds of mares; but it is too difficult to deceive one who loves, so that he does not at least perceive the deception.

<aside>Neptune as a horse</aside>

Neptune, then, who perceived this, at once became a horse too, and in that way enjoyed his love, from which was born the horse Arion. Ceres took this so ill that, drawn almost out of herself by anger, she was on the point of going mad because of it, and therefore the Arcadians then gave her the name of Fury. And although she afterwards grew calm, and having washed in a certain river left there all her anger, nevertheless she remained sad for a good while longer.

<aside>Black Ceres</aside>

From this it came about that she was called Black Ceres at a certain cave consecrated to her, also in Arcadia; for there she was clothed in black, partly, they say, for grief at her stolen daughter, partly for the anger she had at the violence done her by Neptune; and having hidden herself in the cave I have mentioned, as though she wished no longer to see the light of Heaven, she stayed there a good long time, because of which the earth no longer produced any fruit, and a great pestilence arose, which moved all the gods to pity; but they could not provide for the human misery, not knowing where Ceres was.

But it happened that the god Pan, wandering, as was his custom, and hunting here and there over those mountains, came to where she was staying all sad; and finding her, at once gave notice of it to Jupiter, who, solicitous for the good of mortals, without delay at all, sent the Parcae to beg her in such a way that she, laying aside all sadness and wholly appeased, came out at last from the cave, and then the earth began to produce its accustomed fruits, the pestilence ceasing at the same time.

<aside>Statue of Ceres</aside>

Of this, that the memory of it might remain, the people of that country consecrated the cave to Ceres, with a wooden statue, which sat upon a rock and was a woman in all the rest, except that she had head and neck with the mane of a horse, about which some serpents and other wild beasts went sporting. A robe long to the ground covered her all, and in one hand she held a dolphin, and in the other a dove.

> Statue of Black Ceres in Arcadia, goddess of the crops changed into a mare, and in that form made pregnant by Neptune, god of the sea, transformed into a horse, of which was born afterwards the horse Arion.

It is found also that in another part of the same country, Arcadia, there were before the temple of the Eleusinian goddess two great stones fitted in such a way that, one upon the other, they joined together very well. When the time came to make the solemn sacrifices, they lifted one off the other, because they found there a certain writing, which declared all that was to be done concerning the sacred ceremonies. This they had the priests read diligently, and having put it back in its place, they put those stones together again. And when those people had to swear about some great matter, they went to make the oath at the joint of those two stones; where on the top of that one there was a certain round cover, which covered there in the stone the effigy of Ceres. This the priest put on his face like a mask on the solemn day of the festival, and in this way, with a few rods which he carried in his hand by a certain custom, he beat the common people.

<aside>Pulses distributed by Ceres</aside>

They say that Ceres once stayed there while she went seeking her daughter, and that to those who lodged her graciously she distributed all the kinds of pulses, except beans, as an impure pulse; nor did Pausanias, who recounts all this, choose to say why beans were an impure pulse, this being perhaps among the mysterious things which it was not lawful to divulge.

<aside>Beans, an impure pulse</aside>

But it might perhaps be said that beans were judged such because they used them in the ceremonies for the dead, it seeming to him who first introduced this that no other grain suited it better, because on the petals of their flowers there seem to be certain letters, which represent weeping, and are a sign of grief and sadness; and for this reason it was said that the souls of the dead often went to hide themselves in beans. Hence the priest of Jupiter could not only not eat them, but not even touch them, nor even name them.

<aside>Pythagoras</aside>

And Pythagoras commanded everyone to abstain from beans, perhaps because there was danger of eating with them the soul of someone, which he perhaps thought was in that little animal that is born from beans; for it was his opinion that souls went as in a circle from one body into another, and passed often from man into beast, as I shall say later at another time more fully. Or else Pythagoras forbade the eating of beans, wishing thereby to give to understand that one must leave aside sad and mournful things, which lead the mind astray from the consideration of virtues and of divine things; or to remind men to take care not to be like the dead while they are still alive; or for whatever other reason he did it, it is enough that he too judged beans a pulse to be guarded against, as Ceres also did, when she would not distribute them together with the other pulses.

<aside>Pales, goddess of shepherds</aside>

But because, as I have already said, the different powers of the earth were shown by the ancients with different numina, that which produces the joyful pastures was understood under the name of Pales, who was therefore a particular goddess of shepherds among the Romans. Of her I have found no statue nor any image; so that instead of painting her I shall tell those few ceremonies that were performed in celebrating her festivals.

<aside>Palilia</aside>

These were called from her name Palilia, or, as some will have it, Parilia, because her sacrifices were made for the lambing (*parto*) of the sheep; and they were held on the very day of the birthday of Rome, which was the 20th of April. In these no victim was killed, as though it were wrong to give death to anyone on the day of the birth of the City; but first the men were purified with fumigations made of the blood of a horse, of the ash of a calf taken from the belly of a cow already offered in certain other sacrifices, and of the ash of bean stalks. Then they purified the flocks with the smoke of sulphur, putting into it also olive, pine torch, savin, laurel and rosemary; then, leaping, they passed through the middle of the flame lit with a little hay, and then offered to the goddess milk, cheese, sapa, some little vessels full of millet, and certain cakes also of millet, all foods customary to shepherds, and with solemn prayers they ended the sacrifice.

<aside>Pomona</aside>
<aside>Ovid</aside>

From this sacrifice that which was made to Pomona, goddess of apples and of the other fruits, which they offered to her when sacrificing, was not different. In Ovid she has the care of gardens and is the wife of Vertumnus, to whom gardens were likewise entrusted, and he gives her in her hand a small sickle for cutting the superfluous branches of fruit trees and for grafting. Hence whoever wished to adorn her image still better might depict her with all those instruments that gardeners use about trees, to which she was believed to give the power of producing ripe fruits, just as Flora made them first flower, and was therefore the goddess of flowers, and not of trees only, but of all plants, and of the green meadows also.

> Image of Pomona, goddess of gardens and wife of Vertumnus: with the sickle in her hand for cutting the fruits, to which she was believed to give ripeness; with the dog beside her, guardian of gardens.

<aside>Flora</aside>

Of Flora's image I shall speak afterwards, when I come to draw Zephyrus, who was her husband, according to the fables; because the histories say that she was a harlot, either the one who gave milk to Romulus and Remus, or else another, who left a great inheritance to the Roman people.

<aside>Tale of Flora</aside>

And of her such a tale is read. A priest of Hercules, finding himself one day walking in his temple, quite idle and carefree, turned to his god and invited him to play dice with him, on this condition, that if the god remained the loser he should give him some sign that he would do for him something worthy of the greatness of Hercules; but if he won, he would have a most beautiful supper made ready for him, and would also bring to him one of the most beautiful women he could find, who would stay a night with him.

Then he began to play, throwing the dice with one hand for himself and with the other for Hercules, and it happened that the god remained the winner; whereupon the priest, according to the pact which he himself had proposed, prepared the due supper with a most finely adorned bed, and having had a most beautiful woman called by name Larentia brought, who secretly and willingly gave her favours to others, shut her in the temple with Hercules, and left her there quite alone that night, as though she were to sup with that god, and afterwards lie with him too.

<aside>Tarrutius</aside>

They say that Hercules showed that he had held her dear, and that therefore he appeared to her and told her that she should show herself easy and pleasing to the first man she should find in the morning, going to the square at dawn, as she did; whereupon a Tarrutius, a very rich man, fell in love with her, and loved her so much that, coming to death, he left her heir to the greater part of his property, so that in a short time she became very rich; and dying afterwards she made the Roman people her heir. The Roman people, as Plutarch says, who recounts all this, therefore held her always in the greatest veneration; but because they were perhaps ashamed to do so much honour to a harlot, they changed her name and called her Flora, and sacred ceremonies were ordained for her, and certain games, which were celebrated by harlots with the greatest wantonness; and the ancients also held at the festivals of this goddess hunts of timid hares and fleet goats, because these are animals often kept in gardens, which were under the care of this goddess, as she herself says in Ovid. These things were done on the 28th of April and on the first day of May, and hence came the custom, observed among us down to the present day, that on the first day of May many places in the cities are wont to be adorned with flowers and with leaves of various kinds.

<aside>Bona Dea</aside>
<aside>Porphyry</aside>

Besides the goddesses already named there was also the Bona Dea, likewise a numen of the earth; for Porphyry holds, as Eusebius reports, that that power of the earth which embraces the scattered seed, and holds it in itself and nourishes it, was understood by the ancients by the Bona Dea; and he says that a sign of this is her statue, which holds out with its hand some green plants, as if just sprouted. And the victim also that was sacrificed to her, which was a pregnant sow, showed that the ancients understood the earth by this goddess; who was called Bona, as I have said already, because infinite goods come to us from the earth.

<aside>Fauna</aside>
<aside>Plutarch</aside>

And she was also called Fauna, because she is favourable (*fauoreuole*) to all the needs of the living; besides many other names which Plutarch gives her, where he recounts what happened when Clodius, in love with Caesar's wife, entered dressed as a woman at the ceremonies of this goddess.

<aside>Ceremonies of the Bona Dea</aside>

We read that she was once a woman of such chastity that she never saw, nor even heard named, any other man than her husband, and was never seen to go out of her room; and so no man could enter her temple, nor be present at her sacrifices, nor at her ceremonies, but they were often performed in the house of the chief Pontiff, or of one of the consuls, or of some praetor, and then all the men left that house, and only women gathered there, who with songs and music passed the whole night, for these festivals were held by night. And the Bona Dea showed such loathing for the male sex that in her ceremonies they covered every figure of a male that had been painted in the house.

In the temple of this goddess there were herbs of almost all kinds, of which the one who had the care of it often gave to many for medicine against various infirmities; and for this reason some have claimed that she was Medea, who would not see men because of the ingratitude Jason had shown her. But the fables tell that this Bona Dea, or Fauna, so called also because she favours (*fauorisce*) the common use of everyone, was daughter of Faunus; who, falling in love with her, sought many times with words to draw her to his desires, but always in vain, she standing nevertheless firm in her chaste thought.

Therefore he turned to use force, and she, defending herself, struck him on the head with a rod of myrtle and thrust him from her; and so the custom was afterwards observed of not bringing myrtle into her temple, and whoever had brought it would have sinned gravely. But not even for this did the enamoured father withdraw from his love, but by deceit sought to make the beloved daughter drunk, thinking to be able afterwards to do with her his pleasure; which, however, did not succeed for him. And in memory of this a vine spread its branches over the head of this goddess; and the wine which they used in her ceremonies they did not call wine, but milk.

Seeing, then, that in so many ways tried by him he had not been able to enjoy his daughter, and desiring it nevertheless ever more each time, Faunus changed himself at last into a serpent, and in that form lay with her; and therefore in her temple snakes often appeared, which neither feared others nor gave others any fear.

<aside>Image of the Bona Dea</aside>

For these reasons the statue of the Bona Dea, to whom at times a sceptre was also put in the left hand, because some believed her equal in authority to Juno, had over her head a branch of vine, and at her side a serpent with a little rod of myrtle.

<aside>Proserpina</aside>

Very like this goddess in power was Proserpina, the ancients having likewise understood by her that power of the earth which preserves the sown grain; and a fable is read of her too, which is almost the same as that which I have just told, reported by Eusebius when he writes of the sacred ceremonies of Ceres celebrated in Egypt. The fable is that Ceres, having borne to Jupiter Proserpina, who was also called by some Pherephatta, and she having grown up, her father who had begotten her fell in love with her, and changed himself into a serpent, to enjoy her with greater convenience, as he did.

And hence it was that the Sabazians, a people of Egypt, wished that, as a mysterious thing, there should always be present at their sacrifices a great serpent, all coiled and wound in on itself. Pherephatta, made pregnant by her father, bore a son in the form of a bull; and so the poets often sing the praises of the serpent, father of the bull.

<aside>Proserpina as the crops</aside>

We read also that Proserpina signifies the crops that are born from the earth, which is Ceres, but not without the temperate heat which Heaven, shown by Jupiter, pours into her; and they are carried off by Pluto, either because at times, when sown, they do not spring up again, and the earth seems to grow sad and sorrowful, because she does not see herself adorned with them, now green and now all white when they are ripe; or because natural heat carries off the sown grain, embraces it and fosters it until the ripening of the new crops. She signifies likewise the Moon at times, and therefore an image of her can be made in all those ways in which the ancients depicted the Moon, as I believe I have already said when I drew her.

<aside>Proserpina with a goose</aside>

Proserpina is also sometimes depicted with a goose in her hand, as Pausanias recounts, writing of Boeotia, that in a certain part of that country, in the wood of Trophonius, a young girl called Hercyna, playing with the daughter of Ceres, Proserpina, let a goose escape from her hand against her will, which went to hide itself in a little cave not far from there, under some stones. Proserpina, running after it at once, found it and took it, lifting the stone under which the goose lay hidden, from which at once living waters sprang forth, which made afterwards the river called Hercyna, along the bank of which was a small temple with the statue of a young girl, who held a goose in her hand, and this was Proserpina, daughter of Ceres.

> Image of the goddess Proserpina, daughter of Ceres, understood as the crops, and image of the Bona Dea, understood as the earth and as that power which preserves the scattered seeds, with the serpent and goose sacred to the said goddesses.

## Doubtful passages

- [?1] “Where they lodged, except by night” (*se non di notte*, scan 0226). As printed, the sentence says that men were forbidden to go where the Vestals lodged except at night; this reads oddly, and I have translated it as it stands, without correcting it.
- [?2] The Italian sentence on the processional images (scan 0232) reads “la quale portaua il Hierofante che era il Sacerdote principale del Sole, portata da colui, che portaua anco la face accesa”. The feminine participle *portata* needs an image as its noun, so “del Sole” is taken as the next item of the list (“that of the Sun, carried by him who also carried the lighted torch”), parallel to “del Creatore”; this agrees with Eusebius, where the torch-bearer represents the Sun. Read literally as printed, the Hierophant would be “the principal priest of the Sun” and the participle would be left without a noun.
- [?3] The margin note “Vittime perche di Gioue” (scan 0234) is odd: the passage beside it is about why particular victims were sacrificed to particular gods (Servius), and Jupiter is not the subject. It is translated as printed (“Why the victims of Jupiter”).
- Scan 0231: *Pessuoli* (printed “Pessuoli corpi de i serpenti”) is taken as a misprint of *flessuosi* and rendered “sinuous”.
- Scan 0236–0237: in the Palilia fumigation the printed *teda* is rendered “pine torch”, following the glossary.
