SECOND PART
OF THE IMAGES OF THE GODS
OF THE INDIANS.

*Addition to Cartari by Lorenzo Pignoria.*

Herodotus, a sensible writer and not such a liar as he is commonly held to be, says of Egypt that it has more marvellous things than any other country, and that in it, above any other part of the world, there are to be seen works that the pen of writers cannot reach. And truly this remark of Herodotus cannot be called a hyperbole, since the pages, both sacred and profane, are full of the greatness, the strength and the riches of that very great and most noble kingdom. Nor was the glory of its ancient kings small in conquests and in carrying victorious arms against peoples both neighbouring and very distant.

For we read of Sesostris that he subdued Ethiopia, Scythia, Thrace, the Colchians and a good part of Asia Minor, and of Amasis that he made the island of Cyprus tributary. In earlier times (as is gathered from Diodorus Siculus) Osiris travelled through the world, from the desert borders of India to the sources of the Ister and to the sight of the Ocean; and of another king Manetho writes that he subjected the Phoenicians, the Medes and the Assyrians to his crown. And of others on the same subject many other things are read.

Now, if with their dominion their religion too passed to the conquered peoples, it seems to me nonsense to doubt it. Herodotus relates that the people of Colchis received circumcision from the Egyptians in this way, and in the same way the people of Phoenicia and of Syria; which, although it may not be very true (since of the Hebrews in particular we know what we must believe), nevertheless has much that is reasonable, since it is the custom of the conquered to adapt themselves to the customs, usages and rites of the conquerors. And who knows whether the cult of Isis among the Suebi in Germany, noted also by Tacitus, did not have its origin from here? All the more so because the idol of her among those peoples, made in the shape of a frigate, showed some trace of the daring voyages of the bold sailors of Egypt.

The Egyptians did not leave in peace those peoples either which, discovered and subdued within the memory of our fathers by the most valiant Portuguese nation, are included under the general name of the East Indies; for Herodotus writes, again, that Sesostris conquered the peoples around the sea that we now call Red (with a fleet of large galleys, we should say), and that, penetrating still further, he found a sea full of shoals and consequently not navigable, so that he was forced to turn back.

The Ptolemies went further, encouraged perhaps by some discovery of the earlier kings; for Philadelphus, eager to learn and to see new things, as Strabo too calls him, sent a certain Dionysius to explore the Indies, and he afterwards wrote books and reports about them. And Cornelius Nepos relates that a certain Eudoxus, fleeing from King Lathyrus, set out from the Arabian Gulf, today the Sea of Mecca or Red Sea, and wandered so long that he arrived at Cadiz: an undertaking that shows commerce and knowledge of the country.

And perhaps this Eudoxus is the same who in the time of Ptolemy Euergetes sailed to India and to many other places then unknown, as Strabo relates on the testimony of Heraclides Ponticus, though he laughs at it, for certain arguments of his that are not very solid, to tell the truth. But it was a universal heresy of the ancient geographers to hold as fables all the accounts of the new world. And they transmitted this vain opinion to men's minds with such force that even our grandfathers laughed at Marco Polo, to whom in mockery they attached the surname Millione. And Christopher Columbus for the same reason was for a long time reckoned mad. And in Vicenza at Carnival the companies put on masks and told nonsense in imitation of Antonio Pigafetta, who in the year 1522 went over to the Indies with Magellan.

The Romans, masters of Egypt, continued this voyage, for Strabo too makes mention of the Indian tribute, which in his time passed through Coptos, a city of Egypt. And Solinus relates a fine particular: that under the rule of Claudius a freedman of Annius Plocamus, who was collector of customs on the Red Sea, was going to Arabia and, carried off by the force of the wind, made land after fifteen days on the island of Taprobane. There, after six months, having learned the language of the country and having been brought before the king, he afterwards told many things that he had seen and noted; notable among them was the astonishment of that king that in the Roman coinage, struck with different faces, he nevertheless observed the same and uniform weight.

Pliny relates the same things. On this I must say that I cannot see on what the astonishment of that barbarian was based, since among many Roman denarii with the Balance in hand I have found very few that were of the same weight (and I have weighed and handled more than one). But to our business.

Solinus, in confirmation of what we have said, records the voyage that began in his time at Alexandria for India; and of this description of Solinus some trace is still seen in the ancient Itinerary Table published by Abraham Ortelius at the request of the most noble Signor Marcus Welser, a gentleman of the rarest qualities, to whom men of letters owe more than this alone. Arrian, nevertheless, denies that anyone ever came to discover the Ocean completely by the route of the Red Sea; but I believe him little, having on my side the testimonies written above, all the more because he confines himself to the times of Ptolemy the First, son of Lagus, and of Alexander the Great. He concedes, however, that Hanno the Carthaginian dared to pass the Pillars of Hercules and sailed for thirty-five days toward the east, but that, turning to the south, overcome by thirst and heat, he returned back.

Now whether the Egyptians had knowledge of the West Indies or not, there is much to doubt; nevertheless Benito Arias Montano, in his *Apparatus to the Royal Bible*, holds that the land of Ophir named in the Books of Kings and in the Paralipomena was Peru and New Spain; and perhaps he is not far from the truth; for if the Hebrews and those of Tyre [?1] had knowledge of it, it would be nonsense to say that the Egyptians did not have it, since Solomon's fleet for that voyage was fitted out, as the Scripture says, in the port of Ezion-geber, near Ailath, on the shore of the Red Sea, which may be said to be in the Egyptians' house.

I know well that Gaspar Barreiros, a Portuguese, and Cornelis Wytfliet of Louvain have tried to prove that the land of Ophir was Malacca; but I also know that Abraham Ortelius, a man of that exquisite knowledge of geographical matters which the world knows, has embraced the opinion of Arias Montano and rejected that of Barreiros, and with Ortelius other authors also hold. But leaving authorities aside, I wish to avail myself on this point of a conjecture not at all weak, and it is that the peoples of this part of the world have conformed so closely, in the making of their idols, to the images of the Egyptian deities that they could not be more alike.

And I reason that before the Egyptians the inhabitants of these countries worshipped the Sun and the Moon and the host of heaven, as the Scripture says, which was the most ancient kind of idolatry ever seen in the world; and of this the memory was still kept here, nor had their successors forgotten it, but rather had formed from it a mixture that lasted until the introduction of the Gospel. But to give some beginning to this curious discourse, I shall give here the portrait of Homoyoca, god of Mexico, who among that wretched paganism was their Jupiter.

This meant in that language as much as the Creator of the whole, or the first cause, and they also called him Hometeutle, as if lord of three dignities, or lord three, Eli olomies. They called the dwelling of this god of theirs Narihnepaniuhca, which meant above the nine compositions, or by another name Homeiocam, that is, place of the triune lord. And he, according to the opinion of their wise men, begot with the word Cipatoual, and a woman called Xumoco, who are the two that were before the Flood, and who afterwards begot Tocatiutle. And from this it is plainly seen how true is what St Paul writes, that the invisible things of God are understood by man quite often by means of those that are seen; since in the midst of this barbarism there still shone a little light of nine higher causes, which we call heavens, and, besides, of the first cause, in which they obscurely foreshadowed, in their own way, the ineffable mystery of the Most Holy Trinity.

Now as for this Homoyoca, in his garments and in his posture I should say that he was taken little less than bodily from the Egyptians, among whom Osiris was figured in such a manner, as can be seen, and as I noted already on the most ancient Isiac Table of His Most Serene Highness the Duke of Mantua, on whose border a similar image is seen seventeen times, varied, however, in the ornaments.

Another image of Homopoca [?2], or of a similar deity, has come into my hands, which, however, others call that of Quetzalcoatl. And it was obtained from certain sheets that belonged to Philip Vinghernio of Tournai, a most learned young man, and he asserted that he had copied it from a large book that is in the Vatican Library, compiled by Friar Pedro de los Ríos.

The Mexicans had besides this the god Miquitlantecatle, which meant the Lord of Hell, by another name Tzitzimitl, the same as Lucifer; and this one, with some others of the same class, had the right leg drawn up and the left stretched out, with arms and hands stretched out and open. The god Yzpuzteque, that is, the lame devil, who appeared to them along the roads with the feet of a cock; the same as Satan. The god Nextepeua, the scatterer of ashes. The god Contemoque, so called because he plummeted from heaven head first, whom we should call a devil. To each of these they assigned his wife, who will be either the four Parcae of the poets, or the three Furies of the same together with Proserpina, or Persephone, however they call her.

And since we have entered into the pseudo-theology of this barbarism, it will not be out of place to show how the Demon, the Ape of God, went on gaining advantage by imitating the most notable action that ever came from divine hands, I mean the Redemption of the human race. These people represented in a painting an ambassador of the god Citlallatonac (so they called the Milky Way), sent to a virgin who dwelt in Tulan, called by name Chimalman, that is, Little Shield, to whom the ambassador said that God wished her to conceive a son; who was conceived without the joining of a man, and was called Quetzalcoatl; so that this ambassador was the Gabriel of these wretched people (if it is lawful to say so); and thus Satan is transformed into an angel of light.

On this subject the Most Illustrious Cardinal Baronio notes, on the authority of Tertullian, that the Demon in paganism had imitated Baptism, chrismation, and even the most holy sacrifice of the Mass; he had counterfeited the Supreme Pontiff, the state of virgins, the state of the continent. And elsewhere I have noticed some other notable thing in a similar matter.

This is the portrait of the aforesaid ambassador, in which I have noted with some astonishment the ornament of the head, which is very like those scrolls that the Egyptians planted on the head of their Harpocrates, as can be seen in the statue that I have by me, recorded above on fol. 335.

This Quetzalcoatl was also called Topilczin, that is, my much-loved son, and they say that he was born with the use of reason and that he was the first who began to invoke the gods and to offer them sacrifices with his own blood, which he drew from his person with thorns and in other ways. The paganism of our world already had the Bellonarii, the Galli of the Mother of the Gods, and others of the kind who shed blood; but this one was perhaps more ancient, though a disciple of the same school. They called him the god of the wind, and therefore Motezuma, at the arrival of the ships of Hernando Cortés, spread the word that in that fleet the god Quetzalcoatl was coming, so that the common people should not have occasion to riot; and his temples were round, since he was the inventor of them. These temples were called in their language Ques, and were houses of prayer of four kinds: in the first the lords and the more noble of the people fasted; in the second the common people; in the third whoever was there never raised his eye from the ground; into the fourth sinners and men of ill conduct were sent.

The Mexicans attributed to him, as we have said, and to their own industry, the manner of the lofty temples that were in this country. For where their rule and polity had not reached, the country people used altars made of earth in woods or on the peaks of mountains, which were precisely the groves and high places of sacred Scripture. Whoever wishes to see more about Quetzalcoatl, let him read Francisco López de Gómara in the *Conquest* of Cortés; and although this author differs in some things from what I relate, nevertheless what I say I have from a good source, as I shall say further below. Nor do I intend, however, to coerce the reader, but leave to everyone free belief and opinion, let it be said once for all.

And indeed this superstition took such deep roots that the heathens, even though they had temples most noble in riches and in construction, nevertheless retained most obstinately the woods and the peaks of mountains, where the horror and the site invited the superstitious to the worship of their false deities. Evander in Virgil:

> In this wood, and there where this mountain
> is more leafy, a god (it is not known which)
> but certainly a god dwells.
> *(Virgil)*

Pomponius Mela relates that in Ethiopia a certain peak of the mountains was for this reason called the chariot of the gods. The summits of the mountains Haemus, Olympus, Athos, Ida were held in great esteem among the pagans for the same reason. And Theodoret makes a fine point on this subject, that where formerly the abomination had flourished on the heights of mountains, the Christians had introduced choirs of monks, who in the very encampments of the enemy had planted the victorious ensign of the Cross and the triumph of the Crucified. This is just what the glorious patriarch of the western monks, St Benedict, did: he, as St Gregory relates, destroyed and ruined on the top of Monte Cassino the temple of Apolline, and burned the woods which round about with their thickness hid (so to speak) and cloaked the follies of the pagans. But let us return to Quetzalcoatl, whose image was figured in this manner.

In the ornaments of this figure I note four things worthy, in my judgment, of being observed in the matter we are treating. The first is that apex in the shape of a meta that it has on its head, a shape of which the Demon made use both in the ceremonies of Cybele and in the temple of Venus at Paphos, and perhaps the lapis manalis, which they used in time of drought to obtain rain from heaven, was not of a very dissimilar shape. The second is the lituus that it holds in its right hand, given by the pagans to their augurs and held in such great repute. The third is the cornucopia, which is seen before its feet, and which will have been held among these peoples undoubtedly in greater esteem than the fabulous one, either of Achelous or of the goat Amalthea. The fourth, more notable and more worthy of regard than the others, is the figure of the Cross, which is seen in three places, two on the cloak, and one on the body, by the Censer, as the natives called what we have named the cornucopia.

That this is a Cross I do not doubt at all, and this the more because it is seen that our Lord God, in His mercy, made a broad road for the preparation of the Gospel in some of these countries. In Acuzamil a cross was revered beyond measure by those idolaters, particularly in droughts and when the sown fields had need of water; and Wytfliet relates it in his account of Yucatan, who adds, on the testimony of Peter Martyr d'Anghiera, a Milanese, that the natives told how this rite had been left on that island by a man more shining than the Sun, who died on a Cross and passed that way in the time of their ancestors.

It is indeed true that I do not find these things in the rest of Peter Martyr, since he says in his *Oceanic Decades*, printed at Basel, that the Cozumelans were circumcised, and told that they had received circumcision from a certain man who passed that way many years ago, and so on. Besides this, the same Wytfliet relates on the word of Gómara that the Cumanans, who were discovered some time ago near Peru, not far from the sea, honoured the Cross of St Andrew, and crossed themselves against the apparitions of daemons and put the Cross upon their little children as soon as they were born. Many other things that served to smooth the road to the Gospel are related by Botero, who has studiously collected them.

Be that as it may, these crosses of Topilczin are notable, and worthy of being observed by him who has lately composed a very thick volume on the Cross, in our language. And on the subject it comes to my mind to note that a very similar one is seen on a very rare medal of Constantine the Great, not published or noticed by anyone, so far as I know, up to this hour, of which I have set down the drawing, since I have it by me.

I value this medal not a little, since it seems that many have agreed in believing that to Constantine there appeared in the air the sign of the Cross (as all the ecclesiastical historians of those times write) in the two first letters of the name of CHRIST written in Greek, such as the following emperors afterwards carried on the principal standard of the army. And truly the conjecture is not unreasonable, both for the rarity of the medals of Constantine with the Cross, and for the testimony of Constantius his son, who had money struck, as here below, with the motto "By this sign you will be victor".

All the images registered above, with the principal information about them, increased however by me with some historical comparison and with parallels with the ancient superstitions of other peoples, I have had from the Most Illustrious Signor Ottaviano Malipiero, a most grave senator and of the most amiable placidity of nature. They belonged, as far as I have heard, to Cardinal Amulio of glorious memory, and I value them far more than certain other narratives of men little versed, which go about and are read all day long.

I go on confirming all this conjecture of mine, that the religion of these countries conforms to the Egyptian, with what Francisco López de Gómara writes, namely that the Mexicans expressed the conceptions of their mind with figures similar to the hieroglyphs of Egypt. In conformity Peter Martyr writes that the characters of their writings are dice, hooks, snares, files, stars, and the like, laid out in lines in our manner, and that they imitate the ancient letters of Egypt. And I remember seeing in the sheets of Cardinal Amulio such paintings with their explanations; for example they depicted a deer for the ungrateful man; a stone with a dry ear of maize on it for sterility; a lizard for abundance of water; a stalk of green maize for abundance.

I add that the same Gómara writes that in Mexico over the chapel of some of their principal idols they kept the statue of a certain person, whom he does not name, composed of all the kinds of seeds that were in use in the country; of gold, of jewels, of garments and the like, kneaded and massed together. This made me recall the making of the idol of Serapis among the Egyptians, related by Clement of Alexandria, in which were put to use fragments of gold, silver, copper, iron, lead, marble and various jewels. Likewise the keeping of the corpses of the dead, both of great men and of ancestors, out of veneration, as Peter Martyr relates in many places, is it not an Egyptian custom? And because from the Gallery of His Most Serene Highness of Bavaria I have had some drawings of idols of Mexico, they will be recorded here below one after the other.

This first one, in the arrangement of the head, is very like the Egyptian extravagances; indeed that tail which comes out of his chin makes him in everything and altogether equal, in this part, to that figure of the Isiac Table which I, in the explanation of it, called Horus at another time. And something of this kind is seen on a most ancient carnelian of mine, the drawing of which I have had represented on the little tablet placed above. The other idol, I should say, was taken from the cercopithecus of Egypt, since it has more the figure of a beast than of a man.

To the one and the other of the said idols in the aforesaid Gallery there has been affixed a brief statement in the Spanish language of this tenor: Idol worshipped in the City of Mexico, which was sent from the Indies to Cardinal Francisco Ximénez, Archbishop of Toledo and Founder of the University of Alcalá de Henares; with authentic testimony that the Demon was accustomed to speak through it very often. And these two portraits (to give praise where it is due) were obtained by means of the most noble Signor Johann Georg Herwart, privy councillor of that Highness, a man of singular learning.

And in sum, throughout all this which they call the new world, in the West as well as in the East, I have noticed so great a conformity between the Egyptian superstitions and those of the country that I have had to marvel at times. A Father of Jesus writes, as early as 1553, from Goa, that he observed a pagoda in those countries in which was seen a statue with three heads, three legs, three hands, and that it was called the Pagoda of the Elephant; and in 1560 Father Luís Fróis relates that an idol in the country of Goa, called by the name Ganissone, has likewise the head of an elephant; and he relates the reason why in this way.

They told (he says) that when Adam and Eve had been joined in marriage, and had not yet received children, it came about that Adam needed to go out of the house for a certain business; now Eve, attending to some manual chore of hers, began to sweat, and as it came out of her in abundance she set about wiping it off with her hand, both from her head and from her arms, and it had not finished running before this sweat turned in her hand into a man of perfect stature. Adam, returning home and growing jealous at seeing with his wife another man, whom he did not know who he was, laid hand to a sword and killed his son; but repenting afterwards, and learning the fact from Eve, he cut off the head of an elephant and grafted it onto the corpse of his son; and so he came to life, and in that shape was afterwards canonised; and the fable in any case is fine; and to some it will perhaps seem that they have dreamed at other times similar happenings, but not so deliberately absurd.

And these compositions of man and beast belong to no other religion than that of Egypt, as can be seen in the antiquities of that country. In Japan (or, if we prefer, Giapan) the deformities were no less. The aforesaid Father writes, in 1565, that near the city of Meaco, in a certain temple, were seen a thousand images of Canone, son of Amida (Amida was a foreign god, not of the country, introduced there by Xaca, a Chinese, a famous charlatan). These images were well made, of noble countenance, with a multiplicity of arms and hands, and with certain other monstrosities, as is seen in the figure below.

And how close these two figures come to the thoughts of the Egyptians there is no need to prove. In the same city of Meaco there was seen at one time a statue of Amida with pierced ears, half naked, and he sat upon a great rose, as elsewhere the statue of Xaca, made of metal, set between his sons, the one called Canone, the other Xixi, likewise seated on a broad and lovely rose. A similar posture the Egyptians gave to Sigalion, or Harpocrates, their god, as is seen in an ancient jasper which I have.

Of the same Amida, too, not far from Meaco, there was seen a great statue in the habit of a Brahman, with pierced ears, with chin and head shaved; above the statue there hung from the roof, in the manner of an umbrella, many little bells attached to chains. Around it some armed soldiers, and other figures of Moors, of a witch, and of the ugliest devils, made as it were morris dances; besides, there was seen the image of the wind and of thunder, formed in horrible shape.

And because even there the Demon had introduced academies and students, in a temple built for the purpose of examining and conferring degrees on those who deserved it, there was to be seen the figure of the god of letters and of erudition, which was the lizard or green lizard. Of this there was seen neither statue nor altars, but the figure alone on the ceiling of the temple, made in a ring and in round form, as the Egyptians represented the year by the serpent.

The already named Philip Vinghernio [?3], in a certain sheet of his, once drew the temples of some Japanese deities, situated on some high crags, and related that he had copied them from the pictures [?4] that the Japanese ambassadors brought to give as a gift to Pope Gregory XIII.

But while I went about seeking and finding out everything that could be of use to my purpose, in order to enrich this discourse of mine, there came before me, through the diligence of Signor Girolamo Aleandro the Younger — a living and perfect image of the great Cardinal Girolamo Aleandro — the drawing of a Japanese idol taken from the original, which is kept in Rome by the Fathers of Jesus, and it was that same one that gave the answers, or oracles, to the pagans. The name is Maloco or Malocho, of which I do not remember having read anything among those who have touched on the affairs of that country.

Of this idol I should not know what to say, except that it seems that the Demon has made use of the manner of our images to imprint his deceits on the minds of the pagans of those countries. This idol was exactly of the size that is here portrayed, all of gilded wood, except the crown, which is of copper coloured like gold; and of copper too are the infulae (so to speak) that hang from the little cap. The little cap is of wood, but coloured blue. And this image, as I have said, made me marvel not a little by the composure that is seen in it, of reverence, and some kind of devotion. But I was more astonished afterwards, when through the exact diligence of the same Signor Aleandro there came into my hands fourteen little idols of the same country, which are here below recorded in order.

Their circumstances will be told to us by the one who sent them, who writes to me thus:

This idol is bald, with a face laughing in such a way that it shows the teeth, its arm and right shoulder are bare, the hands are crossed, its flesh colour is the ordinary colour of flesh, the toga, or outer garment, is of gold studded with red points, and it appears to be lined with green, the tunic, or garment beneath, is of tawny or roan colour, embroidered with gold. The circle that it has about its head is of copper wire, as also have the 4th, the 5th and the 9th.

It has the flesh colour very red, painted with cinnabar, that is the face, the neck, and the hands. The little cap is ringed with white, and the rest tawny streaked with gold; the bands that hang from it are of gilded copper, as also the circle that it has behind the head. The armour in which it is clad is all of gold, and the garment beneath is green; the stockings are blue. The monster, or whatever it is, under its feet is of the same red flesh colour with a little white mane.

Of dwarf stature, the face and the hands blue in colour, the cap black; the dress green bordered with gold, the rose on the breast is of gold; the hammer, or whatever it is, that it holds in the right hand is of gold. The sack that it holds over the left shoulder is white; the shoes are black. What it stands upon looks like two little barrels, and they are yellow streaked with black.

It has the face of the usual flesh colour; the hat on the upper part is blue, on the turned-back brims it is gilded; the garment beneath, which covers the breast, is gilded; the outer garment is blue, but lined with white edged with red, as is seen in the part marked A. The cushion on which it is placed is likewise white, bordered with red.

Similar to the first, except that it has a face rather weeping than cheerful. The left part of the outer garment, which covers the body in front as well, is of gold, the garment beneath is tawny. And it must be noted that the wide sleeves belong to the garment beneath. This one too is bald and has the hands not crossed comb-fashion, but joined straight, in the manner of suppliants.

The whole flesh colour is blue, the hair and the eyebrows gold, the small ornament that it wears on the head is gold on the upper part, white on the lower, the garment is gold lined with green and with red, the two implements that it holds in both hands are of copper.

The face and the hands of ordinary flesh colour, it has a black little cap, the garment all black; but it appears to be lined with red, the implement that it holds in the right hand is of gilded copper, the cushion, or whatever it is, on which it rests, is the colour of peach blossom, but spotted with white.

The whole flesh colour is very red, painted with cinnabar, it has six arms and six hands, in which the implements that it holds are of gilded copper; those that it had in the upper left hand are lacking. Of the same copper are also the two infulae that hang from its head, and the whole oval that is behind it. The helmet that it has on its head in the form of a lion's head is gilded, the sash that goes from the left shoulder to the right hip is green; the rest of the garment is gilded with red and blue points. The vessel in which it sits is painted with cinnabar, but the foot is gilded.

The flesh colour is very white, and in place of hair it has only a very slight tint of copper green. The garments are in everything similar to those of the 4th.

This one too has the flesh very white, as if of plaster, and of the same colour is the child that it holds in its arms. The ornament of the head is all black, but the hanging infulae are of gilded copper, as is also the circle that it has around its head; and so it is with the next two idols. The garments are of various colours, the one that covers its shoulders is green, the outer garment blue, the garment beneath, which does not reach the feet, is gilded, the one that reaches the feet is the colour of peach blossom, the shoes are red.

The flesh colour is blue, the armour is of gold with various designs of black lines. The outer garment, which is seen behind the shoulders, is red, but lined with green, and part of the lining is what hangs in front, marked B. The breeches that reach the foot are gilded. The hat is red, but the various ornaments outlined with ink are of gilded copper, as also the implements that it holds in the hands, and those "flat roundnesses" (as Apuleius would have said) that hang from the garments, which are seen also on the two following statuettes. The monster likewise that it has under its feet has blue flesh, the part of the garment that covers its head is tawny in colour, the other is white.

Very like the one just described, except that it has the ordinary colour of flesh, both itself and the monster that it keeps under its feet, although it inclines much to red. The armour is likewise of gold, but the garment is blue lined with tawny, and the hat is tawny. The implement that it had in the left hand is lost, and that of the right hand is half broken.

This one too is similar to the two aforesaid; but its flesh colour is ours [?5]; what is under the feet is green. The garment that hangs from the armour is tawny, as also the little cap that it has on its head.

This whole statuette, both in the flesh colour and in the garments and in the vessel on which it rests, is gilded, and so is the saddle, or whatever it is, of the animal that carries it. The little cap is blue; but the two wings and the hanging infulae are of gilded copper, as also the implements that it holds in its hand. The animal is blue in colour, but the belly and the feet are the colour of human flesh. The mouth is red, the eyelashes, the feathers that are attached above the feet, and a certain foliage that hangs from its head, are green, as is also the tail.

In all these I seem to see great diversity; in some the spirit of the Egyptians and of the Orientals, in some things of our own making. And perhaps this is what he means who wrote a Relation of Japan in the Latin language, printed at Louvain in 1566, in which it is read that the Japanese had images of saints, male and female, with diadems in our manner. And besides, that they used to depict a woman with a little child in her arms, called Quaneuoa, to whom, as to a common advocate, the country people were accustomed to have recourse in their needs. And of these images the following will be: the first, the fourth, the eighth, the ninth, the tenth.

The author of the Relation holds that formerly the Japanese had knowledge of the Christian law; and it is a very probable thought; but that idolatry afterwards obscured this light, of which some vestige remained in these statues. But if we wished to reduce these things too to our first thought, we should not lack for something to say. For as to the diadem, what we have said in the exposition of the Isiac Table suffices, and what Signor Girolamo Aleandro has touched on in a most learned little Latin commentary of his, not printed, and the woman with the child is so like Isis with Horus in her arms that nothing could be more so. The second, then, the seventh, the eleventh, the twelfth, the thirteenth and the fourteenth serve our purpose marvellously.

A little idol of the Indies, of ivory, furnished with jewels, is kept among his many precious curiosities by Signor de Peiresc, whom I have so many times named and never praised enough. I have had it represented here on four sides. What it may signify, perhaps Time would tell [?6] us, the best revealer of all hidden things.

THE END.

- The text runs as one continuous discourse, without internal headings or marginalia; the paragraph division follows the Russian file (rule 9).
