**IMAGES OF THE GODS OF THE ANCIENTS,**
collected by Messer Vincenzo Cartari of Reggio.

<aside>Religion the chief perfection of men</aside>

Of all the perfections given to human nature there is none greater, nor more proper to man, than Religion; and so there has never been any people that did not take part in it in some way. And although it is said that reason chiefly makes man different from the brute animals, nevertheless it is seen that religion shows itself in him even before he makes use of reason, as though it naturally accompanied the human soul. So said Iamblichus, the Platonic philosopher, who holds that a certain divine light comes to strike our souls and awakens in them a natural appetite for the good, about which one then reasons and forms judgements.

This some have put under the fable of Prometheus, as though that divine fire with which he gave life to the first man were continually drawing to itself, by certain hidden ways, human souls, and these likewise, sensing where they have come from and from whom they had their first origin, turned naturally towards it.

And from this too, they say, it comes about that when some great matter presents itself, of good or of evil, man at once, before giving it any other thought, lifts his eyes to Heaven, and often his hands joined together as well, as if he naturally felt that every good comes from up there, and so wished to render thanks and praise to the one who sends it, and that help against every evil is likewise to be expected from there, and so asked for it humbly in that manner. All these are effects of religion, which makes one love and fear God, and this cannot be done without having some knowledge of Him.

<aside>Plutarch in the dialogue called Gryllus</aside>

Therefore, even before the discourse of reason, man in a certain way knows and reveres God, and this makes him different from the beasts. Some have indeed claimed that there is something rational in them, but no one has ever been found who gave them any light of religion. And so religion belongs wholly and solely to men, and, guided by it, they have raised their eyes to Heaven and, considering the marvellous arrangement of the universe, have said that there is One who with infinite love and power, and with supreme providence, orders all things, governs them and has continual care of them. And He was called God, because He is the giver of all goods, eternal, infinite and invisible.

But not everyone held to this truth always, because men, beginning to yield to their own weakness and to take too much delight in it, looked no further than what they saw with the eyes of the body; and from this they took occasion to believe that the stars, the sun, the moon and heaven itself were gods, as Plato writes, who says that these were the first to be worshipped, by the Greeks and, even before them, by many barbarian nations; and he holds that they called them gods from the continual movement they saw them make, deriving the name from a certain Greek word.

This deception then grew in such a way that many men too were judged to be gods, and some animals likewise were worshipped as gods, and to all of them various images were set up, as was also done not only for the virtues but for the vices as well, giving each of them the name of a god and of a numen: to the former so that they should always be present and be of help, to the latter so that they should not do harm and should keep far away.

<aside>Multitude of gods</aside>

Hence the multitude of gods among the ancients was almost infinite; for not only nations, but every city, every place, every house and every person made them for himself in his own fashion, and there was hardly any human action from which some god was not named. Nor was this multitude of gods among the ancients found only in the common people, but among those too who were reckoned to know a great deal.

For these, besides a certain first and sole good, which they said was the cause of all things, then set a nearly infinite number of other beings, whom they also worshipped, and some of them they called gods, others demons, others heroes, and to all of them they gave their own appropriate offices and distinct places, just as the manner of sacrificing to the one and to the others was also distinct.

<aside>Herodotus: the twelve principal gods</aside>

Herodotus writes that the Egyptians named only twelve gods at the beginning; and the Pythagoreans seem to have imitated them, because it is read that the Greeks took these things, and the other sciences too, from Egypt, where stood the so celebrated columns of Mercury, all full of profound learning, and above all about the things of Heaven, marked with various figures of animals, plants and other things, which were formerly for the Egyptians in place of letters; and they were explained by the priests, who there were very learned in them, to anyone judged worthy of it, as were Pythagoras, Plato, Democritus, Eudoxus and others, who went to Egypt for this purpose.


<aside>Pythagoreans</aside>

The Pythagoreans, then, said that as in the first sphere there are twelve figures of animals, which are the twelve signs of the Zodiac, so there are as many souls there, each sign having its own, which give them life and movement; and these are the twelve gods: Jupiter, Juno, Neptune, Vesta, Phoebus, Venus, Mars, Pallas, Mercury, Diana, Vulcan and Ceres; from whom they held that the government of the things down here came.

<aside>The Consentes gods</aside>

These same gods were likewise set by the Romans divided into six males and six females, called Consentes, because they were counsellors of the celestial Senate and nothing is decided without them, as is seen in Homer and the other poets, that when there was a matter of some weight, Jupiter had the council called to deliberate on it, although he also often deliberated and acted by himself alone, as the poets themselves have likewise written; and Seneca, where he discusses the nature of lightning, says that there is one kind which Jupiter hurled upon mortals of his own accord and without the counsel of the other gods.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>
<aside>St Augustine</aside>

Nor did all the gods of the ancients dwell in one place, nor did they all stay in Heaven, but the earth, and the waters of the rivers and of the sea and of the underworld had their share; nor were all of them immortal, because the demigods died, of which many tombs of Sileni, which are to be seen at Pergamum in Asia, bear witness (says Pausanias), and the nymphs likewise died. So that there were gods of every sort among the ancients, as can be seen in St Augustine in the book of the City of God, from what he reports of Varro.

<aside>God has no form</aside>

But for all that there were also some who had a certain good opinion of God, holding that He was one only, eternal and invisible, and therefore had no form; and whoever seeks one (says Pliny) indulges his own weakness too much. Hence Antisthenes, head of the Cynic school, said, as Theodoret, bishop of Cyrrhus, reports, that God cannot be seen with the eyes, because He is not like anything visible; and that therefore one must not think of knowing Him by an image or statue made of Him.

<aside>Xenophon</aside>
<aside>Xenophanes</aside>

And Xenophon, imitator of Socrates, said that one knew well that God is great and powerful, since He moved everything and Himself always stood unmoved; but one could not know what appearance He had, nor what face. And on this point Xenophanes, mocking the vanity of men who worshipped the statues made by Phidias, by Polykleitos and by other sculptors, said that if horses, oxen and elephants had had hands and had known how to use them, they too would have made the gods in the form of elephants, of ox, of horse, as men have made them in human form.

<aside>Cicero</aside>
<aside>The Jews</aside>
<aside>The Jews had no images</aside>
<aside>Cornelius Tacitus</aside>

Cicero shows the same by some arguments, where, disputing about the nature of the gods, he has Cotta speak against the opinion of the Epicureans. The Jews, who among the ancients followed the true Religion, worshipped one God only, and looked upon Him not in statues or in images with the eyes of the body, but in His divinity with the light of the mind, as far as human nature allows. And, as Cornelius Tacitus reports, they held impious all those who fashioned the image of God and formed it in various materials in the likeness of human bodies; and therefore in their temples they had no statues nor any image at all.

<aside>Josephus</aside>

Hence, because Herod, king of Jerusalem, had had a great golden eagle set above the main door of the Temple, some young men rose up, as in a fury of the people, having heard that he was about to die, and broke it to pieces and threw it to the ground, as Josephus relates; because, they said, this was against the laws of religion and of their ancestors, and one must not wait for another occasion to avenge the honour of God. But the wretches paid dearly for it, because Herod still had so much life left that he had them seized and burned alive.

<aside>Suda</aside>

Suda reports that when Pilate had brought into Judaea some standards with the image of Tiberius, all those peoples were disturbed, as though he had broken their ancient ordinances, which were to have no image at all in the city. Others did the same, in not wanting any image at all, such as Trismegistus, who said that whoever wanted to see statues of the gods before his eyes showed that he did not believe the gods to be in Heaven, or did not trust that his vows and his prayers could reach up there, and that for this reason images were made and called gods.

<aside>Lycurgus</aside>
<aside>Lactantius</aside>

Of Lycurgus we read that he would not allow that the gods could be likened to a man or to any other animal, and that therefore no statue or image of them should be made. Lactantius writes that from the beginning the elements were worshipped by the Egyptians without their making any image of them. And Numa, the second king of the Romans, did not want it believed that any likeness could be given to God, as Plutarch relates.

<aside>Persians</aside>
<aside>Scythians</aside>

Hence the Romans stayed at the beginning a hundred and seventy years without ever making any image of their gods, as though it were a grave error to draw divine and immortal things down to the likeness of mortal and human ones. The Persians likewise, and those of Libya, in the earliest times had no statues, nor altars, nor temples. Of the Scythians Herodotus writes that, although they worshipped many gods, such as Vesta, Jupiter, Apollo, Mars and others, to whom they gave proper names in their own language, they made temple, altar or statue to none other than Mars, as we shall see later in his image; and yet they sacrificed to all of them in one and the same manner.


<aside>Issedones</aside>
<aside>A skull for an image</aside>

The Issedones, a people likewise of Scythia, worshipped no other image than the skull of a dead man, having, as the same Herodotus relates, such a custom among them that when someone's father died, all the relatives and friends brought sheep, which they then killed and cut all into pieces, and they did the same with the body of the dead man, which they cut into pieces, and from these meats all mixed together, of the sheep and of the dead man, they made a great banquet and ate it all without distinction.

<aside>Pomponius Mela</aside>
<aside>Solinus</aside>

Then they skinned the head of the dead man, which had been kept for this purpose, and cleaned it well inside and out, so that the skull alone remained, quite bare. And this they gilded, and kept as an image, to which they made every year a solemn sacrifice. And Pomponius Mela and Solinus report that they kept it as a drinking cup, and that it was the greatest honour they knew how to do the dead man.

<aside>Suda</aside>

Similar to this is what Suda reports of a certain people of Judaea, who worshipped a gold skull of an ass and sacrificed to it every third year a foreigner, cutting him all into tiny pieces. Those of Massilia in Narbonese Gaul worshipped in consecrated groves without any image, except that sometimes they paid reverence to tall trunks, no differently than if they had believed the numina to dwell in them; so writes Lucan.

<aside>Oaks worshipped</aside>

And in the first times after the flood good and just men lived under the oaks, as is read in Pliny, and held them in place of holy numina and sacred temples, because the oaks gave them acorns, on which they lived, and covered them from the rains and from the other injuries of the weather. Describing Achaea, Pausanias states that in a certain part of that country there were some thirty square stones without any other figure, each of which had the name of one of various gods; and they were guarded with great veneration; because it was an ancient custom of the Greeks to worship such stones no less than the images of the gods.

Cornelius Tacitus relates, where he writes of Germany, that the Germans had neither statues nor temples, because they thought it a great wrong to shut the gods up between walls in the narrow space of a temple, and that it ill became their greatness to draw them down to the small form of the human body. They counted among their gods only those whom they could see, and from whom they felt manifest benefit. These were the Sun, Vulcan and the Moon. Of the others they knew none, as Caesar writes, nor had they even heard them named.

<aside>Origin of the gods</aside>
<aside>Hesiod</aside>
<aside>Homer</aside>

Herodotus writes that from the beginning the Greeks worshipped the gods and sacrificed to them without naming them, until they afterwards got the names from Egypt. But where these gods came from, and whether they came one by one, or all together, or had all been there always, this, he says, in his time was not yet known, except that Hesiod and Homer, who lived about four hundred years before him, introduced among the Greeks the genealogy of the gods with many surnames, and gave them various skills and various forms. Hence one could almost say that from these men the Greeks had learned to form the gods in various ways.

<aside>Marcellus brought statues from the Greeks to Rome</aside>

But let us say, together with the same Herodotus, that they took it from the Egyptians, because these were the first to build temples, set up altars and place statues. As the Greeks took it from those of Egypt, so the Romans had the use of statues from the Greeks; and it was when Marcellus took Syracuse, because he carried to Rome whatever he found there that was beautiful, both to make a spectacle of it in his triumph and to adorn the city, which up to that time had not yet known what delight painting and sculpture gave.

And therefore Marcellus was then blamed by many, first because it seemed that he had wished too arrogantly to lead even the gods as prisoners, making their images seen in the pomp of his triumph, then because he had given the people of Rome, accustomed only to the toils of wars, occasion to give themselves to indolence and to useless leisure, often wasting time in looking at beautiful statues and charming paintings, to marvel at the art and the skill of those who made them.

<aside>Plutarch</aside>
<aside>Livy</aside>

So writes Plutarch, and adds that Marcellus nevertheless took pride in having been the first to show the Romans how to admire the beautiful things of Greece; and before Plutarch, Livy had written the same, saying that from then on the Romans began to admire the works of the Greek arts, and that therefore they afterwards gathered with great licence the spoils of sacred things as well as of profane ones.

<aside>Tertullian</aside>

Tertullian, saying that religion in Rome was ordered by Numa with poor ceremonies and without images, because neither Greeks nor Tuscans had yet gone there to make them, seemed to hold that Tarquinius Priscus was the first who, as a Greek, as he was, and excellently versed in the vain religion of the Etruscans, showed the Romans how to make images of the gods. The use of these came, then, from the Egyptians, and through the Greeks passed to the Romans; but how it began in Egypt is too difficult to know, so differently has it been written about.

<aside>Origin of images</aside>
<aside>Eusebius</aside>

Lactantius says that many have believed that the first statues were made for those kings and valiant men who with prudence and justly had governed the peoples subject to them, these wishing to show in statues the memory they kept of just kings, and the reverent affection they preserved for them even after death. Eusebius likewise writes that the pagans were accustomed to preserve in statues the memory of the worthiest persons, showing in that way how loved, and in what respect held, was one who acted virtuously.


<aside>Serug</aside>

One reads in Suda that a Serug, descended from the stock of Japheth, son of Noah, was the first who introduced the worship of images and idols, made by him in memory of valiant men, whom he had people worship as gods and benefactors of the world. There were also kings who, while living, had statues made of themselves and worshipped, like Semiramis, who, if she was not the first, was certainly among the first.

<aside>Marvellous statue</aside>

She had herself carved in a stone seventeen stades in size, which is more than two Italian miles, and ordained that a hundred men in the manner of priests should go to worship her with solemn ceremonies, offering her various gifts, as to a numen.

Eusebius relates that there was in Egypt a very rich man who, to remedy the grief he felt at the death of an only son of his, had a statue of him made, keeping it with the same affection that he bore the son; so the members of his household, when they felt they had offended him, and therefore feared some severe punishment, ran to the statue, bowed to it, worshipped it and begged pardon, and so were pardoned. From which it came about that they afterwards offered to this statue flowers and various other gifts, as to one that was often the salvation of many.

<aside>King Ninus</aside>

But truly the greater part of writers agree that Ninus, king and first monarch of the Assyrians, was the one who first made statues and gave others occasion to make them; for he bore such love to his father Belus that in memory of him he had a statue resembling him set up, and for those who fled to it and commended themselves to it he willed that any misdeed committed by them should be pardoned and remitted.

<aside>Why gods in human form</aside>

Following his example, perhaps, the Egyptian mentioned above did likewise, and many others then followed him, making statues, to which afterwards, because it seemed perhaps more honourable, the names of various gods were given; and so images of these gods were made in the likeness, for the most part, of human bodies. They did this not because the ancients were all so foolish as to believe that the gods had a head, hands and feet like men, but because, as Varro writes, human souls being similar to divine souls, and neither the one nor the other being visible, they wished the bodies to bear witness to this likeness.

<aside>Porphyry</aside>

Porphyry too said, as Eusebius reports, that the gods were made in human effigy to show that, as God is all mind and reason, so men too have their share in it.

Lactantius holds that Prometheus was the first who made from earth an image of a man, and that the art of making statues began with him; and it is said that Minerva, admiring so beautiful a work, and desiring that it should have every sort of perfection, offered to grant him whatever he might know how to ask of her for it. And when she had taken him to Heaven for this purpose, he, having noticed that all things took their soul from flames and fire, secretly brought a little torch that he carried with him close to one of the wheels of the Sun, and lit it, and carried it back to earth, and bringing it to the breast of the figure he had formed made it animate and alive; hence it came about that to man, imitator of the divine work, was given what belongs to God, it being said that Prometheus had made the first man.


<aside>Prometheus worshipped</aside>

For this reason he had both temples and altars as a numen; and one of the altars consecrated to him was in the Academy of the Athenians, as Pausanias writes, where men went at a certain time to light some lamps, with which in hand they ran one after another; and whoever carried his own lit as far as inside the city had the palm of victory; those who were ahead always yielding in turn (if their lamps went out) to those who came behind; or else they carried a single lamp, and running they handed it to one another, the one nearest to whoever was ahead of him always taking it over.

<aside>Plato</aside>

This ceremony, or game, or whatever it be called, was done not only in honour of Prometheus, although it is read that it was ordained by him, but of Vulcan too, and of Minerva; and they did not always run on foot but sometimes on horseback. Hence Adeimantus in Plato, wishing to persuade Socrates to stay in a certain company, tells him that in the evening he will see the game of the horses, which, running, handed one another the lit torch in honour of the goddess, who was Minerva.

And Herodotus, relating the manner devised by the Persians for sending news of affairs quickly, which was like the one we use today in the posts, when the *pacchetto* runs (as they say in French) and is handed from post to post to the one who runs anew, says that they did as the Greeks do when, running and handing it to one another, they carry the lit torch to Vulcan. Of this game some have said that it represents what Prometheus did when he took fire from Heaven and carried it to earth, as we said above, and that therefore it was so ordained by him.

<aside>Lucretius</aside>

And others said that it shows the course of human life, in which those who go ahead yield the light of life to those who come behind. So said Plato when laying down his laws: that men ought to marry in order to have children, so that the life which they themselves have received from others they, like a burning torch, may likewise hand on to others. And Lucretius, speaking of the succession of mortals, said that, running, they hand to one another the lamp of life.

Among the Phocians there was also a certain small temple dedicated to Prometheus with a statue, which some held to be of Aesculapius; but because opposite it there were some large stones of the colour of sand, which gave off a smell like that of human bodies, it was believed more generally that it was of Prometheus himself, and that those stones had remained of the same material from which he formed that first man, from whom afterwards the whole human race came.

<aside>Providence</aside>

And it may very well be that Prometheus made the first man, if by him we understand, as Plato understood, the supreme providence, by which not only men but all the other things of the world were created and made in the beginning. And so this providence was worshipped by the ancients as a goddess, who like the best mother of a family governs the universe; and her image was that of an elderly woman in the dress of a grave matron.

> Images of the goddess Providence, held by the ancients to be the soul of the world and creator of everything.


<aside>Pliny</aside>

Further, how much the ancients loved statues is seen from the great number of them: for Pliny writes that at Modon [?1] there were more than three thousand of them, and no fewer at Athens, at Delphi and in other places of Greece. And the Romans were no less ambitious in this than the Greeks, because they had so many statues that it was said that there was in Rome another people, of stone.

<aside>Marcus Agrippa</aside>

The ancients made collections not only of statues but of paintings as well, gathering as many as they could get, made by excellent painters and sculptors, and adorned their houses with them not only in the city but outside it too, in the country. This was judged to have too much of the wanton, and not to suit the austere life of the Romans; hence Marcus Agrippa made a fine speech about it, wishing to persuade that all the statues and panels that stood as ornament in private houses should be put on public display.

<aside>Vitruvius</aside>

And this would have been, says Pliny, much better than sending them off, as if into exile, to the country estates. Varro writes that many went to the estates of Lucullus only to see the beautiful paintings and sculptures that he kept there. For these they made places on purpose, as Vitruvius writes about them, saying that they must be large and spacious.

<aside>Suetonius</aside>
<aside>Lampridius</aside>

Then the ancients took up the practice of making statues in such a way that they could at will remove their heads and put others on them. Hence Suetonius, speaking of the vainglory of Caligula, writes that, thinking he had gone beyond the grandeur of all other princes and kings, he began to usurp divine honours and commanded that from all the images of the gods that were remarkable for religion and for art, such as that of Olympian Jupiter and others, the heads be removed and his own be put on. And Lampridius likewise writes that the emperor Commodus removed the head of the Colossus, which was of Nero, and put on his own.

<aside>Statues held in great respect</aside>

Besides this, public statues, whosever they might be, were held in such respect that they were guarded as a religious thing, and it was not lawful to remove them nor to offend them in any way, as Cicero says speaking against Verres, and he gives as an example those of Rhodes, who, although they had had a most cruel war with Mithridates, and therefore hated him as a most grievous enemy, nevertheless never moved nor even touched his statue, which was with them in one of the worthiest places of the city.

And the statues of princes had this privilege, that everyone who fled to them was safe, and could not be dragged from there by force. But this did not avail the son of Mark Antony; for Augustus, as is seen in Suetonius, had him dragged from the statue of Caesar, to which he had fled for his safety, and commanded that he be killed.

<aside>Acilius Glabrio</aside>
<aside>Alexander of Aphrodisias</aside>
<aside>Why statues are nude</aside>

Statues were made sometimes clothed and sometimes nude, and they made some also all gilded; and Acilius Glabrio was the first, as Livy writes, who in Italy made a gilded statue, which he set up to his father Glabrio. Alexander of Aphrodisias writes that anciently the statues of the gods and of kings were often made nude, to show that their power is open and manifest to everyone, and that they are, or ought to be, of sincere and naked spirit, not stained by vices nor covered by deceits.

And Pliny says that it was the custom of the Greeks to make statues nude, whereas [?2] the Romans were accustomed to put cuirasses on them, at least inasmuch as at the beginning they made statues only for one who by some illustrious deed had deserved that his memory be kept.

<aside>Those who scorned statues</aside>
<aside>Ammianus Marcellinus</aside>
<aside>Agesilaus</aside>
<aside>Xenophon</aside>

But this, perhaps, was not afterwards always observed; and statues were given to many for something other than their own worth. Hence Cato never made account of them. To one who once asked him why he had no statue among so many nobles his equals, he replied, as Ammianus Marcellinus relates, that he would rather have good men wonder why he had not one than have them not dare say why he had one. And Agesilaus likewise among the Greeks refused the honour of statues, saying, as Xenophon reports, that these brought praise to the sculptors, but for himself it was acting virtuously that did so.

<aside>Statues carried about</aside>
<aside>Appian</aside>

These statues of princes and of other illustrious men the ancient Romans carried about in public and solemn processions together with those of the gods, taking them from the square where they all stood, except that of Scipio, which was taken from the Capitol, as Appian writes, because while living he had already given the world to understand that his every action came from divine counsel; and as if Jupiter showed him everything he was to do, he often shut himself up alone in the temple of Jupiter on the Capitol. And therefore his statue too was kept there, and then always guarded.

<aside>Sallust</aside>
<aside>Why images were made in different ways</aside>

By these statues and images the noblest families were recognised. Hence Marius, being of an ignoble family, says in Sallust that he has no statues nor images to show of his ancestors, but that he can show in their place the honourable rewards brought home from wars won. But let us return to the images of the gods: they were made in different ways, according to what the customs of the peoples were, showing at times in them that to which the peoples were more inclined.


<aside>Phoenicians</aside>

Hence Suda writes that those of Phoenicia made their gods with money bags in hand, because they judged that whoever was richer in gold was worth more than others. And the Greeks made them armed, because they believed that peoples were held in subjection chiefly by arms. Besides this, the ancients sometimes showed in the statues of the gods what they desired to obtain from them, or had already obtained, because they often made them by vow; and they did the same almost always with the surnames they gave them; but the principal and most fitting were those that signified their nature and the effects that were believed to come from them.

<aside>Statues of hidden meaning</aside>

Nor were they always made in such a way that all understood them, the religion of those times, though vain and false, having introduced the custom of keeping a great part of its matters hidden, so that only the priests knew them, and by the others they were believed simply, without inquiring further than what all were permitted to know.

<aside>King Tarquin</aside>
<aside>Valerius Maximus</aside>

Hence one reads in Livy and in many others that, when some books of Numa were found which might have done great harm to the religion of those times if they had come to light (because they perhaps uncovered its vanities), they were burned in public by order of the Senate, so that the common people should know nothing more of it than what was shown them by the Pontiff and by the other priests who had the care of it. And King Tarquin had drowned in the sea, as Valerius Maximus reports, a certain Marcus Tullius, to whom the book of the secrets of religion had been given in keeping, because he let Petronius Sabinus take a copy of it.

From this it will perhaps come about that sometimes the reason for some image that I shall have described will be left out, because Herodotus, Pausanias, Plutarch and many others, from whom I have taken its portrait, often say either that there is none or that religion forbids them to tell it. But this will happen very rarely, because what one has not wished to say entirely is sometimes gathered in pieces from many; and so have I done as much as I could.

<aside>Aristotle</aside>

Continuing, then, on why the gods were made in different ways: Eusebius, citing the words of Porphyry, says that the ancients, in order to make known the diversity of the gods, made some male and some female, others virgins and others accompanied, and for this reason also dressed their statues in no uniform way. And Aristotle says that the ancients thought the life of the gods to be similar to that of men, because they had also made them in human effigy; and so, as they themselves lived under a king, so they said that among the gods too there was one.

<aside>Lactantius</aside>

Lactantius, having proved by many arguments that the gods of the ancients were men, whose memory was consecrated after death, adds that therefore they were of different ages, one a child, one a youth, and one an old man; and that to each was given a certain and proper image, because their images were made to represent the age and the dress that they had when they died.

And for this too it may be said that so many other things have been feigned which are told of the gods of the ancients just as if they were men. And I shall tell some of them, as occasion offers in drawing up the particular images of many, which I shall take up after I have said of what material they were made.


<aside>Material of the images</aside>
<aside>Wooden images</aside>
<aside>Theophrastus</aside>

Eusebius, taking this too from Porphyry, says that, since God is a most pure light, which cannot be comprehended by our senses, He was made of a bright and shining material, such as the finest marble and crystal. He was made of gold too, to show the eternal and divine fire in which He dwells; and many, making Him of black stone, meant to convey His invisibility. But he spoke perhaps of his own times: since by the most ancient peoples the gods were made of wood, as is read in Theophrastus where he writes of the nature of plants, that they used to make them of cedar, of cypress, of lotus and of boxwood, and some even of the root of the olive.

<aside>Plutarch</aside>

Pliny writes that because the wood of the cedar lasts almost eternally, the ancients made from it the statues of the gods; and that in Rome there was one of Apollo brought from Seleucia. Plutarch writes of this thus: the making of images is a most ancient thing, and the ancients made them of wood, because it seemed to them that stone was too hard a thing to make gods from, and they thought that gold and silver were as it were the dregs of barren and infertile earth, because where the mines of these metals are, little else grows there; and the ancients called that earth sick and unhappy which produced neither grasses, flowers nor fruits; because they, in whose breasts avarice had no force, cared for nothing more than that by which they could nourish themselves and live.

<aside>Plato</aside>

Plato too seems to hold that the statues of the gods should be made only of wood, because he writes thus: the earth being a dwelling consecrated to the gods, their images must not be made of it, nor of gold, nor of silver, because these are things for which those who possess them are envied. On this point Lactantius writes that the rich statues of the gods showed the avarice of men, who under cover of religion took pleasure in having gold, ivory, gems and other precious things, making of them the sacred images, which they held dear more for the material they were made of than for those whom they represented.

Plato continues then in this way: ivory is a thing that had the soul first and then laid it down, and therefore it is not good for making statues of the gods; nor is iron good for this, nor the other hard metals, because they are used in wars and are instruments of killings. There still remained, therefore, according to Plato, only wood for making the sacred images.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

And Pausanias too says that in his opinion in the first times all the images of the gods among the Greeks were of wood, and especially those that had been made by the Egyptians, because a statue of Apollo at Argos, dedicated to him by Danaus, who was most ancient, was of wood. And it seemed that none of the most ancient images was to be found made of anything other than ebony, cypress, cedar, oak, ivy or lotus.

<aside>Epidaurians</aside>

But of olive too there were some, and made on the advice of the Oracle, which showed precisely that in those times the gods preferred to be made of wood rather than of another material. For one reads in Herodotus that those of Epidaurus sent to ask the Oracle at Delphi the way to remedy a very great barrenness, and it was answered them that they should make two images of Damia and Auxesia (these were the demons, or Genii, as we may say, of the country), not of metal nor of stone but of wood of the olive that is not wild. In the first temple that was made to Juno at Argos there was set for her an image of a pear-tree trunk; and in Rome, where she was called Queen, she had two images of cypress, which were carried with solemn ceremonies, as Livy writes, to a certain sacrifice that was ordained the first time Hannibal passed into Italy.

One reads in Pliny that at Populonia there was a very ancient statue of Jupiter, made of a single vine. And this is no marvel, if indeed it was true that vines were found so large and thick that of them the columns of the temple of Juno at Metapontum were made, as the same Pliny writes. And of the chaste tree, popularly called *Agno casto*, a statue was made to Aesculapius, as Pausanias writes, in a certain part of Laconia, where from the material of the statue he was called Agnites.

Of wood likewise the Romans made the gods, as long as they were friends of simple poverty. Hence Tibullus, speaking to the household gods called Lares, says words that in our language sound like this:

> Let shame not seize you, although you are
> made of a dry trunk: for such
> you were also in the happy times
> of our poor grandfathers, when
> faith, piety and justice
> were observed much better than they are today.
> And with welcome poverty they were worshipped,
> in the poor houses, the gods of wood.
>
> *(Tibullus)*

And Propertius has Vertumnus speak in this way of his statue:

> I was made without art from a dry trunk,
> and as a poor little god of wood
> I stood before the time of good Numa
> in the city, which was always dear to me.
>
> *(Propertius)*


In the islands discovered in past years by the Spaniards, which are now called the New World because they were unknown to the ancients, it has been found that those peoples worshipped certain idols made some of clay, some of wood and some of stone.

<aside>Pliny</aside>
<aside>Evander</aside>

And Pliny writes that although the making of statues in Italy was a very ancient thing, as can be known from the Hercules that was consecrated as early as by Evander in the Forum Boarium, which they used always to dress in triumphal ornaments at the times of the Triumphs, nevertheless no images of anything other than wood were given to the gods or to their temples before Asia was subjugated by the Romans. From it the precious statues passed into Italy, because Greece was not always content with wood alone for making her gods, but made them also of gold and of other various metals. And to show herself more splendid and magnificent towards them, says Pausanias, she often had ivory brought as far as from India and from the Ethiopians to make statues of it for them; and of iron too some were made, such as the Hercules fighting the Hydra among the Phocians; but this was so difficult that few statues of iron were made. Hence at Pergamum, a city of Asia, many went to see as marvellous things two iron heads consecrated to Bacchus, the one of a lion, the other of a boar.

<aside>Servius</aside>

Corydon, singing with Thyrsis in Virgil, promises Diana to make her all of polished marble, and here Servius notes that the ancients often made only the head and the breast of marble in statues. Besides this, they almost always made some gods, lowly and plebeian ones, such as Priapus and others like him, who stood for the most part in the fields and in the open air, of wood only, of clay, or of some other similar cheap material; and the others, nobler ones, such as the gods of Heaven, of a more worthy material.

<aside>Gods in the likeness of animals</aside>
<aside>Boccaccio</aside>

Not all the gods of the ancients were always made in human form, but often in the likeness of various animals, and sometimes of man and beast joined together. Hence if, as Seneca writes and St Augustine repeats, they had been alive in the form in which their images were made, they would have been not adored as numina but fled from as monsters. And in Egypt more than in any other country there were these monstrous images, as will be seen in many images, which I shall begin with Eternity: for although not all the gods of the ancients were eternal and immortal, the worthiest were nevertheless held to be such, and therefore it was believed that Eternity always accompanied them; although Boccaccio, where he relates the Genealogy of the Gods, says that the ancients gave her as companion to Demogorgon only, who, he states, was the first of all the gods, and dwelt in the middle of the earth all pale, and surrounded by a very dark mist, covered with a certain downy dampness, just like those things that stand in a damp place. But I have never yet found nor seen an ancient writer who speaks of him.

> Images of Eternity, with the image of the god Demogorgon, companion of Eternity, with the serpent that bites its tail, signifying the year and its revolution.


<aside>Eternity</aside>
<aside>Trismegistus</aside>

So, I say, Eternity always stayed with those gods who were held to be immortal; who she is, her name alone shows well enough, which means a thing that contains in itself all ages and all centuries, so that no span of time can measure it; although in a certain way it may be said that she too is time, but time that never has an end. And therefore Trismegistus, the Pythagoreans and Plato said that time was the image of Eternity, because it revolves upon itself, and it seems that its end is never seen.

<aside>Perpetuity</aside>
<aside>Boethius</aside>

But this may rather be called Perpetuity; for, although it never has an end, it does not, however, possess this infinite life of its own wholly, all at one and the same point, which is proper to Eternity, according to Boethius; who says that although it seemed to Plato that the world had no beginning, nor would ever have an end, those nevertheless are deceived who, following this opinion, call it coeternal with God; because, to give things their proper name, they ought to say, even holding Plato's opinion, that God is Eternal and the world perpetual.

Boethius describes Eternity, then, as the simultaneous possession of all times, and this is proper to God, because for Him time neither passes nor comes, as it does for all created things, even though some of them were to have no end. But let us not seek it now so minutely, as perhaps the ancients did not seek it either, when they called their gods eternal, meaning thereby that they were immortal and would never end, and that Eternity was this infinity of time.

<aside>Claudian</aside>
<aside>Image of the year</aside>

Hence in Claudian, who describes her at length in the praises of Stilicho, a serpent encircles the cave where she dwells, in such a way that it thrusts its tail into its mouth; which shows the effect of time, which is always turning upon itself, he having taken the example from those of Egypt, who likewise showed the year with the serpent that bit its tail; because the times are joined together in such a way that the end of the past is as it were the beginning of what is to come.

<aside>Medal of Faustina</aside>

Eternity is seen on a medal of Faustina, made in this fashion. A woman stands upright, dressed as a matron, with a ball in her right hand, and above her head she has a wide veil spread out, which covers her from one shoulder to the other. But let us see the whole picture that Claudian draws of her, rendered by me in our language in this manner:

> In a place so far from us and secret
> that no mortal trace appears there,
> where the way is forbidden to the human mind,
> and where perhaps not even the gods can arrive,
> a cave lies, glad with years,
> mother of infinite years, and equal to them in age,
> which in a manner that never fails
> sends forth the times and calls them back to its ample bosom.
>
> Around it, with its supple body, there winds
> a serpent full of greening scales,
> which greedily squeezes whatever it finds,
> as though it desired to devour everything;
> and it thrusts its tail into its throat and pretends
> to want to eat it with greedy hunger.
> It goes in a circle, and with its accustomed ways
> returns quietly always to where it set out from.
>
> At the door, with a face deserving reverence
> and full of years, stands nurturing Nature,
> like a guardian who faithfully attends
> to those who come and go with diligent care;
> around fly souls, and each seems to hang
> in due shape
> from the limbs that are given her by lot,
> and they stay with her as long as it pleases Death.
>
> And in the cave, in the immense grotto,
> an Old Man, whose chin and hair are
> sprinkled with white snow, stays, writes and dispenses
> the firm laws given to the Universe.
> And while he thinks of ordering the whole,
> his mind turned to the fair order,
> he divides certain numbers among the stars,
> from which they afterwards appear so lovely and beautiful.
>
> With immutable order he prescribes
> to each when she is to go or to stand;
> from which whatever among us dies or lives
> has life and death; then he turns to look again
> and to see how Mars arrives at his course,
> who, though accustomed to walk
> by a fixed way, goes nevertheless to a fixed end;
> for so the divine laws require.
>
> How with a measured step Jupiter goes round,
> bringing benefit to the world,
> how the Moon hides by day
> and quickly changes her fair fruitful [?3] light,
> how, having departed, slow to return
> is Saturn, grim, sad and barren,
> how beautiful is Venus, and after her
> wandering goes the messenger of the gods.
>
> And when Phoebus approaches the cave,
> at once to meet him comes forth powerful
> Nature, and before the high rays [?4] bows
> the white Old Man, humble and reverent.
> Then of itself the divine
> cave opens, then are seen standing open
> the adamantine gates, and little by little
> all the secrets of that place appear.
>
> There the ages, of various
> metals, are made in varied aspects,
> and each of them seems to hold itself
> on its own throne with chosen companions.
> This one is of iron, by which mortals often
> struck one another with injuries and spites;
> that one of copper, under whose government
> the whole world was a little less troubled.
>
> One of them is of silver, and it shines
> on a fair throne raised high above all around;
> but rarely among us mortals does it descend
> to adorn the world with so fair a light.
> The one who rises higher than the others
> is of gold, and of gold are those he has around him,
> all full of faith and of prudence,
> of goodness, of justice and of clemency.
>
> And these are the blessed years that to mortals
> will bring immense happiness,
> when Phoebus, who dispenses them as he wills,
> shall have pity on our ills,
> and will bring it about that from Heaven, spreading her wings,
> fair Astraea, kindled anew with love
> of seeing the world again, shall come to stay among us
> and never again depart.
>
> *(Claudian)*

<aside>Explanation of the cave of Eternity</aside>

The description and picture of this cave, or grotto, whichever we wish to call it, show, as Boccaccio expounds it, that Eternity stands above all times. And so she is far off and unknown not only to mortals but almost even to the celestial gods, that is, to those blessed souls who are up above in the Heavens. From the great bosom she sends the times to the cave, and these she calls back to the same; because in her they had once their beginning, and revolving in themselves they seem to issue from her and to return again to the same.

This is done quietly, because time passes as if secretly, and we do not notice it. To the door, where Nature stands, many souls go flying around, because they descend into mortal bodies, and when they depart from them they go into the bosom of Eternity; all this is done by the work of Nature, and therefore she stands here at the door.

> The cave of Eternity, with the image of Time, or of Fate, of Phoebus, of Nature, and of the four ages, signifying that everything comes from God and is contained in Him, and the revolution of human affairs.

The Old Man who divides the stars by numbers is perhaps God, not because he is old, for of Him it cannot be said that there is any limit of age, but because men commonly speak so, calling of great age even those who cannot die; and He, ordering the movement of the stars, distinguishes the times. But perhaps it would be more proper to say that the old man is Fate, because he bows to Phoebus, who might be taken for God, when he approaches the cave.

Boccaccio says nothing further of the ages that are there, as though it were a thing easy for everyone, and I too shall say no more of them, in order to come to the image of Saturn, because the ancients took him for time, and of time we have already begun to speak while reasoning of Eternity.

Eternity I do not dare to wish for this labour of mine; but I do pray that whoever can do so will be willing to give it life for some time.
