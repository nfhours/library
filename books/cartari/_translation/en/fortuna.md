# Fortune

> This is she who is so much crucified
> by the very ones who ought to give her praise,
> blaming her unjustly and speaking ill of her.
>
> *(Dante)*

<aside>Dante</aside>
<aside>Petrarch</aside>

So Dante speaks of Fortune, and it is from this that I wanted to begin, since I must now set out her image; for mortals blame her for everything that happens beyond their expectation, often taking badly what they ought rather to judge a great good. And it seems that they hold that the gaining and the loss of honours and riches come from Fortune, as does the overturning of all worldly things. Hence in Petrarch's canzone "Tacer non posso, e temo, &c." she speaks thus to him of herself:

> I have a different power from what you believe,
> and I know how to make people glad and sad in a moment;
> I am lighter than the wind:
> and I rule and turn all those you see in the world.
>
> *(Petrarch)*

<aside>Why Fortune is blamed</aside>
<aside>Fortune does not exist: Juvenal</aside>

And from this arise the endless reproaches that she hears of herself all day long; for it seems that these things, which we call the goods of fortune, go for the most part to him who deserves them least, and that he who would deserve them most remains miserably deprived of them. Whether this is good or bad I leave to be considered by whoever can see how many irksome cares, how many toils and how many dangers the goods of this world bring with them; for there are few who give thought to this, but almost all of us always seek to have them; and because we cannot satisfy our disordered desire, we then complain of Fortune, who, in the opinion of many, does not exist; hence Juvenal spoke of her thus:

> Where prudence is, Fortune has no power
> at all, and her numen is
> altogether empty; but we fools and madmen
> still want to make her a goddess who dwells in Heaven.
>
> *(Juvenal)*

<aside>Lactantius</aside>
<aside>Marcus Tullius</aside>

And Lactantius likewise says that Fortune is nothing but an empty name, which shows the little knowledge of men, agreeing with Marcus Tullius, who had written before him that the name of Fortune was introduced to cover human ignorance, which blames her for everything for which it cannot give an account.

But the ancients were no less mistaken about her than about the other gods, and so they worshipped her as a goddess who dispensed all worldly goods; and they thought that evil too came from her.

<aside>Two Fortunes</aside>
<aside>Pindar</aside>

For this reason two Fortunes were believed to exist, one good, the other bad; from the first came goods and felicities, and from the second all misfortunes and the other evils. Hence some have at times depicted Fortune with two faces: one was white, which showed the good one, the other black, which signified the bad one. And at Praeneste, where she had a temple much celebrated for the sure responses that were carried away from there, she was worshipped, according to Alexander ab Alexandro, under the image of two sisters. And for the same reason, perhaps, in Pindar too, as Plutarch reports, she turns two rudders with her hand.

Nevertheless it is for the most part held that there is only one Fortune, and her I shall depict according to the various designs left to us by the writers, beginning with the one Pausanias gives, where he writes that among the memorials of the ancients no statue of Fortune is found older than the one that Bupalus, an architect and excellent sculptor, made for the people of Smyrna, a people of Greece, in the form of a woman who had a polos on her head and held a cornucopia in one of her hands.

This statue showed what the office of Fortune is, which is to give and to take away riches, represented by the horn of plenty; and these things turn continually, as Heaven turns around the two poles. All those who have depicted Fortune, and made statues of her in whatever manner, have always shown the same thing afterwards, wishing to give us to understand that she has the government of the things down here, and that she can dispense them as she wishes.

<aside>Government of human affairs</aside>

This is read in Lactantius too, who describes how the ancients portrayed Fortune with the cornucopia and set a ship's rudder beside her, as though it belonged to her to give riches and the government of human affairs and temporal goods were in her hand, because in these there is no firmness, nor do they seem reasonably shared out, since the good for the most part suffer great hardships in them, and wicked men abound in them copiously.

> Image of Fortune, giver and dispenser and patroness of riches and human goods, and governess of the things down here, in which there is no more firmness or stability than a ship tossed on the unstable waves of the sea can be said to have.

<aside>Virgil</aside>

And so Fortune was said to be inconstant, blind, mad, and a much greater friend to the wicked than to the good, as is read in certain verses believed to be Virgil's, which in the vernacular run:

> O mighty Fortune, how often
> you change, and how much power, alas, cruel one,
> you usurp! You drive the good away from you
> and call the wicked, yet you do not always
> remain faithful even to these; you bring it about that more of your gifts
> are granted to him who deserves them least,
> depriving him who is worthy of them, and you so dispose
> your affairs that sad poverty
> oppresses the just with heavy hardships
> and the wicked enjoy
> all your goods. In their green age
> you give men bitter death, and those
> whom, burdened with years, life now wearies
> (because you apportion the times with a will
> not just), you still want to keep here.
> To the impious goes what, through you, departs
> from the better, and not in order to stay
> with them: so you change in a little hour,
> fragile, uncertain, faithless, and fleeting,
> you through whom a man does not always rise or lie down.
>
> *(Virgil)*

<aside>Martianus Capella</aside>

For these reasons the Thebans placed Plutus, as I said in his image, in the hands of Fortune, as though that god, who was believed to have all riches in his power, gave them and took them back again as it pleased her, whom Martianus Capella describes in the *Marriage of Philologia* in this way. There was, he says, a young girl, much more talkative than all the others, who never seemed to know how to stand still, all light and slender, and the wind, blowing from behind her, always made her swollen dress flutter in front.

Her name was Lot, according to some, and some called her Fortune, and some others Nemesis; and in her ample, wide lap she carried all the ornaments of the world, which she held out to some with a very swift hand, while from some, as if she were playing like a child, she used to pluck the hair, and some others she harshly struck on the head with a rod. And those same ones to whom she had at first shown herself so agreeable and friendly she afterwards struck on the head with her hand, as though she mocked them.

<aside>Horace</aside>

And Fortune is believed to do exactly this to us when she takes back her goods, leaving us disconsolate; and this would not happen if we did not make much greater account of what is hers than of what is our own: for riches belong to Fortune, and the virtues to us, and we always put the latter behind the former, as Horace says when he cries out indignantly thus:

> O citizens, foolish citizens,
> seek riches first,
> and leave the virtues behind them.
>
> *(Horace)*

<aside>Good and bad Fortune</aside>

Then the ancients showed the good and joyful Fortune, which is when she offers us her goods, and the sad and disconsolate one, as we are when we are deprived of them, both together in this way, although the inscription speaks only of good Fortune, as is often seen on the ancient marbles of the Greeks. A woman sits, modestly dressed in the habit of a matron, sad in appearance and disconsolate; in front of her stands a beautiful young woman, comely to look at, who gives her her right hand, and behind is a girl, who stands with one hand resting on the seat [?1] of the matron. The matron shows past Fortune, and therefore is sad; the young woman who gives her her hand and shows herself joyful is present Fortune, and the girl behind, resting on the seat, is the one who is coming, or is to come.

> Images of joyful and sad Fortune, or of Fortune past, present, and to come, judged by the ancients, although she is only an imagined name, to be greater than all their false gods and the patroness of the things down here; and this numen the ancients imagined as an excuse for their imprudences.

<aside>Nemesis</aside>

But before I go on speaking further of Fortune, I want to say who Nemesis was; for these two are very like each other, so much so that some have believed them to be one and the same thing, as can be seen from what I have just reported of Martianus Capella. Nevertheless each was worshipped separately, and the one and the other had images different from each other, as will appear from my design.

<aside>Ammianus Marcellinus</aside>

Nemesis, then, was a goddess who was believed to show each person what was right for him to do; and Ammianus Marcellinus speaks of her thus:

> This is the goddess who punishes the wicked and rewards the good, knowing all things, so that the ancient theologians imagined her the daughter of Justice, who from a certain secret part of Eternity stays looking upon the works of mortals.
>
> *(Ammianus Marcellinus)*

<aside>Macrobius</aside>
<aside>Rhamnusia</aside>

Macrobius says of her that she was worshipped as the avenger of pride, and in his usual manner he brings her back to the power of the Sun. For the Sun is of such a nature that wherever it appears it dims the splendour of every other light, and often makes appear and shine what before stayed hidden and seemed dark. So Nemesis likewise oppresses the over-proud, raises the humble, and helps them to live well; and in sum this goddess was believed to punish all those who grew too proud of the good they had. The poets often called her Rhamnusia, from a certain place in the country of Athens, where she had a most beautiful marble idol.

<aside>Adrasteia</aside>

She was also called Adrasteia at times, from King Adrastus, because he was the first to set up a temple to her. The ancients depicted her with wings, because they believed that she was ready for everyone with marvellous speed, and beside her they put a ship's rudder, and a wheel under her feet.

> Image of the goddess Nemesis, who points out good works and severely punishes the proud and the wicked; a courteous and generous giver, who rewards good deeds, being held to be the knower of all of them: daughter of Justice, who teaches us to use measure and good sense in our actions.

Nemesis was sometimes depicted as holding a bridle in one hand and in the other a piece of wood with which one measures, wishing thereby to show that men must put a bridle on the tongue and do everything with measure, as two Greek verses say, which were once made on this statue, and in the vernacular their sense is this:

> With this bridle and with this measure
> I, Nemesis, show that everyone
> must rein in the tongue, and never do
> anything unless he has first measured it well.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>
<aside>Ausonius</aside>

Pausanias writes that Nemesis was a goddess exceedingly hostile to insolent and over-proud men, and he continues thus. The barbarians were once punished by her wrath: despising the Athenians, and having come into their lands as though they had already utterly defeated them, they had a most beautiful marble brought there in order to make a proud trophy of it afterwards; but it all turned out the opposite, for the Athenians remained the victors, and Phidias then made out of that marble, brought by the barbarians, an idol of the goddess Nemesis, on which Ausonius wrote an epigram, pretending that the goddess herself says that it was made as a sign of the victory of the Greeks, and to show that she did not leave the vain pride of the Persians unpunished.

This idol had on its head a crown carved with stags and small images of Victory, and held an ash branch in the left hand and in the right a vase with some Ethiopians carved inside it; of which things Pausanias says that he cannot give any account, nor even know what to think of them, and I know even less.

<aside>Nemesis without wings</aside>
<aside>Catullus</aside>

The same Pausanias adds that the statues of Nemesis did not have wings at first, as they afterwards had among the people of Smyrna, who were the first to make her winged, in the likeness of Cupid, because they believed that she had a great deal to do with lovers, as though she punished those of them who were too haughty and proud of their beauty, as Ovid shows in the fable of Narcissus. And Catullus likewise, after begging Licinius, a very beautiful youth, at length to come to him, says at the end: take care not to hold my prayers in little account and despise me, lest Nemesis, the terrible goddess, should some time punish you for it afterwards.

<aside>Justice</aside>
<aside>A. Gellius</aside>
<aside>Justice sees everything</aside>

Because, then, this goddess punished mortals for their proud and unjust deeds, some believed her to be the same as Justice. Her image is described by Chrysippus, according to Aulus Gellius, in the form of a beautiful virgin, terrible in aspect, neither proud nor humble, but such that with honest severity she shows herself worthy of all reverence, with eyes of most acute sight; hence Plato said that Justice sees everything, and that she was called by the ancient priests the avenger of all things.

> Image of Justice, guardian of the good and punisher of the wicked; and image of Justice trampling on and chastising Injury; and a hieroglyph denoting this Justice, and what she must be, appear, and do.

<aside>Apuleius</aside>
<aside>What judges ought to be</aside>

And Apuleius swears by the eye of the Sun and of Justice together, as though the eye of Justice saw no less than that of the Sun. We are to understand that all this must be in the ministers of justice, because they must penetrate with most acute sight as far as the hidden and concealed truth, and be pure like chaste virgins, so that neither precious gifts nor false flatteries nor anything else can corrupt them, but with the firmest severity they must always judge by reason; and they must show themselves terrible and fearsome to the guilty and the wicked, and pleasant and kind to the good and innocent.

Some have also put a balance in the hand of Justice, and sometimes that bundle of bound rods with the axe which the lictors carried before the Roman consuls. And at times Justice was depicted by the ancients in this way as well. A virgin sat naked on a square stone, and held in one hand a balance and in the other a naked sword [?2].

Diodorus writes that in a certain part of Egypt, where the Gates of Truth were, there was also a statue of Justice, which had no head; and he gives no explanation of it, nor shall I, as I come to say that in Egypt they depicted Justice in this way as well. They painted her left hand stretched out and open, because this is naturally colder and lazier than the right, and therefore less able to do injury to anyone.

Hence, among the other things carved on the chest of Cypselus, Pausanias writes, there was a beautiful woman who dragged another behind her, an ugly one, holding her tight by the neck with her left hand and with the right striking her harshly with a stick; and that the first was Justice and the second Injury. For just judges must always keep Injury oppressed, so that no wrong is ever done to anyone; they must see to it that the truth is never hidden from them, and so hear everything that each person says in his own defence, and not condemn the accused on the words of the accusers alone, if they do not wish to be like that judge whom Apelles once painted, as Lucian relates, after he had been freed by Ptolemy, king of Egypt. Ptolemy had been about to put him to death, having believed Antiphilus too foolishly, who out of envy had accused Apelles of being privy to a certain rebellion; but the truth was afterwards discovered by one of the conspirators, and the king, having learned of the deception, freed Apelles and gave him a hundred talents, and willed that Antiphilus, who had accused him wrongly, should afterwards be Apelles' slave forever.

<aside>Painting by Apelles</aside>
<aside>Calumny</aside>
<aside>Repentance</aside>

Apelles, then, wishing to show the danger he had been in, painted a most beautiful panel, which was afterwards called the Calumny of Apelles, in this way. One sat in the manner of a judge, who had long ears like those of an ass, as it is read that King Midas had them. Beside him two women, one on each side, seemed to be saying I know not what very softly in his ears; one of them was Ignorance, the other Suspicion. And he held out his hand to Calumny, who came toward him in the form of a beautiful, adorned woman, but who showed in her aspect that she was full of anger and disdain; in her left hand she had a lit torch, and with her right she dragged behind her by the hair a naked youth, who lamented miserably, raising his clasped hands to Heaven. Before her went Malice (*Liuore*), that is, Envy, who was an old man, thin and pale, like one who has been long ill; and behind her came two women who seemed to flatter her, making much of her beauty and adorning her all the while as much as they could; one was called Fraud, and the name of the other was Treachery. Behind these followed another woman called Repentance, with a few rags about her, all worn and torn, who, weeping abundantly, afflicted herself beyond measure and seemed to want to die of shame, because she saw Truth coming.

> Panel of Calumny painted by Apelles, in which there is the judge with the ears of an ass, denoting Ignorance, and two women speak into his ears, one of whom is Ignorance, the other Suspicion. The old man who goes before Calumny is the envious man, and the one whom Calumny holds by the hair is the Calumniated. Of the two women who accompany Calumny, one is Fraud and the other Treachery. Of the two women below, one is Repentance, looking at Truth, the effect of calumniating, which as its recompense awaits shame, harm, and disgrace.

<aside>Envy</aside>
<aside>Silius Italicus</aside>
<aside>Horace</aside>

Thus Lucian describes the Calumny once painted by Apelles, and he gathers from it that this is nothing other than a false accusation, believed by the judge, against someone who is not present to state his case; which for the most part is caused by Envy, and therefore Apelles put Envy before her. And Envy is the worst sickness there can be of the human mind, because it not only does harm to others, but also does great harm to the envious themselves. Hence Silius Italicus puts among the plagues and monsters that are in hell envy, which squeezes its throat with both hands; and therefore Horace did well to say that

> The tyrants of Sicily did not know how
> to find a greater torment than Envy.
>
> *(Horace)*

<aside>Virgil</aside>

For, as some verses believed to be Virgil's say, rendered into the vernacular in this way:

> Envy is a poison that devours
> the marrow and sucks out all the blood.
> So the envious man has his due punishment;
> for while another's fortune grieves him,
> he sighs, trembles, and roars like a lion,
> showing that his wretched soul is full
> of cruel hatred, which leads him
> to look on another's good with a crooked eye.
> So inside he turns to ice, and outside
> he is bathed in fury,
> which can make others aware of his pain;
> and, armed with a tongue of venom,
> he always bites and blames whatever he gazes at.
> A pale colour tinges his face,
> which gives a sure sign of inner grief,
> and the wretched body becomes such
> that it seems to be destroyed and undone.
> Whatever he sees brings him hatred and disdain:
> so he flees the light, and everything turns
> to ill for him, and with equal
> displeasure he shuns food, drink wearies him,
> he never sleeps, he never has rest,
> and his heart is always gnawed
> by that envious rage, which
> can never have an end; and against its grave sickness
> no physician's remedy avails.
>
> *(Virgil)*

> Images of Envy, who harms herself, since the envious man wastes away seeing another's prosperity, and stops his ears against another's praises, and squeezes his throat to choke himself, seeing them exalted; and this is the worst of vices.

<aside>Ovid</aside>

And Ovid, depicting her in the form of a woman (for, as we said a little earlier in speaking of the painting of Apelles, the Greeks depicted her as a man), describes her thus:

> Pale is her face, her body thin and dry,
> her eyes squint, and her tooth is rusty,
> her breast burns with bitter gall, and foul
> venom fills her tongue, and she never feels
> any pleasure except in another's grief:
> then Envy laughs, who otherwise
> shows herself every hour pained and sad,
> and always vigilant and wakeful for another's harm.
>
> *(Ovid)*

And in describing her house first, gloomy, cold, and murky, he had said that she stayed there eating snakes. Plutarch wrote at some length on envy, and the great Basil, in a speech on it, says that the envious are like vultures and flies. For as those, flying, pass over joyful fields and flowery meadows and do not alight except where they see some stinking body, and in this too, leaving the whole parts, go seeking the corrupt and spoiled ones, so the envious never look at what in others deserves to be praised, or pretend not to see it, and give their attention only to what can be blamed in some way.

<aside>Momus</aside>
<aside>Aesop</aside>

So Momus was believed to act among the gods; he too was a god among the ancients, and was born, according to Hesiod, of Sleep and Night: he never did anything himself, but looked at what the other gods did, and freely reproved and blamed whatever was not done to his taste. Hence Aesop wrote, and Aristotle reports it, that Momus blamed the maker of the bull, saying that he was ill advised to put its horns on its head, for he should have put them on its shoulders, so that it could strike with greater force.

> Image of Momus, god of reproof, or slander, and of blame, son of Sleep and Night, signifying that detractors are good for nothing and never do anything, but only blame the works of others, never looking at anything but what is open to reproof, and never at what is praiseworthy, like moles, which look neither at the sun nor at the day, but only at darkness and gloom.

And of man he said, as Lucian relates, that whoever made him greatly erred in not making a little window in his breast, so that one could easily see what he had in his heart. In Venus he found nothing to find fault with, as Philostratus writes, except that her slippers made too much noise when she walked. His image is described by certain Greek epigrams in the form of an old man, thin and dry, quite pale, with open mouth, and bent toward the earth, which he keeps striking with a stick that he has in his hand, perhaps because all the gods of the ancients were called sons of the earth.

<aside>Euripides</aside>
<aside>Aelian</aside>

Among these gods, Momus, god of reproof and blame, performed the office that some among us perform, who are therefore likewise called Momuses: moved only by a desire to speak ill of others at their pleasure, and without any reason, they blame whatever they see. This comes for the most part, as I have already said, from Envy, which, as Euripides said and Aelian reports, is an exceedingly wretched, wicked, and shameful thing; and it is read that the ancients designated it by depicting an eel, because this, as the same Aelian says, keeps by itself and never goes with the other fish.

<aside>Fraud</aside>
<aside>Dante</aside>

As for Fraud, whom Apelles depicted in the form of a woman, Dante drew her with the face only of an upright and just man, but with the rest of the body all that of a serpent, spotted with various colours, and ending in the tail of a scorpion. His words are these:

> And that filthy image of fraud
> came on, and landed its head and chest,
> but did not draw its tail onto the bank.
> Its face was the face of a just man,
> so benign was its skin on the outside,
> and a serpent's were both parts of the trunk.
> It had two paws, hairy up to the armpits,
> the back, the chest, and both flanks
> it had painted with knots and little wheels.
> With more colours, underlaid and overlaid,
> there never were Tartar or Turkish cloths,
> nor were such webs laid on the loom by Arachne.
>
> *(Dante)*

<aside>Nature of the fraudulent</aside>
<aside>Pine for Fraud</aside>

The explanation of this image is that the nature of deceiving and fraudulent men is to show themselves benign, pleasant, and modest in appearance and in words, but to be otherwise in deeds, so that all their works in the end show themselves full of deadly poison. For this reason the ancients at times also put the pine, wishing to depict Fraud: for this tree is tall and straight, and always green, and beautiful and pleasing to see, but then often harmful to whoever rests in its shade or passes under it without other regard, because its fruits, already ripe and therefore very hard, fall from the high branches and, if they chance to strike him on the head, hit him so fiercely that they kill him or at least make him feel very grave pain, if they happen instead to wound him in some other part of the body.

> Image of Fraud according to Dante, which denotes that the fraudulent and deceivers, under the cloak and face of the pleasant, modest, and loving, seek to arrive at their evil designs, being inwardly poisoned with every vice, fraud, and deceit.

But let us return to the image of Fortune, from which Nemesis led me astray; and I, passing then from one thing to another, did not remember to return to her before now, when nothing more remains for me to say about the painting of Apelles. Apelles, painting Fortune too, placed her seated, and being asked why he had done so, answered that he had never seen her standing; and among the Latins to stand (*stare*) means not only to be firm but also to be on one's feet, and from this he made a witticism, because Fortune is called changeable and unstable.

<aside>Horace</aside>

Wishing to show this in her image, the ancients, as Eusebius writes, depicted her sitting on a large ball and added wings to her, which carry her most swiftly now to one person, now to another. Hence Horace sings thus of her, his verses drawn into our language:

> Unstable Fortune
> attends to a cruel game,
> and always plays to the harm of mortals,
> without any rule
> she changes things, and renders
> honour to this one, to that one gives grave ills,
> and then those who
> were by her favour
> first glad and content
> she makes wretched and discontent,
> and, changing almost every hour,
> to one she gives, from another takes away,
> whether her wishes be kind or adverse to them.
> So I thank her
> when she stays still for me,
> and gladly enjoy her goods.
> But not so much that I do not remember
> my own, and that my thoughts too
> have no firm hope in them.
> So then, if she flies away,
> spreading her light wings,
> whatever she ever gave me
> I refuse, and my mind returns, secure,
> to that sole virtue
> which contents it,
> and does not try to have greater riches.
>
> *(Horace)*

<aside>Cebes</aside>
<aside>Artemidorus</aside>

Cebes, in that tablet in which he painted all human life, depicts Fortune as a blind and mad woman, who stands with her feet on a round stone. And Artemidorus has at times placed her seated on a column lying on its side, and sometimes depicts her beautiful and adorned, and sometimes dirty and badly dressed, and with her hand on a ship's rudder. And in this way we often see her on ancient medals and on ancient marbles.

<aside>Galen</aside>

Galen likewise, when he exhorts the young to the study of letters, says this of her:

> Wishing to set the wickedness of Fortune before our eyes in paintings and statues, the ancients were not content to depict her in the form of a woman (though this ought well to have been enough to show that she was mad and wicked and never stayed in one purpose); they added a round ball under her feet, depicting her without eyes, and then gave her a rudder in her hand, as though blindly and without any foresight she governed the things of the world.
>
> *(Galen)*

<aside>Pacuvius</aside>

Some verses of Pacuvius, which are read in the books of Cicero's *Rhetoric* and run thus in the vernacular, also draw Fortune very well and likewise explain her image:

> Mad, blind, and beastly is Fortune,
> according to what the philosophers have said,
> who have set her upon a stone that turns and rolls;
> so wherever this
> leans, she goes swiftly, and knows not where
> nor sees; hence with reason she was called blind.
> And because she changes too often
> they have called her mad, and beastly
> she has been called because she does not know
> who is worthy and who not, who good and who bad.
>
> *(Pacuvius)*

<aside>Good Fortune</aside>

Besides this, the caduceus was sometimes depicted with a cap on top, which had two small wings, one on each side, and with two cornucopias that embraced the caduceus itself; and this painting signified, according to some, that good Fortune goes almost always together with Eloquence and Learning. In sum, she was believed to be of such strength that there was no lack of those who said that virtue is worth little without her; and that although virtue leads to high undertakings and a glorious name, we shall never arrive there, or only with difficulty, if she does not accompany us, supposing, as the ancients believed, that Fortune is some numen, which in worldly things can do a great deal.

<aside>Seneca</aside>

And that we ourselves are to ourselves good fortune or bad, according as we know well or badly how to govern ourselves and to seize the good that is presented to us or let it go. Hence Seneca writes to his Lucilius that those are mistaken who judge that any good or evil comes to us from Fortune; for although she gives the material of the one and the other, and some beginnings to things that may afterwards turn out well or ill, nevertheless our mind can do much more than she, and draws its affairs as it wishes, so that it is itself to itself the cause of either a happy or a wretched life.

<aside>Ausonius</aside>
<aside>Occasion</aside>

And so, when we seize on what is bad, we ought to complain of our own incapacity and our poor sight over all the misfortunes that befall us afterwards, not of Fortune: as the ancients also showed in the image of Occasion, whom some make one and the same with Fortune; but if these two are not one and the same thing, they are very like each other, as can be seen from the portrait of this one, who was made a goddess by the ancients, perhaps so that, from her image, revered and often looked at, everyone should learn to take things in time, because these change with time and go away, leaving him who did not know how to take them full of sadness and repentance.

The image of Occasion was as follows: a naked woman stood with her feet on a wheel, or on a round ball, and had her long hair all turned over her forehead, so that the nape was left uncovered and as if bald, and at her feet she had wings, as Mercury is painted, and with her was another woman, all grieving, sad in aspect, and full of repentance. Such an idol was once made by Phidias, and an epigram of Ausonius on it is read, in which he describes Occasion as I have said, and gives her Repentance as a companion. For whoever lets the good occasion pass that presents itself in any matter has afterwards nothing but to repent and complain of himself.

<aside>The god Kairos</aside>
<aside>Posidippus</aside>
<aside>Ausonius</aside>

She whom the Latins called Occasion and Opportunity, and revered as a goddess, was called by the Greeks Opportune Time, and so was made by them a god, not a goddess, and his name was Kairos, since this word among the Greeks means opportunity of time, as Pausanias also writes, where he says that an altar was consecrated to him among the Eleans, and that a certain ancient poet in a hymn made for him calls him the youngest of all the sons of Saturn. So the god Kairos of the Greeks was the same as the Occasion of the Latins; of him Posidippus made an epigram describing his image, from which Ausonius perhaps took the subject of his own when he depicted Occasion, for they are wholly alike, except that Posidippus puts a razor in the hand of his god, and Ausonius gives his goddess Repentance besides as a companion. Callistratus too, a noble sculptor, made the god Kairos in the form of a youth in his most flourishing age, beautiful and comely, with his locks scattered in the wind, and in all the rest exactly as Posidippus describes him.

One must, then, keep one's eyes open and one's hands ready to lay hold of things when Occasion shows them to us, because she soon turns, and turns the bald nape to whoever did not know how to thrust his hands into the long locks she has over her forehead, and goes away on the swiftest feet.

> Hieroglyph denoting that good Fortune almost always goes with Eloquence and with Learning, and the image of Occasion, and opportunity, called by the Greeks the god Kairos, whom whoever does not seize when he presents himself afterwards seeks in vain and repents.

<aside>Fortune of the Scythians</aside>

The Scythians showed almost the same in the image of their Fortune, for, as Quintus Curtius reports, they depicted her without feet, but put wings about her hands, because with these she gives and offers goods, but with such speed that scarcely has anyone stretched out his hand to take them than she has already flown away. Besides, although Fortune sometimes joins us hand in hand, she never lets us seize the feathers she has around her hands, because she wishes to be able to fly off again at her pleasure, and to fly off without much delay, since she does not know how to stop, and the felicities that come from her last little.

> Image of Fortune among the Scythians, signifying her speed and instability in worldly things, and that one must go along with Occasion, because she flies away swiftly, and then is followed in vain, and in vain one repents of occasions lost and neglected.

<aside>Fortune of glass</aside>

Hence it was that some once made her of glass, as Alexander ab Alexandro writes; for as glass breaks at once at every slight obstacle, so the favours of Fortune quickly come to the ground. But the ancients did not for this cease to believe in her; on the contrary, they showed such trust in her that they always wanted to have her with them, above all the princes and the emperors; for these kept in their most secret chamber a gilded idol of Fortune, and worshipped it as a sacred thing, and also wanted it to be with them every time they went out in public.

<aside>Idol of Fortune with the emperors. Spartianus.</aside>

Hence Spartianus writes that the emperor Severus, having come to the extreme of life, wished to arrange that there should be two of these sacred statues of Fortune, so that each of his sons, who were two, should have one to accompany him and stay with him always; but being unable to attend to it, because his illness weighed on him too heavily, he commanded as he died that the sacred idol of Fortune should be placed in turn in the secret chamber of his sons, one day with the one and the next with the other, as though this were a sign of the empire divided equally between them.

<aside>The emperor Antoninus Pius</aside>

And the emperor Antoninus Pius, according to the same Spartianus, feeling himself near to death, commanded that the gilded statue of Fortune should be carried into the chamber of Marcus Antoninus, which was the surest sign of the empire transferred to him, as though the emperor who was dying, without saying anything else, designated him in this way as his successor.

Some, and very well, have painted Fortune upon a great tree with a long stick in her hand, with which she goes beating down its fruits, which are sceptres of kings, mitres, purses, ships, ploughs, and other things that denote dignities and all the human arts. Below one sees a great multitude of persons of every sort and rank, who wait to catch the fruit that comes down upon them. And one sees that someone of royal stock, if a plough touches him, must from the prince that he was become a poor farmer, whereas a peasant, if a sceptre or some purse falls upon him, becomes a prince and rich: so that one must settle oneself in a good place, so that, Fortune prospering, some good fortune may chance to touch us.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

Pausanias writes that in Greece, among the Eleans, Fortune had a temple, in which there was a very large idol of her in wood, all gilded except the hands and feet, which were of marble. And he then tells also of some other statues of Fortune made by the Greeks in various places, but does not report them, because they have nothing notable beyond what has already been said.

<aside>Fortune helpful to Love</aside>

I shall rather tell of the one that was at Aegira, a city of Achaea, although I have already spoken of it in the image of Cupid. It was made in this way. On one side she had the cornucopia, and held it in her hand, on the other the god Cupid; and this signified, as Pausanias interprets it, that it avails lovers little to be beautiful, comely, and gentle when they do not have Fortune with them, which seems to mean that in Love one needs, no less than in other things, to have luck and good fortune; and alas, it does mean this.

But to this must be added also that Fortune must carry the cornucopia with her, because without it she will be of little help to Love, thanks to the avaricious female mind, which regards neither beauty, nor virtue, nor gentility, but bends only to precious gifts. Hence one can say with assurance that whoever has gold, silver, and precious gems, all gifts of Fortune and shown by the cornucopia, will always be fortunate and happy in love.

> Image of Fortune at Aegira, signifying that in Love good fortune and riches are wanted, otherwise the desired wish is not obtained. But I believe that, in this as in all actions, one must have virtues, for these make good Fortune, because there is no Fortune at all, but it is an imagined name.

<aside>To women</aside>
<aside>Admonition</aside>
<aside>Against avaricious women</aside>

Forgive me, women, that zeal for your honour now forces me to speak with you in this way, far more than the harm which I have already felt several times from your avaricious desires. Are you not ashamed (and I speak only to those who do it) to give yourselves for a price no differently from the way beasts are sold? Although, like them, you remain in the free power of whoever buys you, you do come back again, so that you can give yourselves now to one and now to another, according to which offers the greater price; but your honour and your good name remain always a prey to infamy, to blame, and to shame.

And if perhaps you were to say to me: what does it matter more that we are shameless for a price than for love alone? In either case, for this as for that, we lose our honour, which you men have confined within the narrowest limits, so that if we are willing to stay within them there will be no love for us: and how then do you want us to set about doing your pleasures for love? I would answer you that there are some deeds which, though perhaps not very good in themselves, yet brought to their end by means of virtue, content him who does them, and are also for the most part praised; whereas he who acts viciously neither contents himself while he stays hidden, nor, when he is made known, finds anyone who praises him.

Love is a virtue, and Avarice is a vice. Therefore what you do for love not only does not trouble your mind, aware of having acted virtuously, but is praised besides by whoever knows of it. But what avaricious desire draws you to goads you always and never gives you rest, so that you always feel a certain remorse which says to you: ah, you did wrong after all. And when it is afterwards learned by others, from gentle and honoured women you become vile and infamous, and often the name of honest gentlewoman is changed to that of shameless harlot, which never happens to her who for love pleases him who loves her; for only those who do it for hire are called harlots.

The limits set to your honour are not at all as narrow as some of you perhaps think, that love is forbidden to you; on the contrary, it is given to you as your own, since by yourselves, without a man, you are worth little. And how will you approach a man with pleasure to both, if love does not come between to bind you together? Therefore Love is not taken from you: but do you know what is taken from you? To do injury to Love, as many do, bargaining over that which they should do for his sake alone.

So it is not for love, nor because, overcome by human frailty, they cannot resist carnal passions (things which very well cover and excuse our errors), that they throw themselves into the arms of the one they make a show of loving, but because they are too greedy and rapacious, and think that by giving themselves to many, in order to have from many, they can better fill their avaricious and insatiable desires. And therefore anyone who has something to give can easily enjoy them.

For this reason, then, Cupid is joined to Fortune, who holds the cornucopia, and this shows also the little firmness of such women, because avaricious women are no less changeable in love than Fortune is. To her image I return, and I leave you, women, who live in your shameful errors; and to those who are far from them I promise to say one day all the good in the world of them, and in such a way that perhaps they will even set some store by it.

<aside>Gregorio Giraldi</aside>

So, besides the designs of Fortune made up to here, I find that some have painted her at sea, where she sails among troubled waves; some have set her on the sharp top of a high rock or of a mountain, so that every little wind that blows makes her turn. And I believe that these were modern paintings, because I find no mention of them made by the ancients, as was likewise the case with this one, which Giraldi reports, writing on the gentiles, where he says thus:

> Some in our times, with a quite beautiful invention, have depicted Fortune on horseback, running away at great speed, and Fate, or Destiny, as it seems one should call it, follows her, holding, like an archer, the bow with the arrow to wound her. This painting shows the speed of Fortune, as though she never rested, but ran away always, driven off by Fate, because where Destiny is, Fortune has no place.
>
> *(Giraldi)*

> Image of Fortune on horseback running swiftly, followed by Fate and Destiny, denoting her speed, and that where these are, she has no power or firmness.

<aside>Apuleius</aside>

Apuleius makes her one and the same with Isis, when he pretends that to him, returned from an ass to a man, the priest of the goddess speaks thus:

> Now you are under the protection of Fortune, not of that one who is blind, but of the one who sees, and gives light to the other gods as well with her splendour.
>
> *(Apuleius)*

<aside>Fortune as the Moon</aside>

And we can say that he therefore meant good Fortune; under her name Macrobius understood the Moon, shown through Isis, as has already been said in her image, because she can do much in the bodies down here, which are subject to the various chances of fortune and go on changing continually. Putting the Moon and Fortune together, then, as though they were one and the same goddess, from whom come the birth and death of things, we may say that Pausanias was not at all mistaken when he said that Pindar would easily make him believe that Fortune was one of the Parcae, and could do much more than her sisters. Though it seems to me that the Parcae agree much more with Fate, or Destiny, whichever we wish to call it, than with Fortune, because this is fixed and certain, just as they too are immutable, since in spinning the life of mortals they assign to each the determined time of dying.

<aside>Bonus Eventus</aside>

But what has this to do with images? Nothing. Let us leave it, then, and speak of Bonus Eventus, that is, prosperous outcome and happy end of undertakings, for the idol of him among the Romans stood on the Capitol with that of good Fortune, as Pliny writes, in the form of a cheerful and well-dressed young man, who held in his right hand a cup and in his left an ear of corn and a poppy.

<aside>Favour</aside>

And with Fortune goes Favour too, who was likewise worshipped by the ancients, because he seems to come for the most part from her, although he is born many times of beauty also, and often of virtue, and in sum of all those things that make us pleasing to others and win us favour, which often makes us grow proud. For the more things succeed happily for men, the more they raise themselves, and, soaring on the wings of human favour, climb above others until the wheel turns; then, falling headlong, they are despised no less than they were revered before.

<aside>Timid Favour</aside>

Therefore let everyone beware of trusting too much in this frail and light Favour, because he soon passes, as his image shows us, which was that of a youth with wings: either because by prosperous and joyful things he rises so high that he no longer deigns to look down, so that he was also painted blind, since men seem to look no more at anyone, or very little, once they are raised to great honours; or because he stays little with us, but soon passes away. And therefore he stood with his feet on a wheel, since he imitates Fortune; and as she turns, so he turns likewise, and goes always wherever she carries her goods, showing himself nevertheless timid, because he wants every hour to climb higher than is fitting for him, pushed by Flattery, who always accompanies him.

Behind him goes Envy as well, but with slow and sluggish steps; she always looks with a crooked eye on the Felicity of others, but Felicity is blessed, and does not fear her at all.

> Image of Bonus Eventus and happy success, of unstable, light, and fleeting Favour, of Flattery and of Envy, who push and accompany the said Favour, and the image of the revolving wheel of Fortune, on which the said Favour rests his feet and falls when it turns, an effect that is ordinarily seen at courts and in the world.

<aside>Macaria</aside>

For Felicity too was likewise worshipped by the ancients, and called Macaria by the Greeks; she was, as is gathered from Euripides and as Pausanias reports, a daughter of Hercules, and she acquired divine honours because the oracle had answered the Athenians that they could be victors in a certain war moved against them by the Lacedaemonians over the children of Hercules, if one of these, killing himself of his own accord, offered himself to the gods of the underworld. As soon as she heard this, she cut her throat and made of herself the wretched offering, gaining the victory for the Athenians, who therefore afterwards worshipped her as one through whom they had been victorious and happy.

<aside>Medals of Julia Mamaea</aside>

Her image, that is, of Felicity (for this is the Latin name, and Macaria the Greek, as I have said), was depicted by the ancients, as is seen on some medals of Julia Mamaea, as a woman on a handsome seat, who holds the caduceus in her right hand and has a cornucopia in her left. We can say that the former signifies virtue, the latter riches, as though neither virtues by themselves nor riches by themselves could make a man happy here, which was the opinion of Aristotle. For what felicity can there be for a virtuous man who finds himself in such poverty that he suffers want not only of many things that would be convenient for him, but of those also that are necessary to him? And on the contrary, he who is deprived of all virtue, even if he had all the riches of the world, can never be called happy, but will be most unhappy, having nothing of what is proper to man. Only those, then, can be called happy here among us, according to the opinion of Aristotle, and as the image of Felicity just drawn shows us, who are virtuous and rich, that is, who have so much of the goods of Fortune that they can provide for their needs and comforts.

Cebes, in his tablet, depicts Felicity as a woman who sits at the entrance of a certain fortress on a handsome seat, well adorned but not with much art, and crowned with most beautiful and pleasing flowers. Everyone seems to want to go to her, but only those arrive who walk under the guidance of virtue, leaving all other things behind them; for it was his opinion, as it was of many others before him, that virtue alone could make a man happy.

This we must say too, speaking as Christians and meaning not the felicity which everyone here blindly craves in this way, because it is not felicity, though it seems so, but that which the blessed souls enjoy in the heavenly seats, true, immutable, and eternal. Everyone may firmly hope to reach it who, guided by the most lucid rays of divine goodness, walks the whole journey of this world in the company of Faith, treading the dry and barren ground with the feet of Charity.

> Image of the goddess Macaria, or goddess Felicity, daughter of Hercules, with the caduceus and the cornucopia in her hands, the former signifying virtue, the latter riches, both necessary to human felicity.
