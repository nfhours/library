<aside>Love</aside>

Of all the feelings of our souls there is none more common, more beautiful, or of greater power than the one that is seen to exist not only in us but also in eternal God (although in him it is pure substance only, not a feeling or a passion), in the angels and in all the orders of the blessed, in each of the elements, and in all the things that are created from them. It is commonly called Love. It removes all foulness from human souls and makes them so beautiful that they then have the courage to go and place themselves before eternal beauty, where, filled with joy and infinite pleasure, they enjoy the desired fruits of their loves. It makes the proud humble, brings the angry to peace, cheers and comforts the afflicted and the disconsolate, gives courage to the fearful, and opens the closed hands of greedy avarice. It has power over the most powerful kings, surpasses great emperors, and in short makes itself obeyed by all people.

For these reasons it is no wonder that the ancients placed Love among their gods. Not yet having seen the light of truth, they gave to creatures what was owed to the Creator of all, and, as if they did not know where the virtues come to us from, they worshipped many of them as gods, set up various statues to them, and depicted them in various images, according to how they work in human souls, as I have already shown elsewhere. So as not to repeat the same thing now, I want to speak only of Cupid, as he was depicted by the ancients.

Although it now seems so evident to everyone that there is no need to write about it in order to teach it (for on seeing a boy with a blindfold over his eyes, a bow in his hand and a quiver full of arrows at his side, everyone can say, “This is Cupid”), still, not everyone will then be able to answer, when asked, the reason why he is so represented. And in these images of mine I wanted to show not only how the ancients depicted him but also to give the reasons for it, as far as I have been able to find them in the most worthy writers. In discussing Cupid, they considered him in different ways and from different angles, because they saw that his powers are various.

<aside>There is not just one Cupid</aside>
<aside>Heavenly Cupid</aside>

Hence it is that they have said there is not one Cupid but many; Plato in particular posited two, just as he likewise posited two Venuses. One of the two Venuses is heavenly, and from her was born the heavenly Cupid, that divine Love which raises the human soul to the contemplation of God, of the separate minds that we call angels, and of the things of Heaven.

<aside>Cupid’s wings</aside>
<aside>Cupid’s arrows</aside>

The heavenly Cupid dwells in the heavens, as Philostratus writes, saying that the heavenly Cupid, who is one, stays in Heaven and there looks after heavenly things; he is wholly pure, clean and most sincere, and therefore he is depicted with a youthful body, all bright and beautiful. Wings are given to him to show the turning that human souls make, moved by loving desire, towards Heaven and what is there; as do likewise those pure minds that are ordered above the heavens, all according to their degrees, which raise themselves as much as they can to the sight of that blessed face which is the eternal fountain of all beauty. That face sends its rays in various ways from the highest part of Heaven to stir and provoke all things to turn towards it, and these are the bolts and sharp arrows that Cupid often looses.

<aside>Cupid’s wings</aside>
<aside>Cupid’s arrows</aside>
<aside>Petrarch</aside>

Whoever, then, considers divine Love in the image of Cupid sees the purity of that Love in the bright body of the image. Through the wings (whose office is to lift up and carry through the air those bodies that could not rise from the earth by themselves) he sees the lifting that Love performs of our souls towards the divine beauties. And by the arrows he can understand the rays of the divine light, which comes to wound us in a thousand ways so that we turn to it and, enamoured of its beauty, value the things down here only insofar as they are a ladder for us to climb to Heaven, as Cupid himself said well of himself when Petrarch, in one of his canzoni, summons him to judgement:

> Furthermore, and this surpasses all the rest,
> I had given him wings to fly above Heaven
> through mortal things,
> which are a ladder to the Maker for whoever esteems them rightly.
>
> *(Petrarch)*

<aside>Love is like the sun</aside>

So as not to go deeper into the matters of divine Love (for there would be so much to say that I should stray too far from my purpose), I add only this: that it is like the sun. The sun scatters its rays throughout the universe and also reflects other rays back into itself, if it happens to touch bright and pure bodies. And as the sun warms wherever it touches, so Love kindles those souls it approaches, so that with inflamed desire they turn to the things of Heaven.

It is for this reason that the image of Cupid has also been given a lit torch: to show the ardent effect with which we follow the things we love, continually drawing pleasure from them (speaking, however, only of divine things). In the torch of Cupid we consider only that which gives light and shines, as pleasant and joyful to behold, not that which burns and scorches, because that hurts and is troublesome; and this suits better the love of earthly things, which never gives delight or any complete pleasure that is without torment, but joins the one to the other, as in a torch the splendour that delights and the flame that torments by burning are together.

<aside>Seneca</aside>

This was then the opinion of Plutarch, who writes that the poets, sculptors and painters imagined that Cupid carried a lit torch in his hand because, of fire, what gives light is most delightful, but what burns is exceedingly troublesome. He took this, with others, perhaps from Plato, who writes in the *Timaeus* that Love in us is mixed of pleasure and pain. This Cupid was born of Vulcan and of the other Venus, whom Plato calls common, worldly and earthly; and he is likewise common, earthly and full of human lasciviousness, as the fables imagined. Hence Seneca, describing him in the tragedy *Octavia*, speaks thus:

> The error of blind and wretched mortals,
> to cover its foolish and vain desire,
> pretends that Cupid is a god;
> so it seems to delight in its deceit.
> In appearance very pleasing, but so wicked
> that he takes pleasure only in the ills of others,
> he is said to have wings at his shoulders,
> his hands armed with bow and arrows,
> and, bound into a small torch,
> to carry the flames that he then goes
> scattering through the universe, so that from his ardour
> every heart is left set on fire;
> and, differing little from human custom,
> to have been born of Vulcan and of Venus
> and to hold the most sublime station of Heaven.
> Love is a fault of the unsound mind,
> when it moves from its own place;
> with a pleasing fire
> it warms the soul, and is born in the green years,
> at the age that can do much but sees little.
> Idleness feeds it, and human wantonness,
> while far away goes
> evil Fortune with her heavy harms,
> spreading her sad wings,
> and good and happy Fortune stays near,
> offering what she holds in her rich bosom.
> But if the latter fails,
> so that blind desire gives in to evil,
> the fire that burned before is wholly quenched
> and Love soon loses all its strength.
>
> *(Seneca)*

<aside>Ovid</aside>

Ovid likewise posited two Cupids, when he said:

> Mother of both Cupids, give me help.
>
> *(Ovid)*

<aside>Anteros</aside>

For we love in two ways: well, when we apply the soul to good things, badly, when we follow what is evil. And as this latter is called dishonest and ugly love, so the former is called beautiful and honest. Some hold that of these two born of Venus only one is Cupid, who kindles and inflames our souls to follow something, and that the other is called Anteros, which we can call counter-love, because he produces effects entirely contrary to those of the first, so that through him we flee from things, cease to love them, and hate them. But whoever believes such a thing is greatly mistaken, because Anteros was worshipped not because he made people cease to love but because he punished whoever does not love when loved, as is read in Suda, who tells a short tale of this kind.

<aside>Tale of Meliton and Timagoras</aside>

There was in Athens a man called Meliton, who loved most ardently a very beautiful youth, noble and very rich, whose name was Timagoras. Timagoras, no less haughty than handsome, showed that he took no account of Meliton except in commanding him things of the gravest danger, all of which the poor fellow did with the steadiest spirit, believing that in this way he would gain the favour of the beloved youth; but everything turned out the opposite, for the more Timagoras felt himself loved and served by him, the more he always scorned him. Then the unhappy Meliton, unable to bear the pangs of love any longer and overcome by despair, threw himself down from the highest summit of the citadel, and was broken all over and remained dead; at which it seemed that so great a pity then came upon Timagoras, when he learnt of it (perhaps because the justice of love did not wish Meliton’s death to remain unavenged), that he went straight to throw himself from the place from which Meliton had first thrown himself, and died cruelly.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

And there, accordingly, an idol was set up of a most beautiful young man, quite naked, who held two cocks in his hand, very beautiful ones, and threw himself headlong. This, then, we can say was a punishment that came from Anteros, as Pausanias says more openly, relating almost the same story in this way.

In Athens there was an altar consecrated to Anteros, by a vow, as they say, of foreigners, and for this reason. Meletus, an Athenian youth, took no account of Timagoras, a foreigner who loved him greatly, and told him one day, rather disdainfully, to get away from him and go and break his neck. Timagoras, caring no more to live and wishing in all things to please the one he loved so much, let himself fall from the high top of a certain crag, and died wretchedly; at which Meletus, repenting of his pride, felt such displeasure that, not long afterwards, in a frenzy, he came to the same end that his lover had; so it was said that Anteros had taken vengeance for Timagoras, and the altar I mentioned was therefore consecrated to him.

<aside>Porphyry</aside>

Anteros was, then, a numen who punished whoever does not love when loved, and not one who made people cease to love; and we can say that he is nothing other than reciprocal love, as is also confirmed by Porphyry, writing of him in this way.

Venus had borne Cupid some days before, when she noticed that he did not grow at all, but remained as small as he was born; not knowing how to remedy this, she asked the oracle’s advice about it, and the oracle answered that Cupid, remaining alone, would never grow, and that a brother had to be made for him, so that love might be mutual between them, and then Cupid would grow as much as was needed. Venus, putting faith in the oracle’s words, soon afterwards bore Anteros, who was no sooner born than he began, level with Cupid, to grow, to put out wings and to walk vigorously. And the lot of these two has been such that seldom or never is one without the other, and if Cupid sees that Anteros grows and becomes large, he wishes to show himself bigger, and if he sees him small, he too becomes small, though he often does this against his will. So love grows when it is placed in a person who likewise loves, and the one who is loved must likewise love; and this the ancients showed by Cupid and Anteros. For this reason the Eleans, a people of Greece, put the one and the other in a certain part of their schools, so that the young men would remember not to be ungrateful to those who loved them, but to return love, loving others as they felt themselves loved by others.

Two images, or statues, of boys stood there: one was Cupid, who held a palm branch in his hand, and the other Anteros, who tried to take it from him and seemed to labour hard at it, yet could not; as if the one who responds in love must show with all his strength that he loves no less than the one who loved first, and for this reason Anteros strives to take the palm from Cupid’s hand.

<aside>M. Tullius</aside>

Speaking of Cupid, M. Tullius, to flatter his Atticus, as Lactantius reports, and as if to jest with him, said that the Greeks were men of great judgement and very bold opinion, to set before the eyes of the young, where they had to exercise themselves in virtuous things, the image of Cupid; as if he believed that with that image one could awaken in young minds lasciviousness and dishonest pleasures, which the ancients said all come from Cupid, no less than kindle them to virtue.

> Image of the gods Eros and Anteros, brothers and sons of Venus, understood, the one as loving, the other as loving in return, or reciprocal love; and the image of Lethean Love, which makes one cease to love and forget the beloved person.

<aside>Mercury and Hercules with Cupid</aside>

Wishing perhaps to remedy this, the Romans placed in their academies, and wherever the young men exercised, not Cupid alone but together with him also Mercury and Hercules, so that the statue of Cupid stood in the middle between these two: to show that he was reasonable and virtuous, because Hercules showed virtue, and Mercury reason. And Athenaeus writes that the ancient philosophers held Cupid to be a very grave god, alien to all ugliness, saying that this could be known from the fact that they set his statue with those of Mercury and Hercules, who preside, the one over eloquence, the other over fortitude; and from the company of these there is born Friendship and Concord.

<aside>Lethean Love</aside>

The ancients also had a Love that made people cease to love and consigned to oblivion all the good that was wished to another; it was called Lethean Love. His statue, which lowered the burning torches into the river and there extinguished them [?1], was in the temple of Venus Erycina, of which Ovid made mention, saying that all the young men who wished to forget their sweethearts went there to offer devout prayers, and likewise the young women who realised they had ill placed their loves. The Greeks had a better remedy for this; for without praying to anyone, merely by washing in the river Selemnus, not far from Patras, a city of Achaea, men and women forgot all those loves of which they no longer wished to be reminded, for so the people of that country held it to be.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>
<aside>Pliny</aside>

But Pausanias, who tells this, says that it is a fable: if it were true, the waters of that river would be valued above all the riches of the world. And Pliny mentions a certain spring called the spring of Cupid among the Cyzicenes, from which whoever drank at once forgot every amorous feeling.

<aside>Many Cupids</aside>
<aside>Cupid</aside>

But if Cupid is nothing other than the affectionate desire that we place on things, then there will be not one Cupid, nor two, but many, as the poets hold, who in their fables often express the powers of our souls, the various passions and their various feelings. For this reason they said that the Cupids were many, as Alexander also writes in his *Problems*, because we do not all love one and the same thing, nor in one and the same way, but each one loves differently, and often different things too: which could not be done if Cupid were only one.

<aside>Propertius</aside>

The ancients, then, imagined that there were many; they depicted all of them as most beautiful little boys with wings and put in their hands, for some, burning little torches, for others, the sharpest arrows, and for others, the strongest snares, as Propertius shows very well, writing to his Cynthia, in these words in our language:

> As the other night, my life,
> I was going wandering after supper,
> without even one in company,
> fate, I do not know how, led me
> to where a troop came to meet me
> of boys who seemed barely born.
> How many they were I do not know, for I could not
> count them, because of the fear that went into my heart
> and made me think of my own situation.
> Nor was it fitting to feel no fear
> of them, although they were little ones;
> for they are very great at giving others pain.
> They all showed their little naked bodies
> so charming, so beautiful and well formed
> that I never saw more beautiful boys.
> And some of them were armed
> with living flames gathered into little torches,
> by which every day many are burned.
> Some, with slender arms, free
> and ready for shooting, carried arrows
> that have already wounded my heart more than once.
> And some others carried certain snares, which
> they showed they had only for binding me,
> for one of them spoke such words:
> “Seize this man, come on, what are you standing there for?
> You know him well,” and they were at once
> around me; I could not escape,
> so that, bound by them, I remain in your hand.
>
> *(Propertius)*

<aside>Philostratus</aside>
<aside>Painting of the Cupids</aside>

Philostratus likewise says in his *Paintings* that the Cupids are many, and represents them as children of the nymphs, as Claudian also does when he writes of the wedding of Honorius and Maria; they govern mortals, because the things that these love are likewise many. And he paints a beautiful picture of them, which is as follows, according to the description that I have been able to draw from it.

There is a most beautiful garden with lovely shrubs planted in such order that from every side they show to those who look a fairly spacious path, all covered with the freshest grass, so soft and delicate that on nothing else could one lie more delicately. From the branches of the beautiful trees hang apples, yellow and shining, so that they seem to be of gold; towards these all the Cupids, quite naked, turn, or fly about them, very lightly, having already hung their gilded quivers, full of pricking arrows, on the trees; and some garments of various colours are thrown there on the grass, which is full of various flowers. Their golden hair serves the Cupids as wreaths; and the feathers of their wings are not all of one colour, but some are red, some yellow, and some sky-blue.

Of them, four, the most beautiful, have moved apart from the others: two, playing, throw apples to each other in turn, and the other two shoot at each other, not showing at all in their faces that they are angry; on the contrary, each of them offers his naked breast, so that the arrows do not go in vain but wound where they are aimed. These things show the beginning of Love and its confirmation, for the two who play with the apples give Love its beginning; so one sees that one of them kisses the apple and throws it, and the other stands with his hands raised to catch it, signifying that he too will kiss it when he has caught it, and will send it back likewise.

<aside>Virgil</aside>

And from this perhaps Suda took what he writes: that to throw an apple at someone signifies inviting him to love. So too in one of his pastorals Virgil gives Damoetas these words:

> Lovely Galatea throws an apple at me
> and then runs away, but before she hides
> among the green willows she still wants me to see her.
>
> *(Virgil)*

The other two, who shoot at each other, confirm the Love already begun, as if they made it penetrate to the heart. The first pair, then, play in order to begin to love, and these shoot so that Love may be confirmed and persevere. Then there is a hare, which sits under a tree eating apples already fallen to the ground; the Cupids hunt it and frighten it, one clapping his hands together, another shouting, and another shaking the garment that was on the ground.

Some fly above it and cry out at it, some go softly after its track, and some dart forward as if they wanted to throw themselves on it; but the animal turns to another side, where one of the Cupids is lying in ambush, thinking to catch it by a foot with his hands, and another, who had already almost caught it, sees it slip out of his hand. At this they all laugh so much that with laughter they cannot keep on their feet but let themselves fall to the ground, some sideways, some face down, and some looking up at the sky.

<aside>The hare suits Venus</aside>

Yet none of them wants to use the pricking arrows; all would like to catch that animal alive, in order to make of it a most welcome sacrifice to Venus, as though the hare suited her very well, because they say it is most frequent in coupling, so that while it suckles the young already born it makes others, and is always becoming pregnant again, so that the hare bears young at all times, as Pliny writes; nor is the male known from the female, but it is believed that in all of them there is the same power, in the male as in the female. Moreover, the same Pliny says that some believed that the flesh of the hare made whoever ate it for seven days much more beautiful and more graceful than he was before; and he adds that he does indeed believe it to be a vain thing, but that one may nevertheless think there is some reason in it, since it is so universally believed.

<aside>Martial</aside>

From this Martial took occasion to make fun of a friend of his called Gellia, writing her this epigram:

> When you, my Gellia, sometimes send me
> a hare as a gift, you send also to tell me
> that in seven days I shall see (hour by hour)
> that I become more handsome by eating it.
> If this is true, my life, it would be
> true also, and one could swear without lying,
> that you have never eaten
> the flesh of hare since you were born.
>
> *(Martial)*

<aside>Alexander Severus</aside>

And since Alexander Severus often ate hare, there was someone who made fun of him in some verses, as Lampridius writes, saying that, although he was Syrian by race, it was no wonder that he was handsome and graceful, because the flesh of the hare, which he ate with pleasure, made him so. Moreover, there have been some who said that there is in the hare a certain something with which amorous charms can be made; Philostratus, who reports it, does not say that this is not so, but he condemns whoever does it, and judges those who want to make themselves loved by force in this way unworthy of being loved. And here his picture ends.

In it, it seems to me, the Cupids are very well painted, and I have described it only so that it may be seen that the Cupids are many, and all of them little naked boys, with curly, blond hair and wings of various colours, and sometimes they have lit torches in their hands and sometimes not, and sometimes they have the bow and the quiver with arrows, and sometimes are without them.

<aside>Silius Italicus</aside>

So Silius Italicus, describing how the Cupids accompanied Venus when she went with Pallas and Juno to judgement before Paris, gives the bow and arrows to only one, and has the others stand around her, adorning her; and his verses, rendered into the vernacular, are these:

> Then fair Cupid, who had awaited
> the time of the great contest,
> guided with his right hand the white swans
> yoked to his mother’s chariot;
> to her he shows the bow that hangs
> from his shoulders, and the little quiver
> full of pricking arrows for her alone,
> signalling to her that for this reason she should not fear
> for the victory, but go sure of it.
> And the other Cupids, charming and merry,
> are around her: one gathers and ties
> the fair hair from her white brow
> in pretty knots, another smooths
> her thin garment, and another girds her where needed.
>
> *(Silius Italicus)*

<aside>Apuleius</aside>
<aside>Cupids’ snares</aside>

When Apuleius brings Venus on the stage accompanied by the Cupids, he says that these are very white boys, who descend from Heaven or come out of the sea with wings at their shoulders, with arrows at their side, and with little torches in their hands. And to show their multitude, he says elsewhere that a whole people of Cupids accompanied Venus, since human desires are almost infinite, and as much as one desires, so much one loves, seldom considering whether it is good or bad, but only taking care to satisfy every desire of ours, even if disorderly and against reason, which Cupid does not value, while he turns wholly to lascivious pleasures; and therefore he binds us so that we remain in his power: and this is shown by the snares that are given to him.

<aside>Cupid is younger than the other gods</aside>

But enough of the many; let us now speak of one Cupid only, making a portrait of him according to the example the ancients have left us. In his *Symposium*, where Agathon praises Cupid and shows what he is like, Plato speaks thus: Cupid is most beautiful, because he is the youngest of all the gods; and that this is true is shown by the fact that he always flees old age, although it is very swift and often comes sooner than would be necessary, and by his nature he hates it and stays among the young, according to the proverb that says that like things gladly keep company.

<aside>Cupid is tender and soft</aside>
<aside>Ate</aside>

He is also tender and soft, and this is proved in the way Homer proves that Ate has tender and soft feet. Ate is a Greek word, and we can call it calamity; but Homer pictures her as a goddess, daughter of Jupiter, who troubles the minds of mortals and puts evil in their hearts, and he says that she walks on the heads of men and never treads the earth with her feet, and therefore they are soft and tender. So Cupid too is tender and soft, because he never walks on the earth, or on stones, or in any place that is hard and rough; he thrusts himself among the softest and most delicate things in the world and stays there.

These are human souls; yet he does not dwell in all of them, but only in those that are agreeable and gentle, and he flees the coarse and hard. All hardness is so far from him that he is almost liquid, like water; for if it were not so, he could not go, as he does, searching through the whole soul, nor enter it in secret and leave it when he wishes.

<aside>Cupid among flowers</aside>

Besides this, Cupid has a most well-made body and is so well composed in every part that his beauty surpasses all others, and therefore there is great discord between ugliness and him; and he has over his whole person a colour so beautiful and so lovely that a better cannot be seen. Proof of this is that one often sees him dwelling, almost always, among flowers; indeed, where there are no flowers he never dwells, and therefore all souls and bodies are deprived of him that are without the flowers of youth and beauty; for Cupid does not wish to stay anywhere but in places that are beautiful, flourishing, fragrant and cheerful.

Many other things could be said about the beauty of Cupid, but for now Plato says no more; from him we can gather that Cupid is young, tender, soft and delicate, well made in body, and of a very good colour.

Apuleius painted him in more detail in the tale of Psyche, when he relates that she, against the command she had received from him, stands with the lamp in her hand gazing at him. And she saw him such that he has his golden hair all wet with ambrosia poured over his whitest neck, his cheeks coloured so that they seem purple, and the beautiful curls, twisted or crisped in various ways, hang partly over his whitest shoulders and partly spread over his beautiful face, and are so glossy and shine so much that they do not let the light of the lamp above them appear; on his shoulders he has two wings sprinkled with the freshest dew, whose light feathers, although the wings are still, move gently, as if touched by a most gentle wind; and the whole body is then so clean and shining that Venus has no cause to repent of having borne him; the bow, the quiver and the arrows lie there on the ground before the bed.

<aside>Petrarch</aside>

Apuleius does not blindfold him, perhaps because there was no need, since he was then asleep, or because he sided with those who do not represent him as blind, like Petrarch, when he writes that he saw him in the eyes of his lady, and says:

> Not blind, but with a quiver I see him,
> naked, except as far as shame veils him,
> a boy with wings, not painted but alive.
>
> *(Petrarch)*

<aside>Moschus</aside>
<aside>The Fugitive Cupid</aside>
<aside>Luigi Alamanni</aside>

And Moschus, a Greek poet, likewise depicts him with bright and inflamed eyes, when he imagines that Venus goes looking for him, and she describes him wholly, so that whoever finds him may recognise him, seize him and bring him back; to whom she promises to give a kiss afterwards, and a still greater reward. This was put into Latin by Poliziano, and then rendered into the vernacular by many, but better than the others, it seems to me, M. Luigi Alamanni has done it, turning it into certain even verses that go two by two. Therefore, besides that I should not have known how, I did not even wish to try to do better than he, and, so as not to do worse [?2], I have used his translation. This, then, is the *Fugitive Cupid* of Moschus, as he gave that name to his verses, rendered into the vernacular by Alamanni:

> Venus went looking for her son Cupid
> and called, saying along every shore:
> “To whoever shows me Cupid, who has fled from me,
> I give a kiss as reward, and to whoever dares
> to bring him back to me, I promise and swear
> that I shall give much more than one pure kiss.
> The boy has such marks, and such trappings,
> that at his first appearing they will be plain.
> His colour is not white, but seems like fire,
> his eyes burning, and moving, and full of play.
> Sweet his voice and speech, cruel his heart,
> nor would he inwardly wish what he shows outwardly.
> A liar, disloyal, and if he grows angry,
> he breathes fury, flame, poison and rage.
> A traitor, a stripling, deceitful, and he plays
> always to the harm of others with snare or whip.
> His brow is covered with locks, and his face fierce.
> His arm small and slender, but nimble and free,
> so that he can hurl a sharp dart far,
> right down to low Acheron, into the arms of Pluto.
> He has his thought veiled, his body naked,
> winged like a bird, bold and cruel [?3].
> Now at this one, now at that, he directs his flight,
> and in the midst of hearts he dwells alone.
> A little bow he has in hand, and on it there is always
> a pricking bolt of bitter temper.
> The shaft is short, but it wounds even Heaven;
> a golden quiver hangs from his shoulders
> and in it are the wicked arrows by which I too at times
> was wounded, and am still in pain.
> Harsh to all, and cruel, but as I see,
> the disloyal one always does worse to his own.
> A little torch he has in hand, which I have often seen
> set Neptune himself ablaze in the waters.
> If you can, seize him again and lead him by force,
> and have no pity if you see him remain
> weeping in pain; watch closely
> that he does not flee at that moment, if he moves to laughter,
> but then hold him tighter. If he wants to kiss you,
> flee, because his lips in every part
> are full of poison. If he should say,
> ‘Take these arms of mine, go away with them,’
> do not dare to touch; refuse the gift:
> flame, plague, torment and death they are.”
>
> *(Moschus, in Alamanni’s version)*

> Triumph of Love, described by Philostratus, Petrarch and other ancient and modern writers, signifying the power of Love.

<aside>Petrarch</aside>

This design touches on a good part of the force and effects of Love, and therefore it represents him as red in colour, and as if kindled over all his body; from which perhaps Petrarch took the example when he placed him on a chariot of fire, making him triumph, where he says:

> I saw a victorious and supreme leader,
> just like one of those who to the Capitol
> lead the triumphal chariot in great glory.
> Four steeds, far whiter than snow;
> upon a chariot of fire a cruel youth
> with bow in hand and arrows at his sides:
> against which neither helmet nor shield avails;
> upon his shoulders he had only two great wings
> of a thousand colours, and all the rest naked;
> around him innumerable mortals,
> some taken in battle, some slain,
> some wounded by pricking arrows.
> What should I say? I pass over it in a single step:
> all the gods of Varro are prisoners here;
> and, burdened with innumerable snares,
> chained Jupiter comes before the chariot.
> This is he whom the world calls Love;
> bitter, as you see, and you will see it better,
> when he is yours, as he is our Lord;
> a gentle child and a fierce old man;
> he was born of idleness and human wantonness,
> nourished by sweet and gentle thoughts,
> made lord and god by vain people.
> One is slain by him; another, under heavier
> laws, leads his harsh and bitter life
> under a thousand chains and a thousand keys.
>
> *(Petrarch)*

<aside>Question</aside>

This shows the ardent desire of lovers, which, accompanied by hope, is rekindled and inflamed ever more, as Alexander says in one of his questions, where he asks why the extremities of the bodies of lovers are sometimes cold and sometimes hot; and he holds that the cause of all this is fear and hope. For since the heart is the seat and fountain of life, and sends through the whole body the spirits that give it strength and vivacity, every time it is oppressed by some pain, not only can it send no more vigour to the distant parts, but it even recalls to itself what it has already sent, in order to be stronger to bear the pain that oppresses it.

But who feels greater pain than he who fears that he will not obtain what he so longs for, and therefore will never be happy? So it is no wonder if the extremities of his body are sometimes cold. They become hot when he hopes to have what he desires, for the heart, because of the joy it then feels, opens as it were and dilates, and sends to the distant parts signs of its joy, which are most lively spirits that warm the whole body and give it colour, as we said earlier of Cupid.

<aside>Blushing in lovers</aside>

Though some hold that the redness in lovers comes rather from shame, as if the soul, conscious to itself of departing from honesty when it attends only to bodily pleasures and desires only those, wished to hide; and therefore, as though covering with a coloured veil the part where it shows itself most, it spreads redness over the face.

But it seems to me that that poet, or whoever he was, revealed very well the power and nature of Love, who in a sonnet goes on describing what he is, concluding in the end that it is impossible to draw the true interpretation from it. The sonnet, because it is artful and charming, moves me to set it down below, and it goes thus.

> Love is a something I know not what, that comes I know not from where;
> sent by I know not whom, I know not in what way,
> born, I cannot say how, or by what deceit;
> in itself it is confused, and confuses others.
> Here it grazes, and is nourished from elsewhere,
> lives I know not on what, does not prize praise [?4],
> glories in pain, has in itself no measure.
> And I do not know how, now it shows itself, now hides.
> It wounds, I know not how, in the middle of the heart,
> and no wound, nor mark, nor blood appears,
> and the one wounded by it, living, dies.
> With the heart, not with the tongue, it makes one speak,
> and is silent within, and then silence outside.
> Now who can interpret this madman?

<aside>Interpretation of Cupid</aside>
<aside>Virgil</aside>

The parts of Cupid with all his attributes are interpreted thus by Servius, in the place where in Virgil Venus begs him to transform himself into Ascanius, when he is to be taken to Dido. Cupid is depicted as a child, because he is nothing other than a mad desire, while it is intent only on lust; and because the speech of lovers is as broken and imperfect as that of children, which Virgil shows in Dido, when he says:

> She begins at times to speak,
> and in the midst of speaking, wretched, stops.
>
> *(Virgil)*

<aside>Terence</aside>

He has wings, then, to show the lightness of lovers, quick to change their will, as can be seen in the same Dido, who in Virgil even thinks of putting to death the man she first loved so much. And Terence showed very well the little steadfastness of lovers when he said:

> All these evils are in love: injuries, suspicions, enmities, truce, war, and then peace again.
>
> *(Terence)*

<aside>Petrarch</aside>

Hence Petrarch, after he has recounted various and diverse amorous feelings, concludes thus:

> In sum, I know how inconstant and wavering,
> timid, bold, is the life of lovers,
> where a little sweetness makes up for much bitterness.
>
> *(Petrarch)*

<aside>The power of Love</aside>

Cupid carries arrows either because these likewise are swift and do not always go to wound where they are aimed, as we have said of lovers, who are very quick to change their will and cannot always reach what they most longed for; or because, as they are sharp and prick, so the pricks of conscience after sinning pierce our soul, which after the deed knows that it has acted ill. Or else the arrows of Cupid are understood to mean the swiftness with which he descends into the hearts of mortals. For at a single glance, almost without noticing, a man is at times so inflamed by another’s beauty that it seems to him that he is already all on fire.

This, I believe, is what the one wished to show who made Cupid with a thunderbolt in his hand, though it is not known who he was; Pliny writes that Alcibiades carried one so on his shield, and there was likewise one of the kind in Rome, in the Curia of Octavia, which some said was made for Alcibiades, since he carried it so on his shield, wishing in that way to show his beauty, for he was most beautiful, as though, just as Jupiter, to whom the thunderbolt belongs, is greater than all the other gods, so in beauty he went far above all the others.

But it can be said further, and perhaps better, that it seemed to that maker that a torch does not show fully the force of amorous ardour, and that he therefore put the thunderbolt in Cupid’s hand, since this not only burns things that burn easily but also at once sets alight those to which another fire would not so quickly take hold; it breaks and shatters whatever it finds that opposes it, however firm and hard it may be, and penetrates with marvellous speed everywhere.

<aside>Propertius</aside>
<aside>Girolamo Benivieni</aside>

All of which fits very well the force of Love, which quickly takes hold in a gentle heart, and breaks and shatters the hard and obstinate, and with marvellous speed penetrates wherever it wishes, as Propertius says in an elegy, in which he paints Cupid, and which was already put into the vernacular by Girolamo Benivieni in terza rima; and it is this.

> Were they not marvellous, in your opinion,
> the hands of him who, in youthful form,
> whoever he was, placed Cupid when he painted him?
> He knew the nature of blind lovers,
> and how, quite outside all reason,
> they lose their first goods for a light care.
> Nor has he wings on his shoulders without cause,
> which make him fly from this heart to that,
> because those souls in which he places his nest,
> while they run through this stormy sea,
> are driven back by alternate waves
> in such a way that they can never stop.
> His curved bow and barbed arrows,
> which hang suspended from his shoulders,
> or with which he always arms his hands,
> certainly mean nothing else to our eyes
> than that, before any one of them notices,
> shaken loose from the string, they descend into the middle of his heart.
>
> *(Propertius, in Girolamo Benivieni’s version)*

> Images of Love signifying the various effects and powers of Love, which in noble and gentle hearts easily finds a place, and breaks and shatters the hard and obstinate ones. It also denotes how easily we let ourselves be lured by lascivious and libidinous effects, especially in youth.

I find Cupid sometimes also represented in another way, without the bow [?5], as in Pausanias, who, writing of Corinth, says that there, above the temple of Aesculapius, in a certain small round chapel of white marble, was Cupid, the work of Pausias the painter, who had thrown away the bow and the arrows and held a lyre in his hand.

<aside>Cupid with Fortune</aside>

And the same Pausanias, speaking of Achaea, says that in Aegira, a city of that country, there was a certain small temple where he saw Cupid standing beside Fortune, wishing to show that she too can do much in the matters of love, although Love by itself is so powerful that it conquers all the most obstinate wills, breaks every hardened heart, and makes the proudest and fiercest souls become humble and meek in such a way that they willingly then hold out their hands to the snares of love.

And this perhaps Arcesilaus wished to show, much praised for it by Varro, as Pliny writes, although some say that he praised him not for this but for the fine art and the great judgement that he showed in sculpture when from a single piece of marble he made a lioness, with which little Cupids played, and some of them held her tied, some offered her a horn and wanted her to drink from it, and forced her to do so, and some others showed that they wanted to kick her. Among all animals the lion is the fiercest, but they say that the lioness is of a still fiercer spirit, much more cruel, and for this reason Arcesilaus made her, to express better the force of amorous feelings.

These feelings were also shown very well by the poets, when they imagined Mars amusing himself in the arms of Venus; her image, together with that of the Graces and the Horae, who always went with her, I shall add to this one of Cupid, so that the son may not be without the mother, and so that the mother among these images of mine may have those who accompany her, as she had among the ancients.

<aside>Cupid conqueror of Pan</aside>

Since, then, Cupid is so powerful, it was said that he conquers all, as if no other were his equal in strength; and the fables therefore imagined that he once conquered even the god Pan, who had himself challenged him first. If this is applied to natural things, it means that universal Nature, the maker of everything, shown by the god Pan, when she began from the beginning to work, began likewise to take delight in the things that she made, and then continuing, as if enamoured of them, she has always sought and still seeks to adorn them as much as she can. Because of the delight, then, that Nature takes in the things made by herself, she came as it were to challenge Cupid; and he was able to do so much more than she that he made her subject to himself in such a way that she does only what pleases him. From this is born the concord of the elements, diverse among themselves, for the generation of things.

> Image of Pan and Cupid, the one conquered, the other conqueror, to show the power of Love over universal Nature, who, enamoured of the delight in her works, thinks of nothing else than making them beautiful and adorned.

And souls, as the Platonists hold, likewise descend through Cupid from Heaven down here into mortal bodies, having already contracted through him a certain affection and desire for these, just as they rise again to Heaven when, stripping themselves wholly of earthly love, they turn to the love of heavenly things only. And because those who considered the things of Heaven said that there were two gates there, through which human souls passed, descending from Heaven to earth and returning from earth to Heaven, and that the one was called that of the gods, the other that of men, Orpheus held that Cupid keeps the keys of these gates, so that no one can pass without him; and therefore whoever depicts him also with keys in his hand could give the reason why he had represented him so.

<aside>Cupid tormented</aside>

But Cupid has not always been so powerful that no other could at times do more than he, as Ausonius shows in a certain fiction of his, which I wish to set down only in order to give, with the mockery, the torments and the cross of Cupid, an end to his image, avenging myself in this way, since I can do nothing else to him, for the thousand injuries that he has already done me, and does me every day. For it is no small vengeance taken on one who does evil to tell of his sufferings and his humiliations, and it seems to console much to remember that those too have been in the gravest dangers who were, and still are, the cause of another’s painful life.

In Ausonius, Cupid, unawares, flies to the place where the souls dwell of those who departed this life wretchedly because of love, and, seized by them, he is bound and set as if on a cross upon a tall myrtle, and while these devise various torments for him, Venus comes. She not only does not try to soften the angry souls against her son, but shows herself angry too against him, and having made some whips of roses and flowers, she beats him so savagely that she moves the souls to pity, and they ask her to forgive him, and they too forgive him, and untie him, letting him go, a thing I certainly would not have done; but since all those who seized him were women, nothing else could be expected. The thing is very beautiful in the Latin; I do not know what it is in the vernacular: let whoever knows Latin read it in its own language; and whoever does not, be content with this, which I have for the time being reduced to the vernacular, until someone comes who may give it better form.

> In the sorrowful fields, where the green myrtles
> make the shady wood that encloses
> the enamoured and unhappy spirits,
> were the souls that had been pitiless and cruel to themselves
> through loving another too much, so that before their time
> they were left bare of their mortal covering.
> And each one, renewing the memory
> of the time gone by, showed
> how and why she had died so early.
> The great wood has little light, and dusky,
> as when at times a dark veil hides
> from us the white face of the Moon.
> Silent lakes, which never show
> murky waves, and slow, quiet rivers
> that run narrowly between flowering banks.
> The misty air seems to forbid
> all gladness to the flowers that are there,
> so that they can never show themselves glad,
> flowers that were, while they were alive,
> youths all of supreme beauty,
> of which they were wretchedly deprived.
> Narcissus, who has such fondness for himself
> because he takes himself for another, and fair Hyacinthus,
> to whom death is given by the one who loved and prized him most.
> Crocus of the golden hair, Ajax overcome
> by anger so that, striking himself in the breast,
> he leaves the ground painted with his blood.
> Adonis, who so many times was clasped
> by the mother of Love to her fair breast,
> gathering pleasing delight from it,
> and who now, made a flower, adorns the ground
> with a purple colour among many others,
> so that the place is full of flowers of every kind.
> And remembering the woes now past,
> the tears, the sighs, the sad loves,
> the sorrowful accents and the mournful laments,
> they renew with these also the pains
> that they felt at their last parting,
> when, dying, they left their first ardours.
> Among these, and among the green grasses that make
> the dense wood pleasant, go the women of old
> who loved wretchedly in this life.
> And they then show how hostile
> to themselves they once were, while they were
> far too friendly to the desires of Love.
> Semele, weeping, shows to what a hard
> plight she was brought when, struck by lightning,
> she brought forth into the world her unripe offspring.
> And she would wish she had not been
> granted what she asked of Jupiter
> when she was deceived by Juno.
> So she shakes herself, and often moves
> her gown with her hand, and makes a wind for herself, and pretends
> that the lightning flame is renewed.
> Anger, born of disdain and heavy grief, grips
> Caenis when she sees herself a woman
> once more, and she paints her mind upon her face.
> Procris, near to death, sits on the ground,
> dries her wounds, and still keeps
> her loving faith towards her wounder.
> With the lamp in her hand, overcome by grief,
> the girl of Sestos leaps into the sea,
> where she sees her lover drowned.
> Nor does Sappho show a foot less swift than hers
> in climbing the hard rock
> to throw herself into the waves, and the dishonourable
> love that inflamed Crete makes the unhappy woman
> go at a slow pace, grieving
> that her heart was set so low,
> and she shows a white bull, and afterwards wants
> there to be seen, no less than her own error,
> what the daughters did for love,
> because of whom the brother was left dead
> by him who left one of them on the shore
> and took the other with him, who had her heart
> once so enamoured of fair Hippolytus;
> but then, not being able to draw him to her desires,
> she hated him as much as she had welcomed him.
> Laodamia seems to rejoice and grieve
> over false dreams, and after the death
> of her Protesilaus to wish to live no longer.
> And others besides, who with a strong arm
> drew their unhappy souls out of their breasts,
> show the hard irons by which they died.
> Thisbe shows that of her bridegroom, whose loving
> delights were cut short by too cruel a fate
> when it should least have been so.
> Canace had hers from her brother, and the other,
> the one Dido had, was from her guest,
> which she indeed does not put down, that she may perish by it.
> And as public rumour has already told,
> here the Moon shows how often she
> came down to the beloved nest of Endymion.
> More than a thousand others then come after,
> each one showing what she had done
> against herself for Love.
> And while each was sweetly lamenting
> her ancient woes,
> since lamenting relieves grief in part,
> behold, unawares, beating his wings
> through the shady wood, Cupid comes
> among these sorrowing people,
> who, although almost as if hidden
> are the burning torch and the golden quiver,
> the bow and the arrows, in the misty air,
> recognise him nonetheless; and at once all those women together
> hurried
> to hold their common enemy.
> The damp and heavy air so presses upon his wings
> that the poor little wretch, who strives
> all the same to flee, and fears his enemies,
> toils in vain, and the women's onslaught
> grows so strong
> that he remains vanquished in the power of others.
> In the great wood there was a myrtle, which
> was the torment of anyone who had been
> unjustly a cause of harm to another.
> There Adonis, bound by Proserpina,
> was once punished for having
> scorned her love for the sake of Venus.
> To this come all the stern and sad women,
> and with them they drag Cupid,
> whom they have held up on the high trunk.
> They have bound his hands and feet; and, setting aside
> every use of pity, they seek to satisfy
> their fury upon the wretch.
> All accuse him, yet they cannot find
> a just cause for giving him punishment,
> but they make whatever they please be just.
> So he feels a cold fear run
> through every vein, which freezes him, and sad grief troubles
> his face, once serene,
> since he sees himself in the hands of the wicked crowd,
> which blames him for their own errors
> and throws every law and order into confusion.
> Each reproaches him with the pains
> of her past death, and then says to him:
> As I did then, so I want you to die now.
> And they all think to make their state
> glad and happy, if they take vengeance
> on him, as seems right to them, even if it be lawful.
> So each shows that by which her life
> was cut off, and in the same manner
> each hastens that Cupid be tormented.
> This one carries a knife, and cries: I approve
> that this be torment and death for Cupid.
> That one shows the firm knot of a noose.
> Another seems to take great comfort
> in showing the deep rivers, because she hopes
> to see in others her own final fate.
> One shows the steep cliffs, another the angry
> and fierce wave of the sea, another the calm sea,
> according as she most desires that Cupid perish.
> One says: Now I shall make my heart glad
> with the death of this wicked one,
> unless I forbid myself the vengeance.
> These flames will make the cruel slaughter,
> and, shaking the burning flames, she wishes
> that Cupid's death be a new example.
> Myrrha, uncovering her ripe offspring,
> tears open her fair womb and then takes in her hand
> the tears for which she still sorrowfully grieves.
> And these she boldly flings from afar
> towards him, who is afraid for himself,
> seeing himself in too strange a plight.
> One is content to mock him,
> showing that she forgives him, and that the anger
> she once had against him is all spent,
> but the mockery is such that Cupid sighs over it
> no less than if he were awaiting death,
> because it brings a heavy torment with it,
> for a sharp stylus is to make spurt
> strongly out of his delicate limbs
> the blood that the roses received as their lot.
> Or else those fair parts
> are to be set on fire with lighted lamps,
> from which persons are begotten.
> Fair Cytherea, who was apart,
> when she hears about her son, gladly wishes
> to have her own share in his torments too.
> At once she comes to him, and does not speak
> pleasantly, as she is wont, but, troubled in look,
> increases his grief and fear with words,
> calling him the cause of all her ill
> fame, and cries to him: Ah, wretch, you know well
> that blame is gained through you alone.
> Then she reproaches him with all he ever did:
> the adulteries of Mars, which Phoebus
> revealed to Heaven with his bright rays,
> the well-endowed Priapus, who opened her womb
> to her in an indecent shape,
> from which she once suffered no little shame,
> Hermaphroditus, whose name remains still
> for one who bears the mark of man and woman
> and in truth is then neither the one nor the other,
> the wicked Eryx, with whom she is indignant
> for his cruelty and because he has made her come
> more than once to be with a mortal man.
> Nor content with saying it, but with the act
> of one who would punish her own error
> in him who had led her to err,
> she gathers together one flower and another,
> and the vermilion roses, with which
> she then beats sad and disconsolate Cupid.
> And she gives him so many that those women
> became pitiful over his ills,
> who had before threatened him with mortal pains.
> So they begged her so much that the fair mother
> laid aside the anger and the heavy indignation
> that had once ill disposed her against her son.
> And each one says that Cupid is unworthy
> of so many pains, and that through him
> none ever reached the sad point
> of giving herself death, but that their own
> fates were the cause of the wretched end
> that they thus destined, they said, for us.
> Venus, therefore appeased, thanks the poor
> women for their pitying office,
> then unties her son with her divine hands,
> and he, now safe from the cruel ruin
> that had been prepared for him, flies away.
> Would that he had gone headlong into a precipice,
> and no word of him were ever heard again.
>
> *(Ausonius, in Cartari’s version)*

## Doubtful passages

- [?1] The printed text says the statue “called” (*chiamaua*) the burning torches into the river and extinguished them there. The sense requires “lowered” (*chinaua*), as in the usual image of the extinguished torch; the translation assumes a misprint and reads “lowered” (scan 0451).
- [?2] “and, so as not to do worse, I have used his translation”: Cartari writes *per fare peggio* (“in order to do worse”) in the Italian. The sense is plainly “so as not to do worse”, as the preceding clause shows (he did not try to do better than Alamanni); the translation follows the sense rather than the printed words.
- [?3] The Italian prints *A lato come augello*, literally “at the side, like a bird”. It is read as *Alato*, “winged like a bird”, which is what Moschus says (πτερόεις ὡς ὄρνις). The final epithet *crudo* means “cruel” here (literally “raw”).
- [?4] The line *Viue non sò di che, non pretia lodo* has the rhyme form *lodo* for *lode*; it is read as “does not prize praise”. Another reading (“I do not praise”) is grammatically possible but does not fit the series of third-person statements about Love.
- [?5] The Italian has “with the bow” (*con l'arco*), but the *in altra guisa* (“in another way”) and the example itself (Pausias’ Cupid, who had thrown away the bow and arrows and held a lyre) show that the meaning is “without the bow”; the translation gives the sense, and the printed wording is probably an error for *senza l'arco* (or for *con la lira*, “with the lyre”).
