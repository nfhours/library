# The Graces

Source: books/cartari/_text/TEXT_reading.md, scans 0493–0501
Rules: books/cartari/_translation/en/RULES.md

---

Now that we have drawn Venus, mother of Cupid, whom we have likewise already portrayed, it is quite fitting that we speak of the Graces and the Horae together, who always go in company with her. For just as Venus and Cupid are the cause that new offspring keeps coming in succession, and that the human race is thereby preserved, so the Graces hold mortals gathered together, because the benefits that men do one another in turn are dear and pleasing, so that they stand joined together by the fair knot of friendship; without which there is no doubt at all that men would be far inferior to the other animals, and cities would become caves, or rather would not exist at all. For which reason it could almost be said that it would have been better for mortals not to be than, being, to live without the Graces.

<aside>Whose daughters the Graces are</aside>

But divine providence, which has care of the universe, wishes that these should nevertheless exist. According to some they were born of Venus and of Bacchus, and dwelt among mortals; which the fables invented, because it hardly seems that anything else is more pleasing to men than what comes from these gods; these I do not repeat, because they can be seen in the images of these gods. Others represent them as born in another way; but it is not for us to say this now, but only what statues they have had from the ancients, or how they have been painted.

<aside>Chrysippus</aside>

And although their names are different, the Graces and the Horae are nevertheless believed to be one and the same thing, but also to have different functions between them. And Chrysippus said that the Graces were a little younger than the Horae, and more beautiful too, and that for this reason the ancients gave them to Venus as companions.

<aside>The Horae are goddesses</aside>

Homer writes that the Horae are goddesses who stand at the gates of Heaven and keep guard there; and that it is their task to send the thick mist down upon mortals, and to take it away again.

<aside>Statius</aside>

Statius, describing the setting of the Sun, shows them coming quickly to take the bridles off the swiftest steeds, saying thus in our language:

> Then, when Phoebus has descended to the West,
> he slackens the course for the burning steeds,
> hiding himself beneath the Ocean,
> the fair and lovely daughters of Nereus,
> dwellers in the deep sea,
> are about him; and with swift steps
> to him at once come the ready Horae
> to loose the reins from the foaming mouths
> of the fierce horses, which they then send
> to the green grass so that they may make good
> the fatigue of the course already run,
> and some of them strip off his locks,
> which give light to the world with fair rays,
> which adorn him in the form of a crown.
>
> *(Statius)*

<aside>Eusebius</aside>
<aside>How many Horae there are</aside>

The Horae are nothing other than the seasons of the year; hence they are made out to be four, just as there are four parts of the year, so distinguished by the Sun and likewise named from him, because among the Egyptians the Sun, besides many other names that he had there, was also called Horus. On this Eusebius writes of them in this way. The Horae, whom they say to be the four seasons of the year and to open and shut the gates of Heaven, are given now to the Sun and now to Ceres, and for this reason they carry two baskets, one of flowers, by which spring is shown, the other full of ears of grain, which signifies summer.

<aside>Ovid</aside>

And Ovid likewise says in the *Fasti* that these stand in company with Janus on guard at the gates of Heaven; and when he afterwards tells of Flora, in whose power are the flowering meadows, he says that the Horae, dressed in the thinnest veils, come into these at times to gather various flowers, to make fair wreaths of them for themselves.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

And Pausanias writes that the ancients placed them on the head of Jupiter, together with the Parcae, wishing perhaps to show in this way that Fate is nothing other than the will of God, from which also come the changes of the times. But I have now said more about the nature of the Horae than is needed to know how they are to be painted. Coming to this, then, I shall give only one portrait of them, as Philostratus paints it in a fine picture, saying that the Horae, having come down to earth, go turning the year (which is in the form of a certain round thing) with their hands; from which turning it comes about that the earth then produces, year after year, everything that is born; and they are blond, dressed in the thinnest veils, and walk over the dry ears of grain so lightly that they break none, nor bend even one; they are pleasant and joyful in aspect; they sing most sweetly; in turning that orb, or ball, or circle, whichever it may be, they seem to give marvellous delight to those who look on; and they go as if leaping almost always, often raising their fair arms on high; they have their blond hair scattered over their shoulders, their cheeks rosy, like one who already feels warmed from running, and their eyes bright and quick in movement.

> Image of the Horae, also called Graces by some, and of Apollo, the former understood as the four seasons of the year, the latter as the Sun that varies the seasons; held also to be goddesses of friendship, beauty, charm and amiability, goddesses of cheerfulness, play and pleasure; they also denote God's mercy toward the guilty.

<aside>Four Graces</aside>

Since these, then, bring it about that the earth returns to us the sown grain and the other fruits with great interest, as though, showing herself grateful for what we give her, she rewarded us in this way, it was said that the Graces were four, because there are as many seasons of the year, called Horae, as I have said, meaning to signify that these and the Graces are the same. They were therefore represented with wreaths on their heads, and one had hers of flowers, and another of ears of grain; the third of grapes and vine leaves, and the last of olive. And the ancients feigned that Apollo had them in his right hand, because the diversity of the seasons comes from the Sun.

<aside>Diodorus</aside>
<aside>Why the Graces are companions of Venus</aside>

And since, as Diodorus says, they were worshipped by the ancients because they thought that they could give beauty of face and of all the other parts of the body, with that charm which so delights at times whoever looks at them, they were for that reason placed in the company of Venus. And it was also their task to bring it about that men should not be ungrateful to one another, but should repay with a cheerful heart the benefits received.

<aside>Two Graces</aside>

For which reason some said that the Graces were two, and among the Lacedaemonians they worshipped only two, as Pausanias writes, because it seems that likewise there are only two effects that come from them: the one, to do a benefit to another; the other, to repay the benefits received.

<aside>Three Graces</aside>

But the same Pausanias also says afterwards that all those who placed the Graces at Delos with the statues of Mercury, Bacchus and Apollo represented them as three, and that there were likewise three at the entrance to the citadel of Athens. Hence it has since always been commonly held that they are three, because a benefit ought not to be returned such as we received it, but much greater, and often doubled. From this it comes that one of them stands with her back toward us, and two look at us, giving us to understand thereby that in returning the good done to us we must be much more liberal than when we are the first to do a benefit to another; which, however, ought not to be done in expectation of reward, because whoever does this may be called a usurer rather than a liberal benefactor.

<aside>The Graces are virgins, glad</aside>

It is said that the Graces are young virgins, glad and smiling, to show that whoever does a benefit must use no deceit at all, but do it with a sincere and cheerful heart. This will be better understood also by whoever notes that they were represented naked and loosed from every knot, as Horace sang of them, because men must be with one another of a free mind, loosed from all deceit, naked and open.

> Images of the three Graces, goddesses of beauty and grace; goddesses also of gratitude and benefit, named Euphrosyne or joyfulness, Aglaia or charm, Thalia or pleasantness; goddesses of conversation, sociability and friendship, and of that cheerful life which men desire to live.

<aside>Eteocles</aside>

Pausanias, though, writes that he never found out who was the first to represent the Graces naked, because from the beginning everyone represented them clothed, and that he does not know for what reason their attire was afterwards changed, so that all have represented them naked, both the painters and the sculptors. Besides this he states [?1] that Eteocles of Boeotia was the first to ordain that the Graces should be worshipped and should be three, but he does not know what names he gave them.

<aside>Names of the Graces: Euphrosyne, Aglaia, Thalia. Pasithea</aside>

So we now name them as they were named by Hesiod, who called one of them Euphrosyne, which means cheerfulness and joyfulness, the other Aglaia, which signifies majesty and charm, the third Thalia, which comes to mean pleasantness. And Homer called one of them Pasithea, the one whom Juno promises to give as wife to Sleep, if he goes to Jupiter and puts him to sleep; and he also calls one of them Grace, by a proper name, who he says was the wife of Vulcan and stayed with him always. She, with fair veils on her head, comes to meet Tethys when she goes to beg Vulcan that he be willing to give arms to Achilles, her son.

In Greece, among the Eleans, the Graces had a temple, in which their statues were of wood with gilded garments, and they had the face and the hands and the feet of white ivory. One of them had a rose in her hand, another a certain thing made like a die; the third a sprig of myrtle. And of these things they give this reason. The rose and the myrtle belong to Venus, and were therefore given to those who for the most part are with her; and that square thing signifies the games that the innocent young virgins play among themselves, to their own pleasure and to that of whoever sees them; which does not happen with women of greater age, to whom more serious things are fitting, not games. All this Pausanias says.

But of the three emblems of the Graces others give another reason, and say that the rose signifies their pleasantness; the die, that they must go and return in turn, as dice go when one plays with them; and the myrtle, that they must always be green and never wither, as this plant is always green.

<aside>Alexander ab Alexandro. Aristotle</aside>

And as Alexander ab Alexandro reports, and Aristotle wrote before him in the *Moral Writings*, the ancients used to build the temple of the Graces in the middle of the squares, so that willingly serving another and repaying the benefits received should be before everyone's eyes, because this is the proper office of the Graces.

This, however, ought not to be done without good consideration, because it is a poor thing to give thus to one who does not deserve it or has no need of it, rather than to one who has need of it and deserves that it be given to him [?2]. This too the ancients taught us likewise in the image of the Graces, depicting Mercury as their escort and leader, who represents reason and sound discourse, so that, following in the footsteps of reason, men may know how, and when, and to whom they are to give and do a benefit, imitating, as far as they can, the divine goodness, which is always ready to do good.

> Image of the Graces guided by Mercury, denoting that helping and beneficence must be done with reason, at the right time, and to the deserving, without hope of reward, with a sincere heart, and that the one benefited must, when occasion offers, return the benefit, if not in deeds then at least in words.

Hence, says Macrobius, the ancients placed the Graces in Apollo's right hand, and the bow with the arrows in his left, to give us to understand that the divine hand is much readier to do good than harm, and, as long as it can (that is, as long as it is not forced by our wicked conduct, because then it uses what it holds in the left hand, to punish us), is a generous giver of its favours to mortals.

And men too must do this, learning it, if they do not otherwise know it, from the image of the Graces, which Seneca explains very well where he writes of doing a benefit to another, saying that these are three, because one does the benefit, another receives it, and the third gives the return for it. Or else that one does, another returns, the third does and returns; so that there come to be three ways of doing a benefit.

They stand with their hands and arms joined together, because the order of doing good to another is that it should pass from hand to hand and return also to the profit of the one who first did it, and in this way the pleasing knot of friendship holds men joined together. They are cheerful and joyful in aspect, because such must whoever does a benefit to another show himself, and such for the most part are those who receive it. They are young, because the memory of benefits received must never grow old.

They are virgins, because in doing good to another it must be done with a pure and sincere heart, and without any knot of obligation; as their ungirt and loose garments also show, which are bright and transparent, because the heart of the one who does a benefit must be within such as it shows itself outside in deeds, and because whoever receives the benefit must not hide it, but make it seen by everyone. For this too is a gratitude: when the benefit received cannot be repaid in deeds, to acknowledge it at least in words, and to make the liberality of the benefactor plain to all.

And here let the image of the Graces end, with a sculpture of these that is seen in Rome in the Colonna house, with Latin verses, which in the vernacular mean this:

> Truly these are the naked Graces, who once were
> made of white marble, polished and fair;
> all three have among themselves a like face,
> so that you can know them for sisters;
> all three are equal in age, and beauty
> is likewise seen equal in all three.
> Thalia stands with her face turned to her sisters
> and joins and knots her arms
> with theirs, who are on the left
> and on the right, looking at us,
> this one is named Euphrosyne, that one Aglaia;
> with pleasing knots of fair arms
> they are entwined together with the third sister.
> Jupiter is their father, and from heavenly seed
> they were conceived by their mother Eunomia,
> who afterwards with a happy birth
> brought them forth into the world as joyful and pleasing handmaids
> to gracious Cytherea, so that through them
> she often, together with fair Cupid,
> increases amorous pleasures in such a way
> that every gentle heart is left conquered by them.

THE END.

## Doubtful passages

- [?1] Scan 0498: “Oltre di ciò mette, Ethocle di Beotia fosse il primo, che ordinasse …” — the construction is defective (“mette” is followed by no “che”; the sentence is evidently elliptical, and the verb is perhaps “mette [in scritto]”, “sets down”); translated “he states that Eteocles … was the first to ordain”.
- [?2] Scan 0498: “perche così è mal dare a chi non merita, ò non ne hà cui fa dibisogno, & merita, che gli sia dato” — the sentence is garbled (after “non ne hà” something is missing, probably “bisogno, che [dare] a cui …” or “che a cui”; “cui” is left without a governing word). Translated by sense, supplying “rather than” between the two persons, as the contrast requires. Another possible reading takes “così … [come]” as a comparison (“it is as bad to give to one who does not deserve it … as [not to give] to one who needs it and deserves it”); the general sense (give to the needy and deserving, not to the undeserving) is the same.
- Scan 0498: “Benche Pausania scriue …” opens with a concessive “benche” that has no main clause of its own (the previous sentence ends at “nudo, & aperto”). Rendered “Pausanias, though, writes”, so as to keep a concessive sense without leaving a fragment.
- Scan 0493: “il che finsero le fauole … le quali non replico, perche nelle loro imagini si ponno vedere” — the antecedent of “le quali” is not stated (the fables, or the things that come from Venus and Bacchus); translated “these I do not repeat”, leaving the reference open.
- Scan 0500: “à farsi bene, che male” (of the divine hand) — “farsi” seems a printer's slip or an impersonal use for “far bene”; translated “to do good than harm”. Also “al farti bene” (scan 0500) is read as “al far bene”.
- Scan 0496: “Perche queste dunque fanno, che la terra … fu detto, che le Gratie erano quattro, perche …” — two “perche” in one sentence; the first is rendered “Since”, the second “because”.
- Scan 0498: “Morali” of Aristotle is rendered “Moral Writings”, without identifying the work.
