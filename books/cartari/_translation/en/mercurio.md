<aside>Messengers of the gods</aside>
<aside>Mercury, and his office</aside>

The fabled gods of the ancients had so divided their duties among themselves that only two were given the charge of carrying the divine messages. One was Mercury, messenger of Jupiter, and the other Iris, who served Juno; but not so that Jupiter did not sometimes command her too. It is true, however, that he used her only when he wanted war, plague, famine or some other great evil to be announced to mortals; and for more pleasant matters he sent Mercury, whose name means "word", who was likewise messenger and envoy not only of Jupiter but of other gods too, according to the fables, which under the fiction of him understood the interpreter of the gods, since speech among us sets forth what the mind, which is the divine part of us, has already conceived. But leaving these interpretations aside for now, let us see how the vain belief of the ancients represented him, holding him to be the god not only of messengers but also one who presided over gain, as he says of himself in Plautus:

> The other gods have granted and given me
> the care of messages and of gain.
>
> *(Plautus)*

<aside>Caduceus</aside>

In the book of antiquities collected by Peter Apian one sees that Mercury was once represented as a beardless youth, with two little wings above the ears, quite naked, except that a cloth, not very large, hung down behind from his shoulders, and with his right hand he held a purse resting on the head of a goat, which lay at his feet together with a cock, and in his left he had the Caduceus. This was Mercury's own emblem, as were the wings on his head and at his feet; so the poets nearly all draw him in this way, giving him feathers on his feet, which they call Talaria, and the Caduceus in his hand, called by them a rod, because at the beginning it was a simple rod, when he received it from Apollo in exchange for the lyre that he gave to him, as the fables tell, at the time when, after the stolen cows, they made peace with each other. Hence in the hymn that Homer sings of Mercury, telling almost the whole fable, Apollo speaks to him thus:

> And then I shall give you the golden rod
> of felicity and of riches.
>
> *(Homer)*

<aside>Why serpents with the Caduceus</aside>

To this rod serpents were afterwards added, either because, as we read, Mercury, having once found two of them fighting together, threw it between them, and they were at once reconciled; or because, as Iamblichus says, Mercury having taught us dialectic, that rod was therefore given to him as his emblem, since that is precisely what the two serpents signify, who look at each other; or again for what Pliny puts forward, who, after saying how serpents knot together in the summer, adds: And this, which shows concord among the cruellest serpents, seems to be the reason why the Caduceus has been made with serpents around it; for we read that the Egyptians, who were perhaps the first to make it, made it in this way.

A straight rod, or wand, whichever we choose to call it, stood with two serpents around it, one male and the other female, knotted together in the middle; and with the upper part of their bodies they made something like an arch, so that their fierce mouths came to meet at the top of the wand, while their tails wound around it from below, from where two small wings came out. And the Latins called it Caduceus because at its appearance it made all discords fall (*cadere*), and it was therefore the emblem of peace.

<aside>Peaceful ambassadors</aside>
<aside>Olive, sign of peace</aside>
<aside>Statius</aside>

Hence it was carried by the ambassadors who went for peace, who were afterwards also called caduceatores. Although we find that among the ancients ambassadors who went as friends likewise carried the olive, as Virgil says when he describes Aeneas sending a hundred of them to King Latinus, all crowned with green olive, and when Aeneas goes to Evander, he shows Pallas, who comes out first to meet him, that he comes as a friend, holding out his hand with a branch of peaceful olive. Statius likewise, when he describes Tydeus going to ask Eteocles, in the name of Polynices, for the kingdom of Thebes, puts an olive branch in his hand, to show that he went as a peaceful ambassador, and then describes him throwing it away when he cannot obtain what he asks; and from this the wicked war began.

<aside>Verminaca</aside>

Appian relates that Hasdrubal, seeing that he could no longer hold the citadel of Carthage, already stormed and almost entirely taken by the Romans, left his sons and his wife there, in the temple of Aesculapius, with many others, who afterwards all burned themselves together by common consent; and he fled secretly to Scipio, carrying in his hand some olive branches, with which he showed that he was going only to obtain peace. Many of his men had done the same before him, having fled to Scipio to obtain, as they did, that whoever wished might leave the citadel safe and go away; but these had carried in their hands not the olive but vervain (*verbena*), which in common speech is called *verminaca*. Although from Appian's words one may understand not that herb alone but all the other herbs and leaves with which the altar and the temple of Aesculapius, which was very beautiful and rich in that citadel, were adorned; since under the name of verbena all the herbs and fronds with which the altars [?1] were adorned on the day of the festival were anciently understood.

<aside>What holding out grass shows</aside>

Among the ancients, to hold out grass to someone with the hand was also a sign of confessing oneself beaten by the person to whom it was held out, and of offering oneself to him as a subject. Festus writes that this was introduced in the earliest times by shepherds, because when they ran races together or contended with one another in some other way, the one who was beaten bent down to the ground and, taking grass in his hand, held it out to the winner.

<aside>Servius</aside>

Nevertheless the true vervain was also a sign of peace, as Pliny writes, and with it ambassadors who went for a truce or for peace were crowned, especially those of the Romans, since other peoples perhaps used something else, as we read in Appian of some peoples of Spain who sent ambassadors to Marcellus to obtain pardon and peace from him; and these carried before them a wolf skin in place of the Caduceus or of the olive branches and the vervain, which were, however, almost universally the most used in matters of peace. The ancients were also accustomed to wind around it some small bands or fillets of wool, which signified the weakness and humility of the one who carried it, because wool is drawn from the sheep, a weak and humble animal, as Servius explains on the first speech that Aeneas makes to Evander in Virgil. And so Peace has been represented sometimes by the Caduceus alone, sometimes by the olive branch alone.

<aside>Peace a goddess</aside>

Peace was likewise a goddess among the ancients, and she had in Rome a great temple, so beautiful and so rich that many went to Rome solely to see it. It was built by Vespasian, though it had already been begun by Claudius, and after the victory won over Judaea he brought into it all the ornaments of the temple of Jerusalem; and one may believe that there was in it also some fine idol of Peace, but I have not so far found that anyone has made mention of it.

<aside>Depiction of Peace</aside>

Let us see, then, how she has been depicted or drawn elsewhere. Aristophanes describes her as altogether beautiful in appearance, and according to him she is a companion of Venus and of the Graces.

Pausanias writes that her statue in Athens was that of a woman who held in her hand, as I have said on another occasion, the boy Plutus, god of riches, because riches are better acquired and preserved in peace than in time of war, since at that time one cannot attend to cultivating the fields.

<aside>Peace, friend of Ceres</aside>
<aside>Tibullus</aside>

And so the ancients said that Peace was a great friend of Ceres and very dear to her; and Tibullus speaks thus:

> It was Peace who first joined the oxen
> under the curved yoke, so that the ground
> was cultivated and then produced the grain.
> And the fair fruit, full of sweet juice,
> is gathered through peace from the vine,
> which she once buried [?2] in the bosom of the earth.
>
> *(Tibullus)*

<aside>Claudian</aside>

And wars are the cause of the contrary. Hence Claudian feigns that Ceres did not wish to marry her daughter Proserpina to Mars or to Phoebus, who both asked for her, because the vehement heat of the sun, if it lasts too long, harms the crops just as wars do. For this reason the ancients sometimes depicted Peace, as can be seen on some ancient medals, as a woman who held in her hand an ear of wheat. And Tibullus therefore said:

> Come, nourishing Peace, with the ear of wheat in your hand
> and with your white bosom [?3] full of fair fruits.
>
> *(Tibullus)*

They crowned her sometimes with olive and sometimes with laurel. And on some ancient medals one also sees Peace with a wreath of roses.

> Image of Peace, and of the hieroglyphs or signs that show her; namely the boy Plutus, whom she has in her hand, god of riches that multiply in peace; ears of wheat, which denote the cultivation of the fields in peace and their fertility; she is crowned with laurel, having under her feet the olive, a sign of triumph and of quiet.

<aside>Concord</aside>
<aside>Seneca</aside>

But although the names are different, and different images of them were also made, it nevertheless seems to me that Peace and Concord are one and the same thing, and both were worshipped by the ancients so that they might give them a quiet and peaceful life. It will therefore be well that, having described the former, I should describe the latter too, who was depicted in the form of a woman holding a cup in her right hand and having a cornucopia in her left, and so Seneca spoke of her thus:

> And to her who can restrain [?4] the bloody hands
> of fierce Mars, granting truce and rest
> to the wearisome wars,
> and brings with her the cornucopia,
> let a wholly gentle sacrifice be made.
>
> *(Seneca)*

At times a sceptre was also put in Concord's hand, out of which some fruits seemed to grow. Aristides, in a certain oration of his, describes Concord as being of beautiful and dignified aspect, well-knit of body and well made, of a very fine colour and altogether lovely, and having nothing in herself that in the least disagrees with her beauty. And he says that she once came down from Heaven to earth by the goodness of the gods, so that the affairs of mortals might proceed in a certain order; for through her the fields are cultivated and everyone securely possesses what is his; by her cities are governed, joyful marriages are made and preserved, and children are then nourished and instructed.

<aside>Fidelity a goddess</aside>
<aside>Silius Italicus</aside>

Concord was sometimes also shown with two hands joined together, which can be seen on a certain ancient medal of Nero, just as the ancients also did with Fidelity, whom they likewise held to be a goddess, and Silius Italicus places her dwelling in the most secret part of Heaven, among the other gods, when he feigns that Hercules goes to seek her for the defence of Saguntum and begins to speak to her in this way:

> O holy Fidelity, who before supreme Jupiter
> were created, and adorn men and gods:
> through you all things have peace; and where
> at times, through human fault, you are not,
> it is rare that Justice is found there,
> because you always go side by side with her.
> And you dwell in chaste and just breasts,
> where holy thoughts are shut in.
>
> *(Silius Italicus)*

For Fidelity must remain secret, that is, the things that are entrusted to others in trust, and must be pure and clean of all deceit. For this reason Numa, second king of the Romans, ordained that the priest sacrificing to Fidelity should have his hand covered with a white veil, as Livy relates, to give it to be understood that fidelity must be kept with all sincerity, and that she was consecrated in the right hand, because we must defend her with all readiness and force.

<aside>Fidelity's own colour</aside>
<aside>Horace</aside>
<aside>Ariosto</aside>

Virgil likewise called Fidelity white and hoary, which Servius interprets as said also because it seems that more fidelity is found in men who are already grey and old. And Horace, lamenting his times, says that Fidelity, clothed in white, is little worshipped; and Acron notes there that, when sacrificing to Fidelity, the priest covered not only his right hand with a white veil but his head too, and almost his whole person, to show the whiteness of the soul that must always accompany Fidelity. For this reason Ariosto said:

> It seems that the ancients painted
> holy Fidelity clothed in no other way
> than in a white veil that covers her all,
> since a single speck, a single blemish, can make her ugly.
>
> *(Ariosto)*

<aside>Hand consecrated to Fidelity</aside>

Since it was believed that the proper seat of Fidelity was in the right hand, and that this was therefore consecrated to her, as I said, she was also often shown with two right hands joined together, and sometimes two little figures were also made who gave each other the hand. Hence the ancients held the right hand in great respect, as a sacred thing. From this it has come about, as some say, that when we want to quiet a noise that has suddenly arisen, we show this hand, raising it on high, and by holding it out open we signify that we bring peace. And therefore one sees that many statues of princes and illustrious captains were once made on horseback and on foot, stretching out the right hand.

<aside>Josephus</aside>

And Josephus, writing the antiquities of the Jews, states that among the barbarians it was a most certain sign of having to trust one another when they held out the right hand to each other, and that, this done, it was no longer possible either for one to deceive the other or for the other not to trust.

<aside>Kissing the hand</aside>
<aside>Plutarch</aside>

And from this perhaps also came the custom of kissing the hand of lords and other superiors, which was as common among the ancients as it is among us today, as can be seen in Plutarch, where Popilius Laenas, after he had spoken at length to Caesar, who was going into the Senate on the very day that he was killed, kissed his hand and went away. And Macrobius, in the speech he gives Praetextatus in favour of slaves, says that there are many of them who through greatness of soul despise riches, and that on the contrary one often sees that many free men and masters, through greed for gain, go basely to kiss the hands of other slaves; and this act showed that he who did it commended himself to the fidelity of the one whose hand he kissed, and therefore recognised him as his superior and lord. And the custom of giving the right hand as a sign of fidelity has likewise come down to our own times, and Fidelity was sometimes also shown with an entirely white dog, because we read of the miracles of the fidelity of dogs.

<aside>Stork, consecrated to Concord</aside>
<aside>Crow, bird of Concord</aside>

But returning to Concord, from whom the drawing of the two hands, which she has in common with Fidelity, has led me astray, the ancients consecrated the stork to her, and so there were many storks in her temple. Although Poliziano holds that not the stork but the crow was given to Concord, and he calls in testimony of this some ancient medals, and Aelian, who says that the ancients, after invoking Hymenaeus at weddings, used to call upon the crow as well, as an augury of the concord that should afterwards exist among those who joined together in order to beget children. But this was also for the sake of the fidelity that husband and wife must keep together, as the same Aelian says, relating that crows are so faithful to each other that, of two that have once paired, when one dies, the other remains a widow always.

<aside>Pomegranates for Concord</aside>

Besides this, pomegranates too were a sign of Concord among the ancients, as the writers of the Hebrews say, and therefore they put them around the garments of their priests.

> Images of Concord, and hieroglyphs denoting Fidelity and Concord, with the image of Fidelity, signifying her secrecy and her purity, and that through Concord the abundance of things, peoples and agriculture multiply; with the birds stork and crow, consecrated to Concord, which denote the same effects.

<aside>Virgil</aside>

But it is already time to return to Mercury, drawn with wings at his feet and a rod in his hand by Homer, when Jupiter sends him to Calypso so that she may let Ulysses depart from her, and to lead Priam into the camp of the Greeks to ask for the body of Hector; which Virgil afterwards imitated so well that it seems almost translated by him in this passage, when he likewise describes Mercury, commanded by Jupiter, going to Aeneas while he was with Dido, in these words:

> Mercury, intent on obeying his father,
> hides his feet in the golden Talaria,
> which, with wings swift as any wind,
> carry him high above the earth and over the waves;
> he takes the rod with which in a moment
> he draws souls from the Tartarean shores,
> and places others there, and gives and takes away
> sleep, and releases many from life as well.
>
> *(Virgil)*

I could put down other poets too, who have described him in the same way; but it seems to me that these two are of such authority that when they bear witness to something, nothing more need be sought afterwards, unless perhaps it were to give a better understanding of what was said by them, which is not needed now.

<aside>Why feathers were given to Mercury</aside>

Feathers were then given to Mercury, as I have said, because in speech, of which he was the god (or perhaps he signified the thing itself as well), words fly through the air no differently than if they had wings. Hence Homer almost always calls words swift, winged, and having feathers.

<aside>Plautus</aside>

That Mercury always had feathers on his head can be seen in Plautus, when, for the short while that he disguised himself, he did not want to be without them, although he said he was doing so in order that the spectators might recognise him and tell him from the servant of Amphitryon into whom he had changed; and these are his words:

> And so that they can recognise me,
> I shall always have these feathers on my hat.
>
> *(Plautus)*

<aside>Apuleius</aside>

For Mercury had a hat as well, and wings were also attached to this; although Apuleius shows him without it, when he tells of the judgement of Paris performed on the stage, in which a youth appears as Mercury, altogether beautiful and lovely in aspect, with blond and curly locks, among which were some golden feathers, little different from wings, that stuck out in the shape of wings; and he had around him only a cloth, which, knotted at the neck, hung down from his left shoulder, and the Caduceus in his hand.

<aside>Martianus</aside>

Martianus describes him as a youth of fine body, tall and firm, on whose smooth cheeks some down begins to sprout, as Lucian also says; and half naked, because only a short little garment covers his shoulders; and he makes no mention of wings or of the Caduceus, but he does say that he shows himself to be nimble and much practised in running and in wrestling.

<aside>Philostratus</aside>
<aside>Palaestra</aside>
<aside>Wrestling</aside>
<aside>Horace</aside>

And wrestling now reminds me of what I once read in Philostratus, and it is that Palaestra, whom we may call Wrestling, was a daughter of Mercury, and was such that it was hard to tell whether she was male or female, since by the face, all smooth and lovely, she seemed to be no less a boy than a girl. The fair hair was long indeed, but not so long that it could be tied. The chest was that of a pure maiden; nor did the fair breasts rise in her any more than they rise in a delicate youth; nor were the arms white only, but tinged with colour as well; and sitting, she held in her lap a branch of green olive, for she loved this plant greatly, perhaps because those who wrestled used first to anoint themselves with oil. So Philostratus paints Palaestra, and calls her daughter of Mercury, because he was the inventor of this kind of exercise, as Horace also sang in a certain hymn that he made to him.

> Image of Mercury, inventor of letters, of music, of geometry and of the good arts, and image of Palaestra his daughter, goddess of wrestling, who holds in her lap a branch of olive, it being the custom of wrestlers to anoint themselves with oil.

<aside>Mercury, inventor of all the arts</aside>
<aside>Thoit</aside>
<aside>Thout</aside>

And Mercury invented, and showed to mortals, the way of exercising not only the body but the mind as well, and Iamblichus says that those of Egypt gave him the discovery of all the good arts, and that therefore they always dedicated to him everything that they wrote. Cicero writes that Mercury showed in Egypt letters and laws, and that he was called by those peoples Thoit, or Theuth, as is read in Plato.

<aside>Square figure of Mercury</aside>

And others have said that, besides letters, music, geometry and the palaestra were also invented by Mercury, and for these four things the ancients used to make his image of square shape and to set it in schools, as it was in a certain part of Arcadia, according to what Pausanias relates, who describes it as made in such a way that it seemed to wear a mantle, and it had no legs or feet below, but was like a small square column.

<aside>Galen</aside>

Galen, when he exhorts the young to the good arts, says that they were all invented by Mercury, and draws him as a youth, beautiful not by art but by his own nature, cheerful in appearance, with bright and shining eyes, and standing on a square base; because he who follows virtue removes himself from the hand of Fortune and, by standing firm and steadfast, fears no injury from her.

<aside>Suda</aside>

And Suda writes that the square figure is given to Mercury out of regard for truthful speech, which always stands so firm and steadfast against anyone whatsoever, whereas the liar and deceiver quickly changes and often turns now here, now there.

But whether for this reason or for another, Alexander ab Alexandro also reports that the Greeks often made the statue of Mercury in square form, with the head alone and no other limb; with such statues they often honoured great and valiant captains, setting them in public, and they also set many of them before private houses, as Suda reports.

<aside>Thucydides</aside>
<aside>Herms</aside>

Thucydides also writes, and Plutarch repeats it, that in Athens there was a great number of these statues, which one night were nearly all mutilated, immediately after the Athenians had resolved to send a large fleet against Syracuse, on account of which Alcibiades, who was one of the commanders of the fleet and had himself mutilated some of them, suffered greatly, as if he had given a sign of a change in the state of the republic by altering those statues, which were called Herms, because Mercury was likewise called Hermes by the Greeks, and they were placed, as I said above, as an ornament in the schools and in the academies.

> Statues of Mercury, called Herms, because he was the inventor of all the good arts, which fear no blows of time or of fortune, and the virtuous fear no injury from them. They signify also the steadfastness of truthful speech.

<aside>Cicero</aside>

Hence Cicero, replying to Atticus, calls the Herm an ornament common to all academies. And another time he replies to the same man that he already likes them, though he has not yet seen them, the marble Herms with metal heads that Atticus writes he has bought; and he begs him to collect as many as he can get, and urges him to send them soon to adorn his academy, or library, as we may call it.

<aside>Herms: by whom first made</aside>

We read that the Athenians were the first to make such statues. And not only in these of Mercury, but in those of many other gods as well, the other Greeks likewise used such a square figure; and more than all, perhaps, the Arcadians, as Pausanias writes, because among them there was an altar dedicated to Jupiter with a statue made in a similar form.

<aside>Cyllenian</aside>
<aside>Force of speech</aside>

And although many write that Mercury was called Cyllenian from a mountain of Arcadia of that name, where he was born, nevertheless there have also been some who held that he was so surnamed from these square images, which could be called truncated and cut short, having no other limb than the head, because the Greeks call Cylli those who have some limb cut off; and these images showed the force of speech, which has no need of the help of hands to do what it wishes, as Festus writes, but when it is well ordered and makes itself heard at fitting times, it is so powerful that it easily bends human minds as it pleases, and often does violence to others at its pleasure.

<aside>Horace</aside>

Hence Horace sings of Mercury that at the beginning he persuaded mortals to leave the woods and the mountains, through which in those first times they went scattered like beasts, and to unite to live together civilly. This he perhaps took from a certain fable of the Greeks, which relates that Prometheus went as ambassador to Jupiter to beg him to be willing to provide that men should at last leave that rude and bestial life which they had been leading since the beginning of the world. So Jupiter sent Mercury with him with a commission to teach those whom he judged most worthy the way of speaking well, with which they could persuade the others of what was necessary to do in order to live a domestic, honest and civil life.

<aside>Tongue consecrated to Mercury</aside>

And for this reason the ancients consecrated the tongue to Mercury, and, besides all the other sacrifices, this was proper and particular to him, to sacrifice to him the tongues of the victims, drinking a little wine.

<aside>God of merchants</aside>

Mercury was also believed to be the first to show the way of making gain, and therefore he was the god of merchants. Indeed they say that he was called Mercury from the care that he has of merchandise (*merci*); hence Suda writes that for this reason they put a purse in the hand of his idol. Fulgentius holds that the wings on Mercury's feet signify the swift and almost continuous movement of those who trade, who, diligent in their affairs, go almost always now here, now there.

<aside>Caesar</aside>
<aside>Cock beside Mercury</aside>

Hence Caesar writes that the Gauls worshipped Mercury more than all the other gods, and had many idols of him; because, besides saying that he was the inventor of nearly all the arts, they believed that he could particularly help others a great deal in gains and in trade; and how vigilant men must be in these, the cock placed beside this god showed, as I have said already, although some hold that it signifies rather the vigilance that wise and learned men must use, because for these it is exceedingly unseemly to spend the whole night sleeping. For if we take Mercury for reason and for that light which guides to the knowledge of things, he does not want us to lie long buried in sleep, but, once the spirits are restored, to return to our accustomed works. For men cannot remain in continual action either of the body or of the mind, and so that brief rest which sleep brings is necessary to them, as the philosophers show.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>
<aside>Sleep with the Muses</aside>
<aside>Hesiod</aside>
<aside>Homer</aside>

And Pausanias, writing of the country of Corinth, states that there was there an altar where sacrifice was made to the Muses and to Sleep together, as if they were very great friends with each other. For the ancients held Sleep likewise to be a god, and made statues of him, as of the other gods, believing him, as Hesiod and Homer say, to be the brother of death.

This was also shown by the images carved on the Chest of Cypselus, where there was a woman who held on her left arm a white boy, who was sleeping, and on her right a black one, who was likewise sleeping and had twisted feet, signifying Death by the black one and Sleep by the white one; and the woman was Night, nurse of both.

<aside>Night described</aside>
<aside>Ovid</aside>

The ancients represented Night in the form of a woman with two great black wings at her shoulders, spread out in such a way that she seemed to fly and to embrace the earth with them, as Virgil said. Ovid gives her a wreath of poppy that encircles her brow, and sends with her a great company of black dreams. The other poets then feign that she has a chariot with four wheels, which signify, as Boccaccio says, the four parts of the night, as they are divided by soldiers and by sailors in their watches. She is entirely of a dusky colour, but the robe that she has about her shines a little, and she is so painted as to represent the ornament of heaven.

<aside>Tibullus</aside>

In Tibullus the Stars, her daughters, go with her, and Sleep and the Dreams, where he speaks thus:

> Take your pleasure now, for Night
> has yoked her steeds together, and comes
> running to us from the Cimmerian grottoes;
> and the Stars, full of lovely light,
> follow their mother's chariot, they whom
> heaven keeps gathered in a fair troop.
> And Sleep, spreading his black wings,
> goes behind them, and there go the uncertain Dreams
> with unsteady foot and uneven steps.
>
> *(Tibullus)*

<aside>Sleep with wings</aside>
<aside>Silius Italicus</aside>
<aside>Seneca</aside>

From these words it is known that Sleep likewise had wings, which Statius also said, when he complains that it is now so many days that he cannot sleep, and begs him to come to him at last and to shake the light feathers over his head; and Silius Italicus said the same. Moreover Sleep is a youth, for the same Statius represents him as such [?5] and calls him the most agreeable of all the gods, as if there were nothing more welcome to mortals, nor that pleases them more after their labours, than the rest that pleasant Sleep brings us; hence Seneca spoke of him thus:

> O Sleep, nourishing relief from the labours
> of mortals, quiet of the mind,
> and best part of human life,
> O swift son of fair Astraea,
> and languid brother of Death,
> who mingle together the true and the false,
> and show us clearly what is to come
> with a sure [?6], and often (alas) a sad message;
> father of all, harbour of life,
> rest of the light, and of Night
> faithful companion, you look no more
> upon the king than the servant, but come equally
> to the one and the other, into weary limbs,
> gently entering, you drive away weariness,
> and to that which mortals fear so much
> you accustom them, so that they learn to die.
>
> *(Seneca)*

<aside>Philostratus</aside>
<aside>Garments of Sleep</aside>
<aside>Dreams</aside>

Philostratus, in the picture that he makes of Amphiaraus, in whose cave he says was the door of Dreams, because sleeping there one saw and heard in a dream what one sought to learn, paints Sleep all languid, with two garments, the upper one white, the lower one black, meaning by the former the day and by the latter the night, and puts a horn in his hand, as nearly all the poets do too, from which he seems to scatter rest over mortals. They say that this was feigned because the horn, when thinned, is transparent, and so shows us things as we see them in a dream, when, however, the Dreams are true; but when they are false, Sleep carries not the horn but a tooth of an elephant, because however thin the ivory is made, it never becomes so transparent that human sight passes through it.

<aside>Doors of dreams</aside>
<aside>Porphyry</aside>

Hence Virgil feigned that there were two doors through which the Dreams come, one of horn, the other of ivory, and that through the former the true ones pass, and through the latter the false. On this Porphyry reasons thus, as Macrobius reports, saying that the soul, withdrawn for the most part from the functions of the body when a man sleeps, although it directs its eyes to the truth, can never see it directly, because of the darkness of human nature; but if this nature thins itself in such a way that the eye of the soul passes through it, it sees true Dreams by the door of horn; but if it remains so dense that the soul cannot penetrate it with sight, the false Dreams come by the door of ivory.

<aside>Vain Dreams</aside>
<aside>Servius</aside>

The same Virgil also feigned that at the middle of the entrance of hell there is a great elm, spreading its leafy boughs, and that under the leaves of these hang vain and false Dreams. Which means, as Servius expounds it, that at the season when the leaves fall from the trees, Dreams are always vain. And others have said that the elm, a sterile tree that bears no fruit, expresses by itself the vanity of Dreams, which were called blind by the ancients, as Suda writes, either because they are deceitful or because they always speak with one who has his eyes shut.

<aside>Rod of Sleep</aside>

Besides this, Sleep sometimes also carries a rod in his hand, with which he touches mortals and puts them to sleep. Hence Statius, once when he could not sleep, begged him to come and touch him with it.

<aside>Ministers of Dreams</aside>

Ovid, after describing the place where Sleep dwells, which he places among the Cimmerian peoples, who have night almost always, although Homer puts it in Lemnos, an island in the Aegean Sea, and Statius among the Ethiopians, and Ariosto has lately placed it in Arabia: Ovid, I say, having described the house of Sleep, lays him down to sleep on a bed of ebony covered all over with black cloths, around which stand innumerable Dreams figured in various forms; of whom three are the most worthy ministers: the one, who represents the human form alone, is called Morpheus; the other is called Phobetor, who shows every sort of beast; and the third, who makes one see earth, waters, rocks, trees, mountain, plain, and every other inanimate thing, has the name Phantasus.

> Image of Night, nurse of Death and of Sleep, and image of Sleep, brother and companion of Death; quiet and sweet refreshment of mortals. And the horn denotes the repose and variety of dreams.

<aside>Why Mercury is beardless</aside>

I say no more of them, but return to the image of Mercury likewise made in square form, as is read in Pausanias when he describes Achaea, that it stood in a certain part of that country on the road, with a beard, and with a hat on its head. Nor do I remember having read of any other statue of Mercury than this one that had a beard, since the poets all describe him without one, by which, they say, they mean to show that speech, when it is fair, lovely and pure, never grows old. But many nevertheless represent him with the first down beginning to show, as I have already said of Martianus, and as I can say the same of Lucian, since in his work on sacrifices he describes Mercury with a few little hairs of the first beard that were beginning to appear on his face.

<aside>Homer</aside>

Homer likewise has Ulysses see him so, when he goes to him and brings him that herb with which Ulysses afterwards defended himself against the enchantments of Circe.

<aside>Stones thrown at the statue of Mercury</aside>

It is also read that at the statues of Mercury that stood on the public roads everyone who passed that way threw stones, as he happened to find them, so that great heaps of them were to be seen gathered round about; whether it was to show that honour must be paid to the gods by offering what first presents itself and is at hand; or because in that way they seemed to clear the public roads, so that others who passed that way, and the couriers entrusted to this god, should find nothing afterwards that could harm them; or truly this was done to give to understand that all discourse is composed in this way of small particles, as those heaps are of small stones gathered together.

<aside>Suda</aside>

Suda writes that these heaps, or little mounds of stones, were dedicated to Mercury on uncertain roads, perhaps so that whoever passed that way should not stray from the good path. And that it was also the custom of the ancients to set the first fruits of the harvest on the public roads before the statues of Mercury, for the service of travellers, who ate of them according to their need.

<aside>Mercury with three heads</aside>

It is read further that Mercury was sometimes represented with three heads; either to show the great force that ornate speech has; or because for him, guide of travellers, one head was not enough to show others the different roads, and especially at crossroads, that is, where there were three ways, and so on each of the three heads it was marked where this road, or that, or the other led.

<aside>Homer</aside>

The ancients also held that Mercury had care of the shepherds; of which Homer bears witness when he says that among the Trojans Phorbas was very rich in herds and flocks, because Mercury, to whom he was more pleasing than all the others, had enriched him so; perhaps because in the first times men knew no other gain than that which they drew from flocks and herds.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

And so Pausanias writes that in the land of Corinth, on a certain road, there was a statue of Mercury made of bronze, which sat and had a lamb at its side. Of this he deliberately keeps silent the reason, as a mysterious thing that cannot and must not be told. And there was another among the Tanagrans, a people of Boeotia, which carried a ram on its neck, because it is said that Mercury, going in that way around the walls of the city, brought to an end a most grievous pestilence.

This Tanagran Mercury has given occasion to the common run of antiquaries to believe that many ancient intaglios of early Christianity are something other than what they truly are. Our people used to wear on signet rings Christ engraved on them in the figure of a Shepherd, with the sheep on his neck, to allude to the words:

> I am the good shepherd.

And I remember seeing in Rome a carnelian on which this figure was engraved, with the name Jesus [?7] beside it. And a friend of mine had two other gems of similar workmanship, and on one of them was the monogram. And in San Lorenzo outside the Walls I recall having seen a marble tomb, on the left hand as one enters by the main door, on which a Shepherd with the sheep on his shoulder could be seen, in the middle of certain ornaments of the parapet of the said tomb. In connection with this Tertullian, cited by Cardinal Baronio in the 1st book of the Annals, writes that the Christians anciently used to put this figure on chalices. Hence the custom was afterwards observed that, when his festival was celebrated there, a most handsome young man went around the city with a lamb on his neck.

Another statue of Mercury also existed, brought from Arcadia, as the same Pausanias recounts, and offered to the temple of Olympian Jupiter, armed, with a helmet on its head, and dressed in a tunic with a short soldier's cloak over it, and it carried a ram under its arm.

<aside>Macrobius</aside>
<aside>Mercury as the Sun</aside>

Macrobius, who holds that all the other gods stand for the many powers of the Sun, draws the image of Mercury likewise to these, saying that the wings show the speed of the Sun, and that the fables' feigning that he killed Argus, guardian of the daughter of Inachus changed into a cow (hence they sometimes put a scimitar in the hand of his statue as well) was because Argus, with so many eyes, is the heaven full of stars that looks down on the earth, and the earth the Egyptians depicted in their sacred letters in the form of a cow; but Mercury, that is the Sun, killed him, as the one who makes the stars vanish when the day begins to show itself. Besides this, the square figures of Mercury, which had only the head and the virile member, show that the Sun is the head of the world and the sower of all things; and the four sides signify what is signified by the cithara of four strings, likewise given to Mercury, that is, the four parts of the world, or the four seasons of the year, or that two equinoxes and two solstices make four parts of the whole Zodiac.

And it was an invention proper to the Greeks, as Herodotus writes, and the Athenians were the first to make, and to show others how likewise to make, statues of Mercury with the genital member erect; and this they did perhaps because the fables said, and Marcus Tullius reports it, that in him it swelled and stood erect in that way from the desire that came upon him for Proserpina the first time he saw her, as the drawing can be seen in our plate 91, on folio 293.

<aside>The Caduceus is applied to the birth of man</aside>

The Caduceus is then applied to the birth of man, as the same Macrobius says, in this way, following the Egyptians. With man, when he is born, there are these four gods: the Daemon, Fortune, Love, and Necessity. Of these the first two signify the Sun and the Moon: the former is so called because the spirit, the heat and the light of human life come from him and are preserved by him, and for this reason he is believed to be the Daemon, that is, the god of him who is born. And the latter is called Fortune, because all her force extends over bodies, which are subject to many and diverse accidents. Love is shown by the two heads of the serpents, which join together as if they were kissing; and Necessity is understood by that knot which they make of themselves in the middle.

> Image of Mercury, god of eloquence, guide of travellers, god of shepherds, understood also as the force of the Sun; and image of the god Anubis, god of sagacity among the Egyptians, who is one and the same with Mercury; and the Caduceus here signifies the Sun and the Moon, the Daemon, Fortune, Love and Necessity, which go with human birth.

<aside>Martianus</aside>

Martianus writes that Philologia, having entered the second Heaven, saw a maiden coming to meet her with a tablet in her hand, on which were engraved all these things that point to Mercury. In the middle was that bird of Egypt like the stork, which they call the ibis, and a head with a most beautiful face, covered with a hat, which had two serpents around it. Beneath it was a beautiful rod, gilded at the top, green in the middle, and turning black at the foot. On the right of it were a tortoise and a scorpion, and on the left a goat with a certain bird like the sparrowhawk.

<aside>Anubis</aside>

These things are almost all taken from the mysteries of the Egyptians, among whom it is believed that Mercury was worshipped under the name of that god whom they called Anubis. For they depicted him with the Caduceus in his hand, as Apuleius describes him, who, recounting those who went with Isis, says thus:

> There was Anubis, whom they said to be Mercury, with a face now black and now golden; he raised the neck of a dog, and in his left hand he carried the Caduceus, while with his right he shook a branch of green palm.
>
> *(Apuleius)*

<aside>Diodorus Siculus</aside>

This god was depicted in Egypt with the head of a dog to show the sagacity that comes to us from Mercury, since almost no other animal is found more sagacious than the dog. Or else they depicted him so because, as Diodorus Siculus recounts, Anubis was the son of Osiris, and, following his father, always showed himself valiant in all the wars, so that after death he was revered as a god; and because in life he wore a dog as a crest over his armour, his image was afterwards made with the head of a dog, those who made it wishing also to convey by this that he was always a sagacious and faithful guardian of his father, defending him constantly against anyone who tried to do him harm.

> Image of Anubis, god of sagacity, guardianship and fidelity, with the crocodile, an animal of Egypt, terrestrial and aquatic, with Apis, Jupiter Ammon, and other mysterious figures.

<aside>Hercules</aside>

Besides this, if Hercules was not the same as Mercury, he was nonetheless little different from him, as is attested by his image made by the Gauls, who worshipped him as the god of prudence and of eloquence, in this way, as Lucian recounts.

<aside>Lucian</aside>

He was an old man, almost in extreme old age, entirely bald except that he had a few hairs on his head, dark in the face, and all wrinkled and furrowed, dressed in the skin of a lion, with a club held in his right hand and a bow in his left; a quiver hung from his shoulders, and at the very tip of his tongue were fastened many very fine chains of gold and silver, with which he drew after him by the ears a great multitude of people, who nevertheless followed him willingly.

<aside>Eloquence and its force</aside>

It is easy to see that this image signifies the force of eloquence, which those peoples gave to Hercules, because, as the same Lucian says, Hercules was believed much stronger and more vigorous than Mercury; and they depicted him as old because in old men eloquence is far more perfect than in the young, as Homer shows us in Nestor, from whose mouth, when he spoke, sweetest honey seemed to drip.

> Image of Hercules among the Gauls, held by them to be the god of eloquence and of exercise, who was held by some to be Mercury also; and this image denotes the force and military discipline, especially in old captains and accomplished orators.

<aside>Gods of exercise</aside>

And for this reason perhaps these two gods also had a single temple in common in Arcadia. The Athenians, who had in their Academy altars of the Muses, of Minerva and of Mercury, wished to have one likewise of Hercules, as though his numen might help those who exercised there no less than the numina of the others; and Pausanias writes that not only the Greeks but many barbarian nations too believed that Mercury and Hercules presided over exercise, and that they were chiefly worshipped in the places where it took place. Hence among the Lacedaemonians in the Dromos, a place where the young men exercised in running, there was a most ancient idol of Hercules, to which those who were already of greater age went to sacrifice. And in a certain part of the land of Corinth those peoples said that Hercules had once offered and dedicated there to Mercury his club, which was of wild olive, which was believed afterwards to have taken root, and to have grown and become a great tree.

I do not say whether there was one Hercules only, or many, although I know that Varro counts forty-four, and says that at that time all men of great and wonderful valour, and those who had overcome some fierce monster, were called Hercules; nor which of the many was placed in the number of the gods, because this does not concern one who wishes only to make a portrait of the idols and statues that the ancients made of them; who worshipped one Hercules as a god, and paid him those honours that they paid to the other gods; and the Egyptians placed him in the number of the twelve (as Herodotus writes) who were first worshipped by them.

<aside>Image of Hercules</aside>

But although many of the things that are read about Hercules were done by different persons of this name, they are nevertheless all attributed to one alone, who was made a god. His idol was for the most part large and showed strength and robustness, on account of which in life he was surnamed Melampygus, which means "from the black rump", because the Greeks called strong and robust men so; and conversely they said Leucopygus, that is, one who has a white rump, of one who was soft and effeminate.

<aside>A pleasant tale</aside>
<aside>Cercopes</aside>

On this subject a certain little tale is read. There were two brothers, as wicked and base as can be said, named one Passalus and the other Acmon, but they were called Cercopes, and they were sons of Memnon. They were rebuked several times by their mother, and begged to change so very bad a nature of theirs, but when she saw that she could not draw them back from their wicked deeds, she begged them at least to take care not to get in the way of Melampygus. Now it happened that Hercules, having one day lain down to rest under a tree, against which he had leaned his bow and his club, these two came upon him, and, seeing him asleep, planned to play some strange prank on him, and were already ready, when Hercules woke; who, rising, did them no other harm than that he seized them, and, tying them together by the feet as if they had been two hares, hung them from the club, put them on his shoulders, and went away.

The Cercopes, while they hung in that way, saw that Hercules had a black and hairy rump and buttocks, and began to talk quietly among themselves of what their mother had so often told them, and said that this surely was Melampygus. At which Hercules, having heard it all, took the greatest pleasure in the world, and therefore, laughing, untied them and let them go; but they were afterwards transformed into monkeys, as Suda writes, because they wished to deceive Jupiter.

Hence by the Cercopes the fraudulent and flatterers were often meant, as is seen in Plutarch, who, speaking of the difference there is between true friends and flatterers, says that princes delight in the latter just as Hercules delighted in the Cercopes. Herodotus also made mention of them, describing the route that Xerxes took in crossing with his army the mountains of Greece, and says that he went to cross the river Asopus by a certain road that was called the seat of the Cercopes, that is, of the malicious, where there was also a rock that was called Melampygus, that is, "black spring"; for this word can mean this as much as what I said of Hercules.

I return to his idol, which was that of a strong and robust man, likewise quite naked, except that he had a lion's skin around him, the head of which with its mouth open served him as a helmet, and he held the club in one hand and the bow in the other, and the quiver hung from his shoulders, as I have already said.

<aside>Armed Hercules</aside>
<aside>Apollodorus</aside>

A similar one, entirely of metal, ten cubits high, was dedicated at Olympia, a city of Greece, by some who had gone with the son of Agenor to look for Europa, as is read in Pausanias. He writes further that the Lacedaemonians had an idol of Hercules, with the skin of the lion around him, and fully armed. The reason for this came about because Hercules, having gone on certain business of his to Sparta, the chief city of the Lacedaemonians, had brought with him a young cousin of his named Oeonus, or Licymnius (as Apollodorus says, recounting the same event), who, going all alone at his pleasure to see the city, arrived in front of the house of Hippocoon, who was then lord and king there, where he was at once attacked by a terrible dog, which he struck with a stone and sent back into the house; but the sons of Hippocoon, who heard of this, came out and flung themselves upon Oeonus with sticks, and killed him.

Hercules, having learned of the matter, driven by anger and by grief for his dead cousin, went all alone, without any delay, against the murderers, and was for some time at close quarters with them; in the end Hercules, wounded in a thigh, withdrew, and took himself out of the fight for that time, not being able to resist the great multitude of persons who were coming at him; but a little later, having gathered many together in his turn, he did so much that he killed not only the sons but the father Hippocoon as well, and destroyed that whole house. And for this reason the Lacedaemonians depicted him armed.

<aside>Aesculapius Cotylaeus</aside>

And the Arcadians afterwards put a scar on the thigh of his idol, in memory of the wound that I mentioned; healed of which, he dedicated a temple to Aesculapius under the surname Cotylaeus, because Cotyle among the Greeks is the same as "thigh" among us, as if because Aesculapius had healed him of the wound that he received in the thigh.

<aside>Arms of Hercules</aside>

Apollodorus writes that Hercules was likewise armed when he fought for the defence of Thebes against the Minyans, and that Minerva gave him the arms. And he adds that Hercules, having learned to draw the bow from Eurytus, afterwards received the arrows from Apollo, the sword from Mercury, the breastplate from Vulcan, and the mantle from Minerva; and that the club he cut and made himself, in the Nemean forest.

<aside>Pliny</aside>

Pliny, reporting some of the most noteworthy metal statues that the ancients had, says that in Rome there was one of Hercules, terrible in aspect, and dressed in a tunic in the Greek fashion. That he was terrible to see is shown by what is read of one man, who was so afraid of him that he turned entirely to stone, seeing him pass by there where he had hidden himself in a certain cave; and that stone was, as Suda reports, in the form of a man putting out his head to see.

<aside>Athenaeus</aside>

Then the fables said that the Sun gave Hercules a great vessel to drink from, with which he crossed the sea, as Athenaeus reports.

<aside>Skyphos, vessel of Hercules</aside>
<aside>Hercules the drinker</aside>
<aside>Cyathus killed by Hercules</aside>

Macrobius interprets this vessel as having been a sort of ship called Skyphos, since that was also the name of the vessel, and it could be likened to what we call a skiff (*schifo*), or boat; hence they afterwards never used any other vessel in his sacrifices. And Virgil, speaking of the ceremonies of Hercules celebrated by Evander when Aeneas went to him, said that the sacred Skyphos burdened the hands of Evander himself, which shows the size of this vessel, with which in hand Hercules was sometimes depicted, either on account of the fable I mentioned, or to show that Hercules was a great drinker, as Athenaeus recounts; which perhaps those also wished to show who, in the land of Corinth, in a certain chapel of his, represented a young man who handed him drink; although Pausanias writes that Hercules, dining there at the house of a father-in-law of his, gave such a blow [?8] on the head to Cyathus, a young man who gave him drink, that he killed him, since it seemed to him that he did not perform that office gracefully, and that in memory of this those statues were afterwards made.

<aside>Hercules the eater</aside>
<aside>Bird of Hercules</aside>

It is read further in Apollodorus, in Athenaeus, and in others, that Hercules was a great eater, and voracious beyond measure, so that he often ate a whole ox by himself, and that for this the ancients consecrated to him the bird that is called gull by the Greeks and coot by our people; because, as Suda also writes, it is by its nature exceedingly voracious and greedy.

<aside>Lactantius</aside>
<aside>Apollodorus</aside>

From this voracity of Hercules arose some of his sacrifices, in which it was not lawful to say even one good word; for, as Lactantius reports, and as is read in Apollodorus, one day when he was passing through the island of Rhodes and was very hungry, he took by force from a peasant, who would not sell him one, both the oxen with which he was then ploughing the land, and ate them with some companions of his. The poor man, in despair at the loss of his oxen, unable to take any other revenge, turned to blaspheming and cursing Hercules, and to saying every evil in the world of him and of all his own; at which Hercules always laughed, and said that he had never eaten with more pleasure than when watching that man speak so ill of him.

Hence, after he was made a god, the people of the country consecrated to him an altar called the Yoke of the Ox, and there at a certain time they sacrificed to him a pair of oxen with the yoke on their necks; during which the priest, with all the others who were present, could be heard blaspheming and saying every possible evil, because they believed that in this way they renewed for Hercules the pleasure that he had in hearing himself blasphemed and cursed by the peasant whose oxen he had eaten.

<aside>Suda</aside>

And on this subject I will not pass over another sacrifice, no less mad and foolish than the one I have mentioned was wicked and abominable, born likewise of the pleasure that Hercules took in seeing that certain peasants, as Suda reports, in order not to delay the sacrifice prepared, the ox that was to be sacrificed having run away, at once made one out of an apple, sticking four little rods into it in place of feet and two in the place of horns.

<aside>Julius Pollux</aside>

Or the matter was as Julius Pollux recounts it: that those who were bringing the victim (which was a ram) to a certain festival of Hercules, not having been able to cross the river Asopus, and the hour appointed for the sacrifice having already come, some boys who were there stuck four straws in place of feet, and two for horns, into an apple; pretending that it was the ram that was to be sacrificed, they performed over it, as if in play, all the ceremonies that belonged to it. This thing gave Hercules so great a pleasure, and was so dear to him, that the custom remained afterwards among the Thebans of sacrificing apples to him in the manner in which that apple was sacrificed for lack of a victim.

> Image of armed Hercules, of Hercules the eater and drinker, and of the bird the coot, consecrated to him for its voracity, and of the altar consecrated to him called the yoke of the ox, a sign of his grateful nature and kindness; he is crowned then with the poplar tree, since he was taken also for time, which devours and consumes everything.

<aside>Labours of Hercules</aside>

But since the valour of Hercules was no less in other, worthier and more glorious deeds than in eating and drinking, many statues and paintings were also made of him for that reason, dedicated to him as well in his own temples as in those of the other gods. Among these he was to be seen as a little baby strangling with his hands two serpents that had come to his cradle; and, grown up, cutting off the heads that were reborn to the Hydra and burning them; catching by running the hind that had feet of metal and horns of gold; and splitting the jaws of a terrible lion, or else strangling it; looking on at some horses that were eating a king set before them; carrying on his neck a fierce wild boar; wounding with arrows in the air certain great foul birds, so large that, spreading their wings, they took the light of the Sun from the world; leading bound a frightful bull that breathed fire; squeezing a giant against his chest and killing him; killing a fierce dragon and taking away from certain gardens the golden apples that were guarded by it; putting his shoulders to the task of holding up the heaven; killing a king who had three bodies and driving off from him a great herd of oxen; killing in front of a cave a terrible robber who breathed smoke and flame from his mouth; dragging behind him Cerberus with three heads, chained by him; shooting with the bow the eagle that devoured the liver of Prometheus, bound to a high mountain; and likewise killing many robbers and many tyrants. It would be too long to tell all the glorious deeds that are recounted of this man; they give matter for making various images of them, and for them he was called tamer of monsters.

<aside>Hercules strong in soul</aside>

But since there are among mortals no uglier or more frightening monsters, nor more cruel tyrants, than the vices of the soul, some have claimed that the strength of Hercules was of the soul, not of the body, with which he overcame all those disordered appetites that, rebels against reason, like most ferocious monsters, disturb man continually and torment him.

<aside>Interpretation of Hercules</aside>

And on this subject Suda writes that, to show that Hercules was a great lover of prudence and of virtue, the ancients depicted him dressed in the skin of a lion, which signifies greatness and generosity of soul; they put the club in his right hand, which shows desire for prudence and for knowledge, and with it the fables feigned that he killed the fierce dragon and carried off three golden apples, which he held in his left hand and which were previously guarded by that dragon. He overcame the sensual appetite, and freed from it the three powers of the soul, adorning them with virtue and with just and honest works.

<aside>Macrobius</aside>
<aside>Hercules as the Sun</aside>

Macrobius, who, as I have already said several times, gives one interpretation for the other gods, holds of Hercules that he is the Sun, and that his glorious deeds, of which twelve are the most celebrated, are the twelve signs of the Zodiac overcome by the Sun, because it runs through them in the whole course of the year.

<aside>Hercules as time</aside>
<aside>Poplar, tree of Hercules</aside>

Others have held that Hercules is time, which conquers and tames everything. And so they put on his head wreaths of poplar branches, for this is the tree that the ancients gave to him; hence Virgil has Evander, sacrificing to him, bind his head with it, and calls it the Herculean foliage, because this tree, with the two colours that it has, shows the two parts of time, the one white, which shows the day, and the other dark, which signifies the night; of which the fables say the cause was this: that when Hercules went into hell to bring Cerberus out from there, he wound some branches of poplar around his head, and the leaves of these became white underneath on the side that touched his flesh, all wet and soft with sweat, and above, towards the infernal air, dark and smoked; and that for this reason he afterwards wished that they should all always be so, and loved them [?9] always afterwards, because they had defended his head from the troublesome smoke of the infernal house.

<aside>Ceremonies of Hercules</aside>

With this, that Hercules was taken for time, agreed some ceremonies of his sacrifices, which, contrary to the custom observed in those of the other gods, were celebrated with the head uncovered, as Macrobius writes; the same reason can be given for it as was given in the Image of Saturn, to whom they likewise sacrificed with the head uncovered.

<aside>Zeus Apomyios</aside>

It is read further in Pliny that neither dogs nor flies went into the temple of Hercules that was in Rome in the Forum Boarium: the former, either because they smelled the club that stood leaning there outside, or because they were hated by him for the causes that Plutarch writes, where he gives the reason why dogs did not go into his temple; the latter, because once when Hercules was sacrificing to Jupiter, and had begged him to remove from about him the flies that troubled him beyond measure, and for this had killed one more victim for him, they flew away at once, all together, and never came again to his sacrifices. And therefore in that part of Greece where this happened, Jupiter was given the surname Fly-Chaser.

<aside>Myiagrus or Myiodes, god of flies</aside>

Although some have said that it was not Jupiter who chased away the flies then, but Myiagrus, the proper god of flies, who is also named Myiodes by some others; and when they made sacrifice to him in a certain part of Greece, all the flies flew out of the country.

<aside>Achor</aside>

The Cyrenaeans, a people of Libya, likewise worshipped the god of flies, called by them Achor, and sacrificed to him to make the plague cease that was caused at times by the great multitude of them.

<aside>Beelzebub</aside>

And the Ekronites in Judaea likewise had the idol of the flies, Beelzebub, for so Blessed Jerome interprets it.

<aside>Women driven from the ceremonies of Hercules</aside>

And as the flies did not go [?10] to the sacrifices of Hercules, so the women were driven away from them, nor could they even see them; which, they say, was ordered by himself because of the anger that he once had when a woman would not give him a drink, excusing herself on the ground that it was then the feast of the Bona Dea, a time in which women could not prepare or give anything to men.

<aside>Women with a privilege</aside>

Hence the rule was afterwards observed that, as men were driven from the ceremonies of the Bona Dea, so women could not see the sacrifices, nor enter the temples of Hercules, except some among the Erythraeans, who had an idol of Hercules, according to what Pausanias recounts, entwined and as if woven among certain timbers fastened together in the form of a Raft, which, carried by the Ionian Sea, they say, landed on a small island that lies midway between the Erythraeans and Chios; and that both sides sought to have it, having already seen the idol, but however much force they applied, it was never possible to remove it from there, until a poor Erythraean, who had once been a fisherman, when he could see (for he was then blind), said, it seeming to him that he had been warned in a dream, that with a rope made of women's hair the Raft with the idol could be drawn wherever one wished. But the women of the city having never been willing to give their hair to do this, some women of Thrace, who, although born free, nevertheless, because they then had no other means of living, served others there, offered and gave theirs of their own accord, and from it the rope was made with which the Erythraeans drew the Raft and got the idol; and for this reason they willed, and made a public edict of it, that only to the women of Thrace should it be lawful among them to enter the temple of Hercules.

<aside>Hercules and Apollo come to blows</aside>

The same Pausanias also writes that of the many statues that were in Delphi there were two, one of Hercules and the other of Apollo, which both held the Tripod, as if each wished to take it from the other, because they once came near to blows in strange fashion, as is read in Cicero, but that Latona and Diana, who were likewise there, seemed to mitigate the wrath of Apollo, and Minerva that of Hercules. This was so feigned because Hercules, angered once that he could not obtain a certain answer from the oracle, took the Tripod and carried it off; but, having regained his good temper, he afterwards gave it back, and had therefore from the oracle what he asked.

> Image of Apollo and Hercules, contending together over the Tripod, and of Latona and Diana pacifying Apollo, and of Minerva pacifying Hercules: signifying the wrath of Hercules at the oracle of Apollo for not having had an answer from it, mitigated afterwards on having had it; and the Tripod is a sign of honour, esteem, and heroic virtue, and of truth.

<aside>What the Tripod is</aside>

The ancients called Tripods certain metal vessels on three feet, which were to them what pots and other kitchen vessels are to us today, which Homer says are of two sorts, and calls one, as we should say, "for fire", the other "without fire", because these were kept in houses and in temples only for ornament, and were therefore offered to the gods as a gift of great esteem, and were likewise given to worthy persons and men of valour.

<aside>Lebetes</aside>

Hence Virgil counts them among the honoured gifts and prizes that Aeneas prepares for the games held by him in honour of his father Anchises, and these were perhaps those that Helenus had once given him, together with other presents of great value, when he left him; although Virgil there calls them Lebetes, with a Greek word, and Servius holds that these were like basins for giving water to the hands, saying that it did not seem fitting to give kitchen vessels to such a personage as Aeneas was.

But Athenaeus, reporting the distinction among Tripods made by Homer, as I have said, says that usage has brought it about that some are called Lebetes, and others like cups and other vessels for wine.

<aside>Truth</aside>

But be they as one may, for that does not serve our purpose much, but rather that the Tripod was a certain consecrated table, on which that young woman sat who gave the sacred responses, since she was filled with the spirit of Apollo, who drove into her body from below; and therefore some held that the Tripod was a stool pierced in the middle, so that the spirit should have somewhere to enter the body of the woman who sat upon it. And we could set it as a sign of Truth, because the oracle that came from it was believed always to speak the truth. Hence Athenaeus reports that the ancients said that everyone who spoke true things spoke from the Tripod.

<aside>Tripod of Bacchus</aside>

And that for this reason Bacchus likewise had the Tripod, which was like a cup or other vessel for wine, since wine often uncovers the truth of things no less than the oracles of the gods, because almost all the gods had oracles, each his own.

<aside>Oracle of Mercury</aside>

And although it might be that I should write some day of all of them, nevertheless now I will not fail to speak of one, that which was Mercury's, in order to end his image with this. Pausanias writes that in a certain part of Achaea, in the middle of a large square, there was an idol of Mercury, all of marble, bearded, raised on a square base not very large, in front of which there was another, of the goddess Vesta, likewise of marble, and that beside this were some little lamps of metal, which he who went to Mercury for counsel lit, having first burned a little incense; then he offered on the altar on the right side a certain coin, which that people then had in common use, and, having asked what he wanted, brought his ear to the idol of Mercury and stood listening for a little; then, rising from there, he at once put both hands to his ears, holding them so tightly closed until he was out of the square, when he opened them; and the first voice that he heard was to him in place of the answer of the oracle.

> Images of Mercury and of Minerva, the former the god of eloquence, the latter of prudence and inventress of the good arts, signifying that it is necessary for eloquence and prudence to be joined together if words are to benefit human actions.
