# Bacchus

<aside>Bacchus has several surnames</aside>

Although it is found that Bacchus was a bold commander, of great valour, and that he subdued various nations, nevertheless his name was celebrated among the ancients not so much for this as because he was believed to be the inventor of wine, and to have shown its use to mortals before all others; hence they afterwards worshipped him as a god. They called him not only Bacchus but also Dionysus and Father Liber, and named him Lenaeus and Lyaeus, expressing in him by various surnames the effects that wine produces in us, as I shall show as the occasion arises in drawing his image. This image was represented by the ancients in many idols and in various statues, now in one way and now in another: for they depicted it sometimes in the form of a tender boy, sometimes of a fierce youth, and sometimes of a feeble old man, naked at times and at times clothed, and now with a chariot and now without.

<aside>Philostratus</aside>
<aside>Bacchus of different ages</aside>

Hence Philostratus writes, in the picture that he makes of Ariadne, that there are many ways for whoever paints or sculpts Bacchus to make him recognisable. For a wreath of ivy with its berries shows that he is Bacchus; two little horns likewise, sprouting from the temples, do the same, and so does a panther, placed beside him.

<aside>Wine understood by Bacchus</aside>

These things are for the most part drawn from the nature of wine, which the poets often understand under the name of Bacchus, because, as I said, he was believed to be its inventor, showing mortals from the beginning how the grapes were to be gathered from the vines and the sweet juice pressed out, so pleasing and useful too to him who uses it with moderation, just as it brings the gravest harm to disorderly drinkers; which the ancients showed in the images of Bacchus. For in depicting him naked they meant to say that wine, and drunkenness, often uncovers what was previously kept hidden with no small care; hence arose the proverb that truth lies in wine, as I too have already said elsewhere, speaking of the Tripod.

<aside>Why Bacchus is old</aside>

The same was signified by his statue made in the form of an old man with a bald head, almost entirely hairless; besides which it showed also that too much drinking hastens old age, and that men drink a great deal at this age. For we grow old for no other reason than that the natural moisture fails in us, and we seek to restore it with wine; but we often err in this [?1], because wine is indeed moist in fact, yet it is so hot in virtue and power that it dries and parches much more than it adds moisture, as Galen says of great drinkers, who inflame their thirst and make it greater the more they drink while seeking to quench it and take it away.

Hence, because wine warms, it is said that the image of Bacchus was made for the most part as a beardless youth, cheerful and merry.

<aside>Comus</aside>

Very like him is Comus, who was among the ancients the god of banquets, for his image too was that of a youth on whom the first down begins to appear, as Philostratus describes him in a picture that he makes only for him, placing him at the door of a room where a joyful and fine banquet had been held for two newly-weds, who were already lying in bed, enjoying the fruits of love.

He was delicate and quite soft, with a ruddy face, because he had drunk too much, so that, being drunk, he could not keep his eyes open, but slept thus on his feet, letting his flushed face fall upon his chest; and his left hand, with which he leaned on a spear, seemed to be slipping too, as it seemed also that from his right a burning torch was falling that he held in it, and it had already gone so low that it would have burned his leg, had he not bent it aside.

<aside>Flowers, when used by the ancients</aside>

Everything around was full of flowers, and the god himself likewise had a wreath of them on his head, because flowers are signs of gladness and, so to speak, of carefreeness, and therefore the ancients used them at banquets, where men are to be merry and carefree; and they made wreaths not only for themselves but for the vessels too from which they drank. For this reason flowers suited Bacchus no less than Comus, as I shall show afterwards; for now I return to say that he was a youth, cheerful and merry, because men who drink in moderation awaken their spirits and become bolder and more cheerful, and are then also thought to be of better wit.

> Image of Comus, god of banquets, according to Philostratus, signifying that modest banquets gladden men and, awakening their spirits, make them bold, and that, on the contrary, immoderate food makes a man sleepy, inept, dull of wit, and weak of body.

<aside>Bacchus head of the Muses</aside>

Hence the ancients made Bacchus, like Apollo, the head and leader of the Muses. And poets were once crowned no less with ivy, consecrated to Bacchus, than with laurel, the plant of Apollo. Hence the fables feigned that Bacchus was brought up by the Muses in Nysa, a most pleasant place in Arabia, after which he was later called Dionysus.

<aside>Water put in wine</aside>

From him, as Athenaeus reports, Amphitryon, king of the Athenians, learned before all others to put water in wine, which was of the greatest benefit to mortals; and therefore in the temple of the Horae he set up an altar to him, because these, which are the seasons of the year, as was said in their image, make the vine grow and bear fruit.

<aside>Silenus</aside>
<aside>Pausanias</aside>

And beside it he placed another to the Nymphs, as a reminder that wine ought to be used diluted, since by them are often understood the waters of fountains and rivers that are good to drink, and because the Muses too, who are often the same as the Nymphs, were (as I said) the nurses of Dionysus, just as Silenus was his tutor. And so Silenus always goes with Bacchus, carried by an ass, both because of his age, since he was very old, and because he was also drunk for the most part, as was shown by whoever depicted Drunkenness giving him drink among the Eleans in a certain temple of his, which was his alone, according to Pausanias, and not shared with Bacchus, as all the others were, perhaps to show that the power of the two of them was equal.

In Plautus, Silenus appears as a great counsellor of Bacchus, having come on stage riding an ass to speak the prologue of the Bacchides, and he says that the two are always of one will. He is also represented as a god of Nature, and in Virgil he sings of its beginnings, compelled by two little Satyrs and a beautiful nymph, who, having found him asleep in a certain cave, quite drunk, with a large drinking vessel beside him, bound him with his own wreaths, woven of various flowers, which had fallen from his head; and the beautiful nymph stained his face, whose veins were all swollen with wine, with blood-red mulberries, at which he laughed and showed that he was pleased, when he was awakened.

<aside>King Midas</aside>
<aside>Plutarch</aside>

And these creatures seemed unwilling to say what they knew except under compulsion. Hence we read that Midas, king of Phrygia, wishing to learn something not very plain to men, hunted one of these Sileni for some time, and at last caught him by the smell of wine, which he generously spilled in a certain fountain, which Pausanias writes was still shown in his time for this reason. And Plutarch reports that this king learned from Silenus that it was much better for a man to die soon than to live long.

<aside>Pliny</aside>

It is told in Pliny that on the island of Paros, from which came that most beautiful white marble, when they split a large piece of it, they found inside the image of Silenus. How it was made will easily be known by whoever, besides what I have now said of it, looks at what I said of the Satyrs when drawing the image of Pan: for Pausanias writes that these were called Sileni when they were old, since they grew old and died, though they were thought to be gods.

<aside>Diodorus</aside>
<aside>Bacchus in two ways</aside>

We read in Diodorus that the statues of Bacchus were made in two ways, one rather severe, with a long beard, the other beautiful, with a cheerful, delicate and youthful face; understanding by the former that wine drunk beyond measure makes men terrible and irascible, and by the latter that it makes them merry and cheerful when drunk in moderation; leaving aside for now that there was not one Bacchus only but two, or perhaps even three, because that would be rather to write his history than to paint him.

<aside>Bacchus the same as the Sun</aside>

Macrobius, who, as I have said several times already, holds that by all the gods are understood the powers of the Sun, understanding the same of Bacchus too, says that his image was made at times as a child, at times as a youth, now as a bearded man who has already reached full age, and now as an old man, because all these different ages are seen in the Sun. For at the time of the winter solstice, when the days begin to grow, he may be said to be a small child, and at the spring equinox he has already taken on much strength and become a youth; and when he reaches the summer solstice, at which time the days can grow no more, he is a man of full age and has a beard; but because from then on his light begins to fail us, as if his strength failed with it, he afterwards becomes as an old man.

> Image of Bacchus signifying the effects of wine, of which he was the inventor; and, according to Macrobius, the various effects of the Sun, since by him the Sun is understood, that is, the variety of the seasons of the year; and animals sacred to him.

<aside>Horns of Bacchus</aside>
<aside>Martianus</aside>

And since horns were also added to the statues of Bacchus, some have taken these to mean the rays of the Sun. But Diodorus writes that this was because Bacchus was the first to show mortals how to yoke oxen together, put them to the plough, and with them cultivate the fields. Hence Martianus puts in his right hand a sickle, which showed the cultivation of the fields, as I have already said in the image of Saturn, because the vines must be pruned with this if one wants them to produce grapes abundantly, and in the left a drinking vessel, and then describes him as quite cheerful and pleasant in appearance.

<aside>Athenaeus</aside>
<aside>Persius</aside>
<aside>Catullus</aside>

Some understand by the horns boldness, as though drinking much makes men bold, daring, and often insolent too; so say Philostratus, Festus and Porphyrion. But Athenaeus shows best of all, on the authority of many of the ancients, the different effects that wine produces in us, when it is drunk in moderation and when we drink it beyond measure; and from Persius, from Catullus and from other poets it can be gathered that in the sacrifices of this god they used horns.

<aside>Musonius</aside>
<aside>Bacchus in the form of a bull</aside>
<aside>Drinking vessels of horn</aside>
<aside>Theopompus</aside>

Musonius writes as follows on this point. Not only were horns given to Bacchus, but he was also called Bull by some poets, because the fables feigned that Jupiter, changed into a serpent, lay with Proserpina his daughter, who, being made pregnant by it, later gave birth to Bacchus in the form of a bull; hence among the Cyzicenes his image had the face of a bull, perhaps because the ancients drank from the horns of oxen, or from vessels made of horn, since Theopompus writes that in Epirus there were oxen with horns so large that whole drinking vessels were made of them, to which they fitted at the top, around the mouth, some a ring of gold and some of silver; and he goes on to prove, by the testimony of many, that the ancients used the horns of oxen in place of drinking vessels, so that the Athenians too drank from certain silver vessels made in the shape of horns.

<aside>King Lysimachus</aside>
<aside>Statue of Seleucus</aside>

Some have moreover held that by the horns we should understand a few locks of hair that came down on both sides of the head, as in our day we see the Armenian priests have, who are then shaved over the forehead and at the nape. And they take it that the statue of Bacchus was made in this way, and not that he truly had horns. And they say that King Lysimachus was likewise depicted with horns for this reason, as can be seen on some of his ancient medals. And the statue of Seleucus, who was surnamed Nicator, was also given horns, as Suda reports, not for this reason, but because a bull that had been set out for sacrifice had escaped from Alexander, and Seleucus seized it by the horns and held it fast.

<aside>Seneca</aside>

That Bacchus had long hair is shown by Seneca, when he says thus:

> Without shame Bacchus lets his long locks fly,
> wanton and soft, and carries light thyrsi,
> brandishing them with a trembling hand,
> nor is he ashamed to walk with slow step
> and to trail behind him the wide, long robe,
> all adorned with barbarian gold.
>
> *(Seneca)*

<aside>Chorus of Ariadne</aside>
<aside>Catullus</aside>

For they sometimes dressed Bacchus in women's clothing too, as Philostratus does in the picture of Ariadne, when he paints him going to her in a beautiful robe, purple, long and ample, and crowned with roses. Nor was it needful to depict him otherwise in that act of love, because he was going to unite himself in love with Ariadne when she was abandoned by Theseus; and so all these who were almost always with him, such as bold and fierce women, various lovely nymphs, Sileni, Satyrs, Silvans and others like them (who, as Strabo writes, were the ministers and followers of Bacchus, and were called the chorus and company of Ariadne, carved once in white marble by Daedalus in Crete), follow him crying out with joyful voices, as is read in Catullus.

> Some went shaking the green thyrsi,
> some carried the torn limbs
> of the calf; one part
> girded themselves with twisted serpents,
> and another part, carrying in hollow baskets,
> celebrated the fair mysteries,
> mysteries sought in vain by the impious.
> Some struck with open palms
> the resounding timbrels, or with rods
> of bronze made a light and slight sound.
> And some made the air resound
> with shrill horns [?2], and many made
> the song of foreign pipes be heard.
>
> *(Catullus)*

<aside>Phallus</aside>

These were almost all the mysteries of Bacchus, and the ceremonies that they used at his feasts, which were at first celebrated with such pomp. An amphora of wine with vine branches was carried in front, and it was followed by one who dragged a goat behind him; then came one who carried a basket of nuts, and last was the Phallus, which was the image of the virile member.

Plutarch relates it thus, where he speaks of the desire for riches, which began to despise those poor things even at the Bacchanalia, and introduced two golden vessels, precious garments, and carriages with sumptuous masquerades, as anyone who wishes can see in Athenaeus, who describes one of these most ostentatious Bacchanalian processions, staged once by Ptolemy Philadelphus, since for me to relate it here would serve for nothing but wasting time.

> Images of Bacchus, and of the procession, feasts and Bacchanalian ceremonies, and the dress of the Bacchantes, or Maenads, his followers, signifying that the Bacchanalian sacrifices purged souls of their sins, as wine purges them of their thoughts.

<aside>Sieve of Bacchus</aside>
<aside>Drunkenness the sacrament of Bacchus</aside>

They also used to carry the sieve given to Bacchus and placed among his sacred things; because, as Servius says, the ancients believed that the sacraments of Bacchus greatly helped the purification of souls, and that by his sacred mysteries souls were purged just as grain is purged with a sieve. And Boccaccio reports that some believed that this purification was brought about in men by drunkenness, which is the sacrament of Bacchus, because when this has passed, whether by vomiting or in some other way, and the brain has settled, it seems that the mind has forgotten every trouble and, having stripped off all irksome thoughts, remains cheerful and tranquil, as Seneca also says, where he writes of tranquillity of mind.

<aside>Father Liber</aside>

Some have said that Bacchus was called Father Liber because a man who drinks freely frees himself (*si libera*) from tiresome thoughts, and speaks much more freely than when he is sober. But there have been others who held that he was rather so called from Liberty (*Libertà*), of which he was believed to be the god, because, as Plutarch writes, he fought a good deal for it. Hence the ancients, as Servius says in his commentary on Virgil, were accustomed to set up in free cities, as a sure sign of liberty, the idol of Marsyas, who was one of the Satyrs, ministers of Bacchus.

<aside>P. Munatius</aside>
<aside>Marsyas</aside>

And it is told in Pliny that Publius Munatius was put in prison because he took from the statue of Marsyas a wreath of flowers and put it on his own head. Of Marsyas the fables have also said that he was flayed by Apollo, because he challenged him to play, having found the pipe that had been thrown away by Minerva; at which the nymphs and the other Satyrs wept so much that with their tears they made that river which from his name was called Marsyas.

<aside>Suda</aside>

But the truth was that this man was an excellent musician and the inventor of the pipe, as Athenaeus reports from Metrodorus. As Suda writes, Marsyas lost his wits, threw himself into the river and drowned there, and the river was afterwards called Marsyas after his name. And Pausanias writes that on the citadel of Athens there was an idol of Minerva striking Marsyas, because he had picked up the pipe thrown away by her.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

But returning to the robe of Bacchus, they say that it was a woman's, because too much drinking weakens the strength and makes a man soft and unmanned, like a woman. Hence Pausanias writes that among the Eleans, on the Chest of Cypselus, Bacchus was carved with a beard, in a robe long down to the ground, and that, lying in a certain cave surrounded by vines and other fruit trees, he held out a cup with his hand.

<aside>Bassareus</aside>

We read also that Bacchus was called Bassareus from a certain sort of long robe that he used, which the priests likewise afterwards used in his sacrifices, called bassara, from a certain place in Lydia where it was made, or else from the skins of foxes, called bassarae in Thrace, which his followers the Bacchantes put around themselves, who for this reason were likewise called bassarae.

They were also called Maenads, which means mad and frenzied, because at his feasts they went with hair loose and thyrsi in hand, making the gestures of women possessed, to represent what those same women did when they first went with Bacchus, at the time when, showing himself quite wanton, he had with him almost an army of valiant women, by whose work, while he ran through the whole world, he subdued some kings.

<aside>Poplar a tree of the underworld</aside>

Nor did those women dress only in the skins of foxes, but for the most part also in those of panthers and tigers, carrying the thyrsus in hand and spreading their hair to the wind, which they sometimes bound with wreaths of ivy and sometimes of white poplar, because this was believed to be a tree of the underworld and to have grown on the banks of Acheron; and therefore the ancients gave it to the ministers of Bacchus, because they held him too to be a god of the underworld. Hence, as I have already said, the fables feigned that he was born of Proserpina, which is true whenever by his name the Sun is understood, of which I said in his image that it is sometimes taken for a god of the underworld.

<aside>Claudian</aside>

And as I have drawn the Bacchantes, so Bacchus himself is also often depicted, as Claudian describes him, saying:

> Bacchus comes, cheerful, crowned and girt
> with triumphal ivy, and his shoulders
> are covered by the dreadful skin of an Hyrcanian tiger.
> Then, moist with wine, with the thyrsus
> he steadies his steps, and so helps himself on the way.
>
> *(Claudian)*

<aside>Ferula given to Bacchus</aside>

And what Claudian here says of the thyrsus, others have said of the ferula: that Bacchus supports himself on his feet with it, and they have put it in the hand of all those who go with him.

<aside>Eusebius</aside>
<aside>Diodorus</aside>

Eusebius gives the reason for this, taken from Diodorus, saying that since at the beginning men, drinking a great deal, became drunk, and therefore, like madmen and raving, often came to blows with one another, and wounded each other savagely with thick, hard sticks, so that many died, Bacchus persuaded them to carry light ferulas instead of hard pieces of wood, because even if they struck one another with these, no harm followed afterwards, for the ferula is a plant very like the reed. And because its leaves are very pleasing to asses, the ass too was given, as Pliny writes, to that god to whom the ferula belonged.

<aside>Diodorus</aside>

Besides, Diodorus writes that Bacchus armed himself in the wars, and sometimes also put the skins of panthers around him, for he was not always drunk, but fought often, and so valiantly that he overcame many kings, such as Lycurgus, Pentheus and others; he subdued all of India, and returning from there victorious on an elephant he led a fine triumph.

<aside>Triumph invented by Bacchus</aside>
<aside>Magpie given to Bacchus</aside>

Nor do we read that anyone before him had ever triumphed for wars won, and therefore to Bacchus, as the first triumpher, the magpie was consecrated, a garrulous and talkative bird, because at triumphs everyone shouted, and everyone was allowed to reproach the one who triumphed with his vices, and by shouting could say every ill of him, as Suetonius writes of Caesar.

> Images of Bacchus the triumpher and inventor of the triumph, after conquering India, and of his chariot drawn by tigers and panthers, with various plants sacred to him and many animals besides, which signify the nature and effects of wine and drunkenness.

<aside>Wreaths invented by Bacchus</aside>

The ancients also attributed to this god the invention of wreaths, according to Pliny, who says that he was the first to make them of ivy. Hence Alexander the Great, wishing to imitate him when he returned victorious from India, had his whole army crown itself with ivy.

<aside>Why ivy is given to Bacchus</aside>

This plant was given to Bacchus for many reasons, as many have written. Festus holds that it was because he is always as young as it is always green; or because, as it binds everything it clings to, so wine binds human minds. Plutarch says that ivy has in itself a certain hidden power and force that moves human minds out of place and as it were fills them with fury, so that without drinking wine men afterwards seem drunk.

<aside>Kissos</aside>

The Greeks call ivy *kissos* (*Cisso*); *cissare*, if we draw their words to our way of speaking, means to be given to lust. For this reason Eustathius writes that ivy was given to Bacchus as a sign of lust, to which men are greatly incited by wine; hence there is an ancient proverb that Venus can do nothing without Bacchus.

<aside>Thyrsus</aside>

When Macrobius gives the reason for the thyrsus given to Bacchus, which was a spear with a sharp iron at the tip, entwined with ivy, he says that the ivy showed that men ought to bind with the bonds of patience the angers and furies by which they are so ready to do harm to others, because this plant encircles and binds wherever it grows.

<aside>Diodorus</aside>

Diodorus writes that the Egyptians called ivy the plant of Osiris, and consecrated it to him as discovered by him, and in the sacred ceremonies they made more account of ivy (because in all seasons it has green leaves) than of the vine, which loses them in winter. And this was observed by the ancients in other trees too that stay always green, and for this reason they consecrated the myrtle to Venus and the laurel to Apollo.

But Bacchus was not always crowned with ivy alone, but sometimes with fig leaves also, in memory of a nymph who was named Syca, which among the Greeks means the same as fig tree among us, once loved by him, as the fables say, and then changed into this tree, as is read also of the boy Kissos, likewise loved by him, who then became ivy, and of the nymph Staphyle, who was in the same way changed into a vine when he loved her; so it is no wonder that all these plants were afterwards dear to him, and that he often wanted wreaths of them on his head;

Besides which, the ancients adorned his chariot, shield and spears with the same, and they further made wreaths for him sometimes of narcissus, and sometimes of many other various flowers, as the poets describe him; and Diodorus writes that in time of peace, on solemn days, Bacchus wore beautiful robes, soft, delicate, and all painted with flowers.

<aside>Statius</aside>

And rightly the vine was his plant, as that which suits him more than any other; for if Bacchus shows the wine pressed from the grapes that grow on the vines, what else can be given to him that is more proper to him than the vine? For this reason Statius pictures his chariot covered and entirely surrounded with vine, when he says:

> Already Bacchus is drawing near the maternal walls
> with his chariot all surrounded
> and covered with vine; the panthers
> on one side and on the other go with him,
> and lick the bridles and the other trappings
> sprinkled with wine, the swift tigers.
>
> *(Statius)*

<aside>Timaeus of Tauromenium</aside>
<aside>A pleasant tale</aside>
<aside>Notorious drunkards</aside>

Boccaccio gives this reason for the chariot given to Bacchus: that too much wine often makes the brain of men turn as the wheels of chariots turn. Besides the proof that we see of it every day, this is also attested by a rather pleasant little tale written once by Timaeus of Tauromenium and reported by Athenaeus in his *Suppers*. It concerns certain young men of Agrigento, a city of Sicily, who, having gathered one evening to feast together in a certain house, drank so much and became drunk in such a manner that it began to seem to them that they were on a galley that was being strangely tossed by the troubled waves of the sea; and their brains so turned that even the next day they all thought they were in a great storm at sea. Fearing that the galley might perhaps go down, they threw out of the windows beds, tables, benches, chests, and whatever they found of the household furniture, it seeming to them that the helmsman commanded it, to lighten her.

Then the sergeants of justice, not knowing what this was, went in there and found all those young men sprawled, some here, some there, on the floor, feeling nothing; and having shaken them so much that they seemed to wake a little, they asked them what they meant to do; and they answered that the toil of the sea had so wearied them that they could do no more, besides the labour they had undergone in putting out of the ship so many goods, which loaded her too much: and I, said one of them, for the great fear that I have had, have withdrawn here below deck.

<aside>House called the Galley</aside>

Those sergeants still wished to bring them back from their folly, but seeing that they were losing time, they went away, having told them to take care in future not to drink more than they needed. And the young men, still stupefied, said: We thank you; and if ever we can come out of so great a storm, one of them went on, and arrive safely in port, we shall place you, when we have returned to our country, among the other gods of the sea, acknowledging that we owe our safety to you. And the good drunkenness lasted many days, so that house was always called the Galley.

<aside>Why panthers are with Bacchus</aside>

The chariot of Bacchus was drawn by tigers and panthers, because wine makes men fierce and terrible, as is the nature of these animals. Philostratus says that panthers go with Bacchus because they are very hot animals and leap lightly, as the Bacchantes did, and as men are often heated by wine far more than is their nature.

<aside>Ship of Bacchus</aside>
<aside>Philostratus</aside>

And he describes his ship too, saying that its prow was in the form of a panther, and that many resounding cymbals were hung around it on the outside; in the middle a long thyrsus was planted in place of a mast, at the top of which were fastened the purple and shining sails, on which Tmolus, a mountain of Lydia, was woven in gold, and the Bacchantes who were running about there. The ship was covered on top with green ivy and with vine bearing most beautiful grapes that hung from green branches, and below, from the lowest bottom, a spring of most sweet wine gushed forth, from which all who were there drank freely.

Thus Philostratus paints the ship of Bacchus in the picture that he makes of the Tyrrhenian pirates, who, thinking they had made a good prize of this god, still youthful and almost a boy, were changed by him into so many dolphins, while they wanted to carry him to a part other than that where he asked to go, as Ovid relates the fable in full, saying that Bacchus, perceiving the deceit of those men, made the ship stop at once, and ivy came in such abundance that it bound all the oars and spread over the mast, the yards and the sails, and he girded his head with green branches of vine with grapes attached, and holding the thyrsus in his hand showed himself accompanied by tigers, panthers and leopards, at which those treacherous pirates had so great a fear that they threw themselves into the sea, where they were then dolphins, as I have said.

> Image of the ship of Bacchus, of Mount Tmolus in Lydia with the Bacchantes running over it, and of the Tyrrhenian pirates who seized Bacchus and were changed by him into dolphins for their misdeeds, signifying that vices and sins make a man lose his reason.

Almost the same ship may be seen in our times too, made in most beautiful figures of mosaic in Rome in the church of Saint Agnes, which was once a temple of Bacchus.

<aside>Plutarch</aside>

The fables have said of him also that when he was a little child the Parcae girded him with most fierce serpents, which without hurting him in the least went over his chest and his face. Hence it came about afterwards that the Bacchantes, celebrating his ceremonies, handled serpents without feeling any harm from them, as Plutarch writes in the life of Alexander, where he speaks of Olympias his mother, to whom it seemed that she had been made pregnant by a serpent; which was believed also by the mother of Scipio, as the same Plutarch reports, because a great snake was often seen entering her room.

<aside>Young bull torn apart in the ceremonies of Bacchus</aside>

Catullus had in mind that ceremony of handling serpents of which I spoke when he said of the ministers and followers of Bacchus that some girded themselves with serpents; just as he showed another mysterious ceremony too, saying that some carried the limbs of the torn young bull. For it is read that Pentheus, king of Thebes, was a despiser of Bacchus and of his ceremonies, and would not have them celebrated in any way. Bacchus took such vengeance for this that he made Pentheus appear to his mother and to the other women who were celebrating the Bacchanalian feasts as a young bull, or a boar, as Ovid says, that had come to disturb the sacred ceremonies; whereupon all of them were at once around him and tore him to pieces, which they then carried in their hands while they went furiously running about, glad of the vengeance; and in memory of this the Bacchantes at times, at the feasts of their god, used to tear a calf apart, and each of them carried off one of the torn limbs.

<aside>Osiris</aside>
<aside>Tibullus</aside>

Of this it could perhaps be said that it was done to represent what the fables relate that Typhon did with the companions of Osiris, because Osiris was in Egypt what Bacchus was among the Greeks; hence Tibullus attributes to him all that we have already said of Bacchus, and describes him thus, saying:

> The first who ever made the plough
> was Osiris, and the first who showed
> how the earth was to be cultivated.
> And how it was then to be sown
> he showed also, and when sweet fruits
> a man might find on the unknown tree.
> From him all the others learned
> to wed the weak vine
> to the stake, so that it might bear better afterwards,
> and to cut those branches by which
> the plant's strength to produce
> the grapes so welcome to mortals is hindered.
> For from these, ripe in their season,
> the rough feet squeeze out the sweet juice,
> as the same Osiris taught to do.
>
> *(Tibullus)*

And after, some verses later, he goes on thus:

> In you, Osiris, no sign is ever seen
> of sadness, and from you always stay far
> the sad thoughts, the weeping and the sighs.
> But a fair chorus singing in joyful measures
> accompanies you ever wherever you go,
> so that love, play and laughter are always with you.
> You are adorned with fair flowers, and your brow
> is girt with ivy, and a golden robe
> that reaches the ground you drag behind you.
> Sometimes a purple one is given you too,
> and with sweet sound there accompanies you
> the hollow pipe and the basket crammed
> with mysteries that are always hidden.
>
> *(Tibullus)*

<aside>Osiris in the form of a hawk</aside>

The Egyptians sometimes depicted this Osiris in the form of a hawk, a bird that sees very well and flies swiftest of all, as the Sun does too, of which he was the image. Hence more often still they depicted him, again in Egypt, as Plutarch writes, in the form of a man who has the natural member erect and a red cloth around him. The reason for this I shall give a little further on, when I take up Priapus, who was a part and member of Osiris.

<aside>Osiris killed and torn apart</aside>

For it is read of him that Typhon his brother, having formed a conspiracy of many against him, killed him, and having cut him into many pieces distributed them all among the conspirators, except the virile member, which none of them wanted, and which was therefore thrown into the Nile, which carried it away. Isis his wife, grieved at the loss of her husband, of whom she did not know what had become, and whom she had been seeking for a good while, as soon as she learned this, went against Typhon and defeated him, and recovered from the conspirators the limbs that they had divided among themselves, which she put all together in order, and not finding among them the one that had been thrown into the Nile, she was extremely sorry for it, and ordered that in future his image should be revered and worshipped with many ceremonies, as it always was afterwards under the name of Priapus.

<aside>Ceremonies of Osiris</aside>
<aside>Lucan</aside>

And in memory of all this she ordered also that every year at a certain time, with solemn ceremony, weeping and lamenting, men should go seeking Osiris, and soon after should hold a feast with great gladness, carrying about with solemnity a beautiful boy who represented Osiris already found. Hence, because this ceremony was renewed every year, Lucan said of him that he was never sought enough.

<aside>Horus</aside>

Almost the same happened with Horus: Isis his mother mourned him for a while, thinking she had lost him, but then found him and was very glad. By him, who was likewise worshipped in Egypt, some, such as Macrobius, have held that the Sun is to be understood, and that from him the Hours (*Hore*), those small parts of time that measure the day, were named. Others have understood the world by him. His idol was that of a youth who held with one hand the shameful parts of Typhon, because it is read that he defeated him; he did not kill him, but he rendered vain all his power, though Typhon, changed into a crocodile, fled from him. Hence there was a law in Apollinopolis, a city of Egypt, which commanded that no regard be had for crocodiles, but that everyone should hunt them and kill as many as he could, and all those that were caught and killed were placed before the temple of Horus.

<aside>Typhon</aside>
<aside>Plato</aside>

Of Typhon the fables feigned, as Apollodorus recounts, that he was begotten by the Earth in vengeance for the Giants once killed by the gods of Heaven. He was of two natures, human and bestial. Hence Plato too in the *Phaedrus* calls him a beast of many natures, burning and furious; and he surpassed in size of body and in strength all that were ever born of the earth.

The upper part was in the form of a man, all covered with feathers, so great and so tall that it went above all the highest mountains and often touched the stars with its head; and stretching out his arms he reached with one hand to the West and with the other to the East, and from the one and the other came a hundred serpents, which held their heads forward. The legs were serpents, and there were others around them, which went winding up over the terrible body until they reached the high head, which was covered with horrid and filthy locks that hung down over the neck and shoulders; and such was the beard too, which descended from the great chin over the broad chest; the eyes were terrible and sparkled as if they were of fire, and the wide mouth likewise poured forth most burning flames.

The heavenly gods had such fear of him, when he turned against them, throwing fiery stones towards Heaven, that they fled into Egypt, and did not hold themselves safe even here before they were changed into various animals, as I have already said of many of them in the images drawn so far. But in the end he was defeated by Jupiter, according to Apollodorus, or, as others have held, as I said a little above, by Horus, who, though he had a different name, was nevertheless the same as Osiris. Hence in Hermopolis, a city of Egypt, they depicted the hippopotamus with a hawk fighting it while standing on it, and by the former they meant Typhon, image of all the evil that comes from the earth, and by this the virtue that resists him and renders vain all his furious onset, shown through Osiris, or Horus, who are therefore the Sun, just as for other reasons they were Bacchus; and as it was said in Egypt of Osiris that Typhon cut him into pieces, so the Greeks said that the Titans did the same to Bacchus.

> Images of Typhon, brother of Osiris, and of Horus, son of Osiris, who is Bacchus among the Greeks, which Horus defeated the said Typhon, though changed into a crocodile; with the hippopotamus and the hawk, hieroglyphs denoting Virtue, which fights evil, resists it, and in the end defeats and tramples it.

<aside>Bacchus torn apart</aside>

This, as I said, perhaps the Bacchantes represented with the limbs of the torn calf. But that Bacchus was killed by the Titans, cut in pieces and cooked, and afterwards put together again and smeared with plaster, so that he might no longer be recognised, as Suda reports, signifies that the grapes are trodden and all broken by the peasants who press the wine from them, which boils, purging itself, in the great vessels, not only of wood but of stone also, and at times of plaster, and seems almost to be cooked; and some do cook it, as though it would keep better that way afterwards; and afterwards the torn limbs are put together again, because the vine in its season produces the grapes whole again.

<aside>Bacchus with the Eleusinian goddesses</aside>

Moreover, because Bacchus was believed also by some of the ancients to be that hidden power which gives all plants the strength to produce ripe fruits, Herodotus writes that he was a numen familiar to the Eleusinian goddesses, and went often with them. These were, as I said already, Ceres and Proserpina, who were believed to make the scattered seed germinate. And it is read in Pausanias also that the Athenians had in the temple of Ceres, among the other idols, that of Bacchus too, which held out a burning torch in its hand.

<aside>Porphyry</aside>

Hence Porphyry said, according to Eusebius, that Bacchus was represented with horns and dressed as a woman, to show that in plants both powers are present, of male and of female; and although it is read of the palm that it has both, and that it bears badly unless both are close together, nevertheless it is seen that in general every plant produces leaves and fruits by itself, without any other joining it, which is not so with animals, because these cannot generate unless the male and the female join together.

Hence perhaps the fables feigned that Priapus was born of Bacchus, to show the whole seminal power, which takes its strength from the Sun, in plants as in the other things produced by the earth. This was understood also in the image of Osiris that I drew a little above, the red cloth that he had around him showing that celestial heat which gives strength to the seed down to the bowels of the earth.

<aside>Suda</aside>
<aside>Priapus</aside>

And Suda writes that Priapus is the same as Bacchus, who in Egypt was called Horus, whose image was in the form of a youth who holds a sceptre in his right hand, as if he were lord of all that is born in this world of ours, and in the left the natural member, erect and extended, because the hidden seminal power comes from him; he has wings, to show how swift he is, and beside him is the disc. This was a broad, flat, round thing made of stone or metal, with which the ancients exercised themselves by throwing it up high, and here it showed the roundness of the universe, because the Sun, which is understood by Horus, encircles the world through the three that I mentioned [?3].

> Image of Horus, god of the Egyptians, who is Priapus and also Bacchus, understood as the seminal power and as the Sun; with the drawing of the disc signifying the roundness of the world, which is illuminated by the Sun and into which the Sun pours its power.

<aside>Ceremonies of the Bacchanalia</aside>
<aside>Phallophori</aside>

To show how alike Bacchus and Priapus were, or perhaps even one and the same thing, the ancients at the feasts of the Bacchanalia used to carry round the neck the figure of the virile member made of fig wood, and called by them Phallus, which they made afterwards of red leather also, as Suda reports; and having fastened it in front between their thighs, they went leaping with it in honour of Bacchus, and were then called Phallophori. They also covered their faces with very thin bark of trees, or with some skin, and wreathed their heads with ivy or violets.

<aside>Herodotus</aside>
<aside>Priapus</aside>

Herodotus writes that instead of this the Egyptians devised making some little statues, only a cubit long, with the natural member extended and almost greater than the whole body, which the women carried about at certain times through the villages, on certain little carts made on purpose for this, with pipes in front, singing in honour of Bacchus. And the Roman women afterwards did the same, carrying this member about with solemn pomp, and many ceremonies were ordained for it, which I pass over for worthy reasons; besides, they are of no use in drawing the image of Priapus. He was depicted as a boy, stout, ugly and ill-made, with the virile sign as large as all the rest of the body, like the little statues I have just spoken of, as Suda also describes him. Suda says that Juno, by touching Venus's belly, caused him to be born so, out of spite against Jupiter her husband, who had made Venus pregnant; although it is read also that Bacchus was the father of Priapus, as I have said above, and as Theodoret reports, who gives the reason for it by saying that by Venus wanton pleasure is understood, and by Bacchus the heat of wine drunk without measure, and that when these different things join together, Priapus is born of them, because he who lay down before rises thus and makes himself seen, and perhaps it was not known that he was there.

<aside>The god Mutinus</aside>
<aside>Varro</aside>
<aside>Lactantius</aside>
<aside>Saint Augustine</aside>

Like him, or rather the same, was the god Mutinus, who, seated, likewise showed a great member; and the new brides, before coupling with the husband, went to sit on his lap with solemn ceremony, wishing to show in that way that they gave him the first flower of their virginity, as Varro wrote, and Lactantius and Saint Augustine in the *City of God* have reported.

<aside>God of gardens</aside>
<aside>Tibullus</aside>

Priapus was also called by the ancients the god of gardens, and was therefore depicted in the form of a man with a beard and tousled hair, quite naked, and with a curved sickle in his right hand, as Tibullus describes him, pretending to ask him how it is that handsome youths love him, not at all handsome or adorned; and he speaks thus, in verses drawn into our language.

> O if you, kind Priapus [?4], could have
> shady roofs, so that neither snow nor Sun
> might ever come to touch your bare head!
> Tell me with what art you bring it about
> that every handsome youth wishes you so much good,
> and as best he can reveres and honours you?
> You are not handsome, and full of squalor
> are your uncombed locks, and your beard is tousled.
> Where then does it come from, that everyone loves you?
> You go naked as in the frozen season
> of cold winter, so in the Sun
> of the scorched, glowing summer.
> All these were my words,
> and he answered me with the sickle in hand,
> the rustic offspring of Bacchus, thus.
>
> *(Tibullus)*

> Image of Priapus, god of gardens, and of the virile member, and of the ass and the goat, sacred to him, since he is understood as the seminal or generative power. He is a god who punishes thieves and theft, which is signified in the sickle, and the animals are a sign of powerful generation.

<aside>Horace</aside>

They dressed him sometimes also in a cloth, which he held gathered with his hand, and carried fruits of every sort in the lap. And they made wreaths for him of everything that grew in the gardens, at the guard of which he stood with a long reed on his head to frighten the birds, and threatened with the big member that he held in his hand whoever should go to steal any of the things guarded by him. Hence, when Horace wishes to describe him, Priapus speaks of himself thus:

> I was a fig trunk, good for nothing,
> when the carpenter got me,
> and he was long in doubt what to do.
> Not knowing what to make of me, and now wishing
> to see a bench made of me, he thought
> that to make a Priapus would be far better.
> On this he resolved, and bestowed
> his work on it, and I became a god,
> an immense terror to thieves and birds.
> For, having armed my right hand
> with a curved sickle, I give thieves great fright,
> and with the member from which each of you was born;
> and the reed which I feel planted
> on my head makes every importunate bird
> flee from the gardens swift as the wind.
>
> *(Horace)*

The ass may sometimes also be depicted with Priapus, because the ancients sacrificed it to him as a victim proper to him, either for the likeness of the great member between them, as Lactantius reports; or for the hatred that he bore this beast, because Silenus's ass with its importunate braying disturbed the pleasure that he was preparing to take from Vesta, whom he had found asleep at a certain feast of the Great Mother, as the fable related by Ovid tells; or else because, as is set down by those who write of the stars of Heaven, among which two in the sign of the Crab are called the Little Asses, an ass puffed up by the human speech given to it by Bacchus as a reward for having carried him over a certain river came to a contest with Priapus over the size of the natural member, and won, but to its very great harm, because Priapus, angered at this, killed it; and perhaps the ancients afterwards imitated this by sacrificing the ass to him.

<aside>Goat as a sign of Priapus</aside>

In Egypt, when they wished to show this god in their sacred signs, they depicted a billy goat, because it is read of this animal that, when only seven days old, it begins to mount, and is almost always ready for coupling; so it is no wonder that the member serving generation, worshipped by the ancients under the name of Priapus, was shown by it.

<aside>Apollodorus</aside>
<aside>Goat given to Bacchus</aside>

With the same animal Bacchus too was sometimes shown, because it is found that he changed into it when with the other gods he fled from the hands of Typhon into Egypt. Apollodorus writes that Jupiter changed Bacchus, still a little child, into a kid in order to hide him from Juno, and sent him by Mercury to the nymphs to be nursed, and therefore the goat was afterwards always a very welcome victim to Bacchus; or perhaps it was because this beast is greatly harmful to vines.

<aside>Gregory of Nazianzus</aside>

Besides, it is read that there was sometimes put in the hand of Bacchus a sceptre with the virile member on top, which perhaps showed the common power that Priapus had with him, although some give for it a certain other reason, so little decent that it does not seem to me that I ought to tell it, even if the interpreter of the first oration of Gregory of Nazianzus against Julian the Apostate reports it, and Theodoret, bishop of Cyrrhus, hints at it too.

But I will rather say that the form of the member spoken of so many times appeared in the house of Tarquinius Priscus on the hearth, as the histories recount, and from it a maidservant of his wife, called Ocrisia, who had been seated there, rose pregnant with a son whom she bore in her time, and he was brought up with great care, as though he had been conceived of the seed of the Lar, the household god, and for that reason was to be a great man, as indeed he was, for he was the king of the Romans called Servius Tullius.

<aside>Lar</aside>
<aside>Lampridius</aside>
<aside>Tibullus</aside>

The Lar, or Lares, because there were many, were certain gods, or rather daemons, worshipped by the ancients in their own houses as guardians of them, in a certain place appointed for this besides the hearth, a place which, as I said already, was therefore called a Lararium. There were also other images there, as is read in Lampridius, that Alexander, emperor of Rome, had two Lararia. In the one, which was the larger, he kept Apollonius, Abraham and Orpheus, and had in the other, which was the smaller, Cicero and Virgil. Nor were the Lares guardians of private houses only but of the whole city also, and of the fields too outside, at the country estate, as Tibullus shows when he says:

> And you, Lares, once guardians of the rich,
> now of the poor fields, accept the gifts
> that humbly I offer and consecrate to you.
>
> *(Tibullus)*

<aside>Figures offered to the Lares</aside>
<aside>Lares</aside>

Hence they were often worshipped at the crossroads of the streets, where on certain days they hung up for them some balls and little figures of wool; the former were for the servants, the latter for the others; and each person put up as many of the one and of the other as there were people in the house, so that when the Lares came they would catch hold of these, and not afterwards do harm to the persons; for some believed that they were daemons of the underworld, who, coming above ground when certain days were celebrated for them, would have done harm to the persons, had they not found something to amuse themselves with around the little figures I mentioned.

Or the ancients did this because others said that the Lares were our souls, already gone out of mortal bodies, who came to these feasts, and needed to find some body in which to rest; both of which are gathered from Festus.

<aside>Dog with the Lares</aside>

But for the most part the Lares were thought to be certain private daemons, guardians of houses, and were therefore depicted in the form of youths clothed in the skin of a dog, with the dog also at their feet; the ancients wishing to show by this animal that they were faithful and diligent guardians of houses, formidable to strangers and agreeable to those of the house, just as dogs are, according to Plutarch's report; and Ovid had already written the same, giving the reason why the dog is with the Lares. They were also sometimes clothed in tucked-up garments, turned over the left shoulder so that they come under the right, in order to be more nimble in their office, which was, as the same Plutarch says, to go seeking out all that each one did, and to spy diligently on all human deeds, so that through them the impious and wicked men might then be punished for their misdeeds.

<aside>Penates</aside>
<aside>Cicero</aside>

Like these Lares were the Penates, at least in guarding cities and keeping good watch over them; and some held that among the Romans they were Jupiter, Juno and Minerva. Others said that they were Apollo and Neptune, who built the walls of Troy. Cicero wrote that the Penates were certain numina in private houses, worshipped in the most secret parts of them. Hence Demipho in Terence says that he wishes to go home to greet the Penates, to return afterwards to the square to his business; and from this it is seen that these too, no less than the Lares, dwelt familiarly in houses; and their image, as Timaeus the historian writes, was two long, twisted rods of iron, like those that soothsayers held in their hands when they took auguries, with a certain earthen vessel; and the ancients kept these things among their sacred mysteries.

<aside>Dionysius</aside>

We read in Dionysius that in a certain small temple, not far from the Roman Forum, there were two figures of youths seated, each of whom held in his hand a pilum, which was a kind of spear once used by the Romans in war, with letters that said, Gods Penates, and that in many other ancient temples similar images of youths were seen in military dress and adornment; and such are to be seen also on some ancient medals.

> Images of the gods Lares, that is, guardians of private houses and of individual cities, and observers of human deeds, or harmful gods; and image of the gods Penates, and their hieroglyph, signifying that they too are household gods and guardians of the cities and houses of private persons.

<aside>Genius</aside>
<aside>Horace</aside>

Besides these the Genius also was a household numen, proper to each man, whom some held to be the god of hospitality, of pleasure, of good times and of nature; and therefore it is said of one who gives himself a good time and does all that nature puts before him that he agrees with his Genius, but that he who does the contrary wrongs it. Horace, writing to Julius Florus, discourses on the instability of the things of the world and the various wills of men; then he poses a question, how it comes that of two brothers one will delight to be always at his ease, the other always to toil, and he answers thus:

> The Genius knows it, god of Nature,
> who tempers and governs the natal star
> of each and always accompanies him,
> and changes often, so that he shows himself
> now white and beautiful, now ugly and black.
>
> *(Horace)*

> Images of the good and the evil Genius, guardian and observer of the birth of man, of actions, of cities and of private places.

<aside>Censorinus</aside>
<aside>Double Genius</aside>

Others, such as Censorinus, have said that the Genius was worshipped by the ancients as the god of generation, either because he has the care of this, or because he was begotten together with us, and then always stays with us as our guardian; and they therefore held that there were as many Genii as there were men, as if one were given to each; or even that there were twice as many, and that each had two, one good and one bad: the former always exhorts and encourages to good, the latter to evil, just as we Christians say precisely of our guardian Angels and of the daemons, zealous tempters; except that these are not born with us, as the ancients understood that the Genii were born, each with each.

<aside>Genius of the Prince</aside>

The same they said also of the Lares, so that these differed little from one another; and therefore the Romans placed at the crossroads of the streets and in the country estates the Genius of Augustus with the Lares, and worshipped them together. Yet each one worshipped his own Genius by himself too, celebrating his birthday gladly and with much pleasure, but that of the Prince was revered by everyone more than all the others. Hence whoever had sworn falsely by the Genius of the Prince would have been punished at once, because among the ancients this was a most grave oath. And therefore Caligula, a very cruel prince, when putting many to death for the slightest causes, as Suetonius recounts, used to say this of some of those whom he had put to death, that he did so because they had never sworn by his Genius, as though they thereby despised him and showed that they judged him not worthy to be worshipped.

<aside>Iamblichus</aside>
<aside>Genius of places</aside>

The Genius was therefore a certain numen that from their very birth always accompanied men; and such numina were sometimes given to places also, as the philosopher Iamblichus says, showing that to those gods who are particular guardians and wardens of some place sacrifice must be made of those things that grow there, because the things governed are dearer than others to him who governs them.

<aside>Virgil</aside>

And Virgil describes a great serpent appearing to Aeneas while he is renewing the funeral rites for his father Anchises:

> whose back is green, painted
> with golden spots, and the scaly
> back, shining, resembles the heavenly bow,
> which among the clouds, set against the Sun,
> shows with great beauty many different colours.
>
> *(Virgil)*

He leaves in doubt whether that was the genius of the place, or something else. Hence some have depicted the Genius in the form of a serpent, some others of a child, others of a youth, and others of an old man, as Cebes in his *Tablet*.

<aside>Sosipolis</aside>

Pausanias writes that the Eleans worshipped a certain god under the name of Sosipolis, which means Saviour of the City, as their Genius, proper to the country. He was in the temple of Lucina, and they sacrificed to him every year with certain ceremonies; and the reason for it was that the Arcadians having once marched against the Eleans on account of a certain war that was between them, a woman who had a little baby at the breast in her arms said to the captains of the Eleans: Sirs, this is my son, and when I bore him, not long ago, I was commanded in a dream to give him to you as a companion in war, and therefore here he is, I give him to you.

The Eleans did not at all disdain the good woman, but rather, taking it that this was not without some great mystery, took the baby and set him quite naked at the front of their army; where the Arcadians, who attacked them soon afterwards, saw him change at once into a great serpent, at which they were all so terrified that they dared go no further, but turning their backs took to flight, so that it was easy for the Eleans to drive them from their borders. Victorious for this reason, the Eleans called that child Sosipolis, crediting him with the preservation of the city. Serpent as he was, he seemed to thrust himself underground into a certain cavern, where the Eleans afterwards built a temple in the name of Lucina, and made also, as we would say, a chapel to Sosipolis, ordaining there honours and ceremonies proper to the one and to the other, because they believed that she had caused him to be born and had sent him for their safety.

His image, although he changed into a serpent, as I have said, was that of a child with a robe of various colours about him, loaded with stars, who held out with his hand the horn of plenty, because such he once appeared, as Pausanias says, to a man who afterwards reported it.

<aside>Medals of Hadrian</aside>

On some ancient medals of Hadrian, and of other emperors too, the Genius is seen depicted in the guise of a man, who holds out with his right hand a drinking vessel, which he appears to pour over an altar all adorned with flowers, and a whip hangs from his left side. And on other medals, also of Hadrian, is the image of a man of war with a robe wrapped around him down to mid-leg, who holds in his right hand something like a cup, as one who sacrifices, and has the horn of plenty in his left, and there are letters round about that say: To the Genius of the Roman People, which was perhaps to show that numen kept so secret by the Romans that they did not at all wish its name to be known, as I have said at another time.

<aside>Plane tree given to the Genius. Tibullus.</aside>

The ancients also made wreaths for the Genius from the branches of the plane tree, whose leaves are little unlike those of the vine; and sometimes also of various flowers, as is read in Tibullus, where he writes thus:

> Now with fair flowers girding his sacred locks
> let the Genius come to see what in his honour
> we do, celebrating his joyful name.
>
> *(Tibullus)*

<aside>Euclid the Socratic</aside>

But since I have already said that the Genii were two, as Euclid the Socratic holds, according to Censorinus, let us now see the other, that is the evil one, how he was represented, for the good one is he whom we have drawn up to now. Of the evil one I have not found that the ancients made any statue or image; but it is indeed read that he appeared to many, and I shall portray him as they saw him, according to the example that the histories have preserved for us.

<aside>Plutarch. Appian. Florus.</aside>

Plutarch, Appian, Florus and others write that Brutus, having withdrawn at night into his room all alone, but with a lamp, to think by himself, as he was accustomed to do, saw an image of a man appear before him, all black and frightful, who said to him, when he asked, that he was his evil Genius, and soon vanished.

<aside>Evil Genius. Valerius Maximus.</aside>

Valerius Maximus writes that the wicked Genius appeared likewise to a certain Cassius, who was of the faction of Mark Antony, a few days before Caesar had him beheaded, and it was in the form of a very large man, of a dusky colour, with long hair and a horrid, unkempt, tousled beard.

And among the people of Temesa, once a people of Italy, in the Abruzzo, there was a very wicked and gloomy Genius, who was of a dusky and dark colour, wholly formidable to see, clothed in a wolf's skin, and who did such harm to those people that, as Pausanias recounts, and Suda also reports, they would have abandoned the country if the oracle had not shown them the way to appease the ghost of a companion of Ulysses, who was killed there because, being drunk, he had done violence to a young girl: for this was the wicked Genius who went about taking vengeance, of which Ulysses, passing by, took no account.

<aside>Evil Genius driven out</aside>

The people of Temesa therefore, by the counsel of the oracle, built a temple to him, and vowed to sacrifice to him every year one of the most beautiful girls of the city. As they did so, that diabolical Genius troubled them no more; but he stayed in the temple to receive the cruel sacrifice, until he was driven out of it by Euthymus, a man of great valour, who, arriving there just at the time when the wretched sacrifice was to be made, and having learned the cause of it, was moved to pity for the misery of that people, but more for the beautiful girl destined for the cruel sacrifice, for whom he felt himself at once kindled with a most ardent love, and therefore stopped everything. At this the cruel beast, enraged, came against him with the greatest fury, but Euthymus withstood it so well that after fighting a good while together he remained the victor, and chased it until he drove it to go and drown itself in the sea, and delivered that people from so great a calamity. For this reason the people gave him the girl he had freed for his wife, since he would have no other reward, and with the greatest feasting and gladness had the joyful wedding celebrated.

## Doubtful passages

- [?1] "ma ci habbiamo spesso" (scan 0384) is evidently corrupt in the printed text (perhaps "c'inganniamo", "we deceive ourselves"); the rendering "we often err in this" follows the sense of the sentence, which goes on to explain why drinking wine to restore moisture is a mistake.
- [?2] "Strideuoli cerni" (scan 0390): "cerni" makes no sense and is surely a misprint for "corni" (Catullus has *cornua*, horns), which agrees with Cartari's own remark that horns were used in the sacrifices of Bacchus; rendered "shrill horns".
- [?3] "per gli tre, ch'io dissi" (scan 0402): it is not clear which "three" are meant; no three things through which the Sun encircles the world have been named earlier in this passage. Rendered literally.
- [?4] "Deh se tu possi hauer a'mo Priapo" (scan 0404): "a'mo" is obscure; perhaps "almo" (kind, nourishing), which is how I have taken it, or an abbreviated word of address. The sense of the verse (wishing Priapus shady roofs) is not affected.
