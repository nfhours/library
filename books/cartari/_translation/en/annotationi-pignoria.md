**Annotations of Lorenzo Pignoria on the Book of Cartari's Images.**

<aside>Fol. 2, line 3.</aside>
That no light of religion was ever attributed by anyone to animals is not wholly true. For the Egyptians said great things about their sacred animals, such as the cynocephalus, the scarab, and others. On this see Horapollo, and our Commentary on the Hieroglyphic Table, which once belonged to Cardinal Bembo, of glorious memory, and is now to be seen in the Gallery of His Most Serene Highness the Duke of Mantua. Indeed, particular things of great wonder are told of elephants. And read Aelian in Book 7 of the *History of Animals*, at chapter 39, and in Book 4, at chapter 9; Pliny in Book 8, at chapter 1. Among the moderns, Porcacchi on Sannazaro's *Arcadia*, at fol. 174 of the Pavia edition of 1596.

<aside>Line 14.</aside>
The Latins call God DEVS, which without doubt comes from the Greek word ΖΕΥΣ (Zeus), changing the Z to D, as has been the custom in all languages, on the testimony of Claudius Mitalerius on Valerius Maximus. Some others hold that it derives from the Greek word ΔΕΣ [?1], which means fear; hence Petronius Arbiter said:

> Fear first made gods in the world.
> *(Petronius Arbiter)*

Lactantius Placidus, commentator on Statius Papinius, was of this opinion, in Book 3 of the *Thebaid*, at verse 661, where he cites Lucan and Mintanor the musician, who is reported also by Fulgentius in the first book of the *Mythologies*. Arnulf, bishop of Lisieux in France, alludes to this view in the sermon he delivered at the Council of Tours, and he confirms it with the verse of Psalm LX:

> You have given an inheritance to those who fear your name, O Lord.
> *(Psalm LX)*

Diogenes Laertius writes some fine things about the name of God in the life of Zeno, near the end.

<aside>Fol. 3, line 18.</aside>
This senate of twelve of the great gods is read in two verses of Ennius in Martianus Capella, in the first book of the *Marriage of Philologia and Mercury*. Indeed, antiquity had so apportioned them that one fell to each month, as is plainly seen in the Rustic Calendar published and explained by Fulvio Orsini, a most exquisite observer of ancient curiosities. See Macrobius in Book 1 of the *Saturnalia*, at chapter 12, and the *Sacred Fasti* of Ambrosius Novidius, who with a praiseworthy invention has striven to correct the superstition of the ancients.

<aside>Fol. 5, line 10.</aside>
Lucan does not say that the people of Marseilles paid reverence to tall trunks, and that there were no idols in the woods. Let his verses be the witness:

> — but barbarous in ritual
> were the sacred rites of the gods, altars set up on dreadful altar-tops.
> *(Lucan)*

Here are the altars. Further down:

> — then abundant water falls from black
> springs, and the mournful idols of the gods
> lack art and stand shapeless on hewn trunks.
> *(Lucan)*

Here are the statues. But there is no man who does not err.

<aside>Fol. 9, line 39.</aside>
On the respect paid to statues see Dio Chrysostom in the oration he made to the people of Rhodes, in which he censures the abuse of removing the name from the statues of men of the past and putting another on them. Moreover, read Cassiodorus in Book 7 of the *Variae*, at formulas 13 and 14, where he says that in Rome there was another people of statues; and it must indeed have been so, because although the galleries of princes and of private persons, in Italy and outside Italy, have been adorned with the statues of Rome, the vein is still not exhausted, and every day they are found in such quantity. Let whoever wishes see Justus Lipsius in Book 3 of *On the Greatness of Rome*, at chapter 9, and the Incruscato in his *Compendium*.

<aside>Fol. 15, line 39.</aside>
Statius speaks of Demogorgon in Book 4 of the *Thebaid*, according to the exposition of Lactantius Placidus, an ancient grammarian; and see Mazzoni on Dante, in Book 1, at chapter 63. And perhaps it was to Demogorgon that the sorceress Erichtho wished to allude, in Lucan in Book 6.

<aside>Fol. 17, line 7.</aside>
Some who have sought the reason for this adjective “eternal” (*eterno*) have said that it comes from *eui terno*, as Varro does in Book 5 of *On the Latin Language*. Others have held that it derives from the ether (*etere*), as Isidore attests of some in Book 7 of his *Origins*. I would say with Varro that it has its origin in the Age (*Euo*), which Arnobius in Book III of *Against the Gentiles* calls *Euità*. Censorinus describes it in the book *On the Birthday*, at chapter 16 (I cite the edition of Ludovicus Carrio), as immense, without origin and without end, which always was and always will be in the same manner. On Eternity, whoever wishes to see some refined thought should read Antonio Agostini in the 2nd *Dialogue on Medals*.

<aside>Fol. 25, line 39.</aside>
Theophrastus in the *Characters* writes that the superstitious man, coming upon the anointed stones that are found where three roads meet, will anoint them, and will not depart before he has knelt down before them. On this passage see what Casaubon writes. Lucian too touches on this custom in *The False Prophet*, saying that that swindler, wherever he caught sight of a stone, anointed or wreathed, at once knelt down. It is read in the *Apology* of Apuleius, in the one they call the first, and in Prudentius, in the second book against Symmachus.

A similar point is touched on by the Capitulary of Charlemagne, in the first book, at chapter 64, where it says that some madmen kindled lights to trees, stones and springs. And in the same Capitulary (in the imperfect one, however, that goes about under the name of Laws of the Lombards) is seen a law of King Liutprand that forbids the worship of springs, or of the tree that the peasants call Holy. It is in Book 2, at title 37. See Carolus Paschalius in the book *On Wreaths*, Book 4, chapter 4; Pliny the Younger, Book 8, letter 8; Seneca, letter 41; Justus Lipsius on the 14th book of Tacitus's *Annals*.

<aside>Fol. 29, line 8.</aside>
Martianus Capella does not say that Saturn had, as an ornament of the head, now a serpent, now a lion's head, and now a boar's; but that he seemed now to have the face of a dragon, now of a lion, now of a boar, and so people have sought to represent him. And of these faces I could say nothing else, except that Martianus had an eye to the malignity of the influences of this planet, which, as Natale Conti writes in Book 2 of the *Mythology*, at chapter 2, inclines men to be melancholic, envious, malicious, proud, avaricious, and tenacious in anger.

<aside>Fol. 31, last line.</aside>
Homer speaks of the Prayers in Book 10 of the *Iliad*, in Phoenix's admonition to Achilles. And Andrea Alciato made a most beautiful emblem of it, which is the hundred and thirtieth. And from Homer's Prayers (*Lite*) perhaps the Latin verb *litare* has its origin, which means to obtain by entreaty, as is read in Nonius Marcellus; although Varro derives it from *luere*, which is to pay, as can be seen in the same grammarian; and Hadrianus Turnebus reports it in Book 18, at chapter 20. But in favour of my opinion stands the authority of Festus.

<aside>Fol. 34, line 12.</aside>
Whoever wishes to see exactly this manner of counting on the fingers cannot come upon a better book than that of Bede cited by the Author. But note that the corrected edition of that little book is that of Elias Vinetus; the others will be defective. And the ancient authors are full of this custom of reckoning. Suetonius in the life of the Emperor Claudius, at chapter 21; Quintilian in Book XI of his *Institutes*, at chapter 3; Pliny in Book 34, at chapters 7 and 8; Seneca in letter 88, and in Book 3 *On Anger*, at chapter 33; Aelian in Book 6 of the *History of Animals*, at chapter 57; Martianus Capella in Book 7 of the *Marriage*, etc., right at the beginning; Symposius in Riddle 100; Aristides in the 2nd of the *Sacred Discourses*, and many others.

<aside>Fol. 36.</aside>
The first images of the Seasons are taken from the gold medal of Antoninus Caracalla in Sebastiano Erizzo, and from another, of the Emperor Diocletian, which was to be seen in the study of the late Signor Lelio Pasqualini. The second are according to the Author's own idea, except that in the place of Vulcan and Aeolus, Vesta has been put.

<aside>Fol. 37, line 27.</aside>
What the Author calls a Temple is seen in Rome, near the church of St George, in the Forum Boarium. Bartolomeo Marliani had it drawn in his book, Book 3, chapter 14, and so did Antonio Agostini in Dialogue 4 on Medals, who calls it an Arch, and rightly in my opinion. See Suetonius in the life of Augustus, at chapter 31, and on Suetonius, Livinus Torrentius, bishop of Antwerp, and Casaubon. And our Author himself, a little further down, calls triumphal arches Jani. But whoever wishes fuller information about Janus should read Barnabas Brissonius in Book 1 of the *Formulae*.

<aside>Fol. 43.</aside>
Of the two images of Hebe, one has been taken from the medal of the Emperor M. Aurelius; the other has been represented in the dress of the ancient cupbearers, as can be seen in my book on the offices of slaves among the ancients. On the same medal “Juventas” is read, who among the Latins is the one who among the Greeks is Hebe. See Lambinus on ode 30 of the first book of Horace, and Servius on the 1st book of the *Aeneid*.

<aside>Fol. 46, line 26.</aside>
Whoever wishes more on the Muses should see Goffredo Liuocerio in a refined little book of his, printed with the *Mythology* of Natale Conti.

<aside>Fol. 52, line 28.</aside>
The text of Martianus Capella is corrupt, as I have already shown in my Exposition of the Hieroglyphic Table, which is now to be seen in the Gallery of His Most Serene Highness of Mantua. For it must be understood that on the ship seven brothers german stood at the helm. That on its prow there is seen the figure of a cat; of a lion on the mast; of a crocodile on the outside. See the text; for the correction is clear.

<aside>Fol. 53.</aside>
The figure of Apollo and Daphne has been taken from a most beautiful cameo that belonged to Monsignor Grimani, Patriarch of Aquileia, of most honoured memory; and on the laurel one can read Natale Conti in the *Mythology*, and Ammianus Marcellinus in Book 29.

<aside>Fol. 60, line 33.</aside>
In Rome in the year 1606 I saw a large piece of marble in the square of the Capitol that represented a grotto of Mithraic things, but much damaged and worn. In its substance it bore a great similarity to the figure that has been represented above. On the belly of the bull these words were read: “To the god Sun, the Unconquered, Mithras”; at the bottom of the neck it had these carved: “Nama Sebesio”; at the bottom of the right thigh: “Amycus Seronensis”. There were two figures standing, one on each side, but ruined; and above the grotto a quadriga of the Sun and a biga of the Moon. And I have thought at times that this large piece of marble was one of the remains of paganism, whose ruin was brought about by that Gracchus of whom St Jerome makes mention in writing to Laeta, and Prudentius at the end of Book 1 against Symmachus. The words of St Jerome are:

> A few years ago your kinsman Gracchus, whose name sounded of patrician nobility, when he held the urban prefecture, did he not overturn, break and burn the Cave of Mithras and all the monstrous idols by which the Raven, Misus, Meles (so Martianus Victorius reads), the Lion, Perses, Helios, Bromius, the Father are initiated?
> *(St Jerome)*

And to the purpose of these names recalled by St Jerome, a passage of Porphyry in Book 4 of *On Abstinence* works marvellously, where he relates that in the mysteries of Mithras the men were called lions, the women lionesses, others ravens, some eagles, and certain ones hawks.

In the antiquities of Rome, printed some time ago and put together in a quite large volume, one of these images of Mithraic things was to be seen, which someone, I do not know who, has explained and reported as a portrait of Agriculture, but out of place in my opinion.

<aside>Fol. 63.</aside>
Beside the image of Apollo is seen the ox Apis, taken from a medal of Julian the Apostate, who, as Ammianus Marcellinus reports in Book 22, had this beast diligently sought out. And whoever wishes more on it, let him see my exposition of the Isiac Table.

<aside>Fol. 66.</aside>
From two ancient intaglios we have taken the images of Serapis and Isis that are seen here; on which whoever wishes more should read the first chapter of the Isiac Table, and see at the end of the same book certain drawings of ancient figures that belonged to Signor Lelio Pasqualini.

<aside>Fol. 70.</aside>
The figure of Aesculapius, which is seen small on a cameo, is taken from the medal of the Emperor M. Antoninus Caracalla, and is like an ancient statue that I saw in Rome eight years ago, in the house of the Signori Massimi, at the Valle. But it must be noted that the engraver, in this one of ours, not understanding Aesculapius's hair, has made him a Janus.

<aside>Fol. 71, line 17.</aside>
Peter Apian (if I am not mistaken) was notably deceived in this figure of Aesculapius. For if it is an ancient thing, which from the garments seems to me not to be, I would say that it was rather either the goddess Copia or one of the seasons of the year. Nor will it seem strange to anyone that I give this note on Apian; for Antonio Agostini too, in his Dialogue 11, says that the said author's book is full of feigned ancient inscriptions.

<aside>Fol. 77.</aside>
In the cameo Providence has been represented, as she is figured on a medal of the Emperor Antoninus Pius, which I have. And it is clearly seen that whoever shaped her wished to say that the Providence of the Emperor is the salvation of the world.

<aside>Fol. 79.</aside>
This figure has been taken from the cameos of the Most Reverend Patriarch Grimani. And on the subject of Aesculapius's daughters it is to be noted that he had four, as many as Aristides names in the oration he composed in praise of Podalirius and Machaon, sons of Aesculapius. And they are Iaso, Aegle, Hygieia, and Panacea. And I remember seeing an ancient copper medal on which a serpent came out of an altar, and beneath it was written Iaso.

<aside>Fol. 95, at the end.</aside>
In the study of the Most Illustrious Signor Federico Contarini, Procurator of St Mark, of glorious memory, there was to be seen a most beautiful ancient carnelian in which, with Cupid looking on, Apollo wrote in a flower these characters I A, and in the flower was inserted a most beautiful youth as far as the navel, who with open arms seemed to lament his misery. See the fable in Ovid in Book 10 of the *Metamorphoses*, who holds that A I A I was written in the flower, against the authority of our sculpture, which, enlarged, is the one that follows.

See Servius on the third eclogue of Virgil, and Probus at the same place; Palaephatus, and Philostratus, the elder and the younger, in the *Imagines*.

Nor less celebrated among writers is the victory that Apollo won over Marsyas, set out in the figures written below, taken from the antique, where in one cameo Marsyas is seen playing the pipes, on which see Apuleius in the first book of the *Florida*; and in another the same victory of Apollo. On which Apollodorus relates that Apollo made a show of his skill with the cithara played backwards, and commanded Marsyas to do the same with the flutes, and since Marsyas did not succeed, he left his skin there.

We shall seal this chapter with the figure of Lucifer, companion of the Dawn, which I once saw on an ancient carnelian by a skilled master, in which, however, I do not see observed the rule of Lactantius Placidus, on the sixth book of Statius's *Thebaid*, namely that his chariot is drawn by a single horse. For he says that the poets give four horses to the Sun, two to the Moon, a single one to the stars. It is quite true that the engraver of the gem may not have been a poet.

<aside>Fol. 94, line 10.</aside>
To show this uniformity of Juno and Diana, beside the image of Lucina the portrait of Juno Lucina, taken from ancient medals, has been placed. But on this subject one should note what Martin Delrio writes on chapter 14 of Genesis; namely that the city of Astaroth Carnaim received this name from an idol of Juno or Diana with two horns, which was worshipped in that city. And whoever reads the theologians of paganism will not judge this exchange strange, since in the same writers greater follies are read about these masquerades, especially according to the custom of the Orientals. And I have touched on something of this in my exposition on the Table of Isis. But who knows whether this two-horned Astarte was not Isis;

I for my part believe it. Nor does the name of Astarte give me trouble, because perhaps the Hebrews applied it to all the female deities, in the way that Baal, or Beel, was applied to all the males. The true interpretation, however, of Astaroth Carnaim I think can be drawn from what Eusebius writes in Book 1 of the *Preparation for the Gospel*, in the last chapter, namely that Astarte, wife of Heaven, made for herself on her head, as an adornment, a pair of horns. The image found in the time of Pope Leo X will be the one set below.

<aside>Fol. 104, line 9.</aside>
This imperial hecatomb is related by Julius Capitolinus in the life of the Emperors Maximus and Balbinus.

<aside>Fol. 106, line 7.</aside>
Whether [?2] the emperors could have had a shortage of these real animals I would not dare to imagine, for since they were masters of the world, what wonder will it be if they had them in hundreds? That Democares, of whom Apuleius makes mention in Book 4 of the *Golden Ass*, had no few animals for the games that he had to put on. Pompey the Great, as Dio writes, at the dedication of his theatre had 500 lions killed. These men did not yet have the means that the emperors afterwards had of gathering together so many beasts. See Suetonius in the Life of Titus, at chapter 7; Claudian in the 3rd Panegyric on Stilicho; and among the moderns Julius Caesar Bulengerus in his treatise *On Hunting*. But I believe that our Author is speaking of the poor, as he seems to declare further on.

<aside>Fol. 107, line 5.</aside>
Nicolas Remy, privy councillor of the Highnesses of Lorraine, has composed three fine and curious books with the title *Demonolatry*, in which, from trials lawfully conducted against wizards and witches, he goes on to show the diabolical tyranny over those wretches who have done homage with their souls to the enemy of souls. Now in Book 1, at chapter 7, at the end, he shows that Empusa and Hecate are demons, who, among the ancients too, were accustomed to appear both by night and at midday, especially when sacrifice was made for the souls of the dead. And to this view he fits the verse of Psalm XC:

> from the arrow flying by day, from the business walking in darkness; from onslaught and the noonday demon.
> *(Psalm XC)*

The same author, in chapter 23 of the same book, goes on to relate in what shapes the demon transforms himself when he wishes to be seen by someone: now into a dog, now into a horse, now into a fly, now into a cat; and many times in human form, though dressed in black and in a long garment, at the first meetings, so as not to frighten with the monstrosity of the feet, which he has inseparably. And I have heard tell from a trustworthy person how in Padua he appeared to a poor man so dressed; but that from beneath the hem of the garment claws were seen like those of an eagle, or of another bird of prey.

Martin Delrio also discusses Hecate in his *Magical Disquisitions*, Book 2, question 27, section 2, and describes her in exactly the manner in which she is seen portrayed in many ancient memorials, in gems and various stones for rings and for what the ancients called amulets. And note that Diana of Ephesus too was represented thus, and is seen in the cameos at fol. 109 and 91 and continually on ancient medals, and Minucius Felix makes mention of it in the *Octavius* with these words:

> Diana meanwhile is a huntress with her dress girt high, and the Ephesian, heaped with many breasts and udders, and Trivia, dreadful with three heads and many hands.
> *(Minucius Felix)*

And these last words explain the cameos that have been placed at fol. 104. But this figure has great conformity with Isis giving milk to Horus, as is seen on an ancient carnelian and elsewhere.

<aside>Fol. 108.</aside>
In the cameo Isis has been represented as she is seen on the ancient medals of Hadrian and Antoninus Pius. It is quite true that the draughtsman forgot to put in her hand the sistrum, which is clearly seen on them. And this figure, in my judgement, signifies the Ship of Isis, which is mentioned in the ancient Rustic Calendar. And on the medal of Antoninus there is seen a port lighthouse, which confirms the conjecture all the more. Read Apuleius in Book 11.

<aside>Fol. 108, line 12.</aside>
The Author calls the sistrum a cymbal, and he is mistaken, because these two instruments were very different from each other, as can be seen in my Commentary on Slaves, at fol. 88 and 91. And a similar licence is seen in Antonio Agostini (if, however, the translator is not to blame for it), who calls the tympanum by the name of crotalum.

<aside>Fol. 110, last line.</aside>
Martianus Capella, cited by the Author, makes no mention of cymbals, but of Nilotic sistra. What a sistrum was is seen in the preceding image; and it is seen figured in our book on slaves at fol. 88. Nor was it a crotalum, as Joseph Scaliger holds, but very different. Nor was the tympanum otherwise a crotalum, as Antonio Agostini wrote in the second and fifth dialogue (unless we wish to say that this was an error of the translator). A most beautiful ancient sistrum, quite whole, was formerly to be seen in the hands of Monsignor Mocenico, Bishop of Nona. And perhaps Michele Mercati in his book on the obelisks of Rome, at chapter XI, fol. 120, where he mentions half-circles of gold and silver that are seen with hieroglyphic figures, understood nothing other than sistra, since sistra were made both of gold and of silver, as one also reads in Apuleius in Book XI of the *Golden Ass*.

<aside>Fol. 112, at the end.</aside>
Philostratus writes in the *Imagines* that in Athens the Dragon of Pallas, which until then had its dwelling in the citadel of that city, loved the Athenians for their gold; since they used it to make an ornament for the head, with figures of golden cicadas. Isidore in Book 19 of the *Origins*, at chapter 30, means the same, although the rest, being corrupt, has *Cyclades* for *Cicadas*. Gregory of Nazianzus touches on the same usage in writing to Nicobulus.

<aside>Fol. 113, line 30.</aside>
The Egyptians in ancient times made great account of the lotus, and of its flower in particular, since in the Isiac Table expounded by me the flower is seen in many places. And in my said book I have noted something about it. And in particular that the Basilidian heretics of antiquity, so called from Basilides of Alexandria, who lived around the year of the Lord 124, very frequently had the flower of the lotus engraved on their monstrous seals, upon which Harpocrates sits. And in my above-cited book six drawings of them are seen, in the last plate of the five placed at the end.

<aside>Fol. 114.</aside>
In the cameo (as the painters say) the image of Jupiter the rain-bringer and thunderer has been figured; taken from the Antonine Column, in the sculpture of which paganism, so as not to give the honour of the Marcomannic victory to the Christians, had Jupiter represented in the manner seen in the air, pouring water, hail and lightning bolts upon the barbarians. But the truth of the history is seen in Onofrio Panvinio, in Book 2 of his *Fasti*, and in Cardinal Baronio, in vol. 2 of the *Ecclesiastical Annals*.

<aside>Fol. 123, line 38.</aside>
In ancient monuments, that is in gems and in marbles, satyrs of two kinds are seen: some with the thighs and legs of a goat; some with a wholly human form, except that they have behind a small tail and the ears of a beast. We have had both kinds portrayed here on a little tablet.

<aside>Fol. 114, line 36.</aside>
Whether they were truly satyrs or not, there is much to be said. And besides what Cartari writes of them, one should read Casaubon in his books *On Satire*, in Book 1, chapter 2, where there is a collection of observations on a similar subject. And this author narrates that not many years ago there was brought before King Henry IV of France, of glorious memory, a certain man who worked as a charcoal-burner and who, at seven years of his age, began to put out a horn on his head, and some other particulars worthy of consideration. And by all means see St Jerome in the Life of St Anthony the Abbot, and Plutarch in the Life of Sulla, cited by Cartari himself, in which I do not know how to doubt, as Casaubon seems to be hinting. The case of the French charcoal-burner was in 1595, and the author of the *Chronicum Chronicorum* notes it, in Book 2.

Pliny, describing the painting of Philoxenus of Eretria, does not say that he painted three satyrs who with cups in their hands drank deeply; but that three Sileni were eating together.

<aside>Fol. 135, line 5.</aside>
Honour was figured by antiquity as both male and female. Male on this marble and on a medal of the Emperor M. Aurelius the Philosopher. Female on the medals of Galba, Vitellius and Vespasian. Whoever made it male perhaps had regard to the conception that men generally form of it, among whom great account is made of it. Whoever made it female, to its substance, because it is the shadow of Virtue. See the Author at fol. 330.

<aside>Fol. 135, line 32.</aside>
Because many among the ancients could not decide well whether the horn of plenty was Achelous's or that of the goat Amalthea, in an ancient gem, the drawing of which we record below, the goddess Copia sits with two poppies in her hand, upon the shoulders of a goat and of a bull. Beside this there is another, with a nymph who adorns the horn with flowers and fruits, as is read in Ovid, if I am not mistaken. The principal figure, then, is taken from an ancient medal of Trajan that is mine, very well preserved; in which is clearly seen what Antonio Agostini noted in his 2nd Dialogue, namely that in the middle of the horn the point of the ploughshare is seen. And all this was done to give it to be understood that the cultivated earth is what produces abundance, or Copia, as we may say.

<aside>Fol. 137, line 14.</aside>
On Jupiter worshipped at Terracina see Stephanus Pighius, in the *Hercules Prodicius*, at fol. 433, and together with him Vincenzo Mirabella on the third medal of Syracuse.

<aside>Fol. 143, line 1.</aside>
The Author notes that the thunderbolt was never given to Minerva, neither in statue nor in painting. Which, however, is not very true. And we see on the medals of the Emperor Domitian the thunderbolt in the hand of Minerva on more than one of them: as, for example, in the *Twelve Caesars* of Enea Vico, in copper on plate 2 at no. 22, in silver on plate 2 at no. 24. And Antonio Agostini touches on it in Dialogue 5.

And Domitian perhaps had regard to the verses of Virgil:

> — Could Pallas not burn up the fleet
> of the Argives and sink the men themselves in the sea
> for the guilt and the furies of one man, Ajax son of Oileus?
> She herself, hurling from the clouds the swift fire of Jupiter,
> scattered the ships and overturned the waters with winds.
> *(Virgil)*

And of this violence of Ajax Oileus, Antonio Agostini gives a drawing taken from a rather beautiful ancient marble.

Of this Ajax a fine account is read in Philostratus in his *Heroes*, and in Book 2 of the *Imagines*; and see the painting of Polygnotus described by Pausanias in Book 10.

<aside>Fol. 145, line 14.</aside>
The Author's not having understood well the words of Quintus Curtius has caused all the figures that up to now represent in this book the idol of Jupiter Ammon to have turned out false. Quintus Curtius in Book 4 writes thus:

> That thing which is worshipped as a god in this place is not like the figures that craftsmen commonly form of the deities. Up to the navel it is like a ram, and is composed of emeralds and gems.
> *(Quintus Curtius)*

Thus it ought to have been represented. But on account of the difficulty of getting the draughtsman, and the tedium (be it permitted to confess it) suffered in so long an undertaking, which turned out harder than was at first estimated, it was thought enough to advise the reader of what had to be done, leaving the care to whoever will, of forming the image in conformity with the truth [?3]. And that Ammon and Serapis were not different from Jupiter was the thought of the person who had the ring gem engraved that we have represented in the preceding figure.

<aside>Fol. 147, line 27.</aside>
That Jupiter was wreathed with olive was noted also by Carolus Paschalius in his *Wreaths*, and he proves it with the authority of Cornutus and perhaps of Sophocles. But the same author warns that antiquity gave Jupiter three kinds of wreath: of flowers, of leaves, of metals; and in another place he cites Tertullian, who reports on the testimony of Diodorus that Jupiter was the first to use a wreath: and this after the victory over the Titans.

<aside>Fol. 154, line 36.</aside>
Iris was messenger not only of Juno but of the other gods too, as Lactantius Placidus notes in Book 2 of Statius's *Achilleid*, and the same notes in Book 1 of the *Thebaid* that Iris is the passage of the gods.

<aside>Fol. 158, line 4.</aside>

<aside>Fol. 209.</aside>
The principal image has been taken from an ancient half-relief found in a sarcophagus in my time in Rome, of which, and of other things, a short account that I then made of it will be read here below. The cameo has been taken from an intaglio in carnelian of most beautiful colour, which was at other times in the hands of Monsignor de PEIRESC, Royal Senator in the court of Parliament of Aix in Provence, a gentleman most knowledgeable in all these matters.

In the year 1608, in the month of February, while a vineyard of the Signori Leni outside the gate called St Sebastian's, near Capo di Bove, was being worked, besides many other vestiges of antiquity, such as of a temple of Minerva and Rhamnusia built, as is believed, by Herodes Atticus, a noble sophist of the time of Hadrian, besides some statues and heads of emperors, herms of illustrious men, and precious columns, there was uncovered an ancient sarcophagus, covered, of plain work but well conceived, and without any memorial of any sort. There was found in it a cloth, well preserved, of the finest wool, and a silk garment, both of that colour which the dry leaf shows, and they tended to rust-coloured, as the Latins say, with certain broad stripes in the woollen cloth which were of purple colour; and both these garments smelled strongly [?7] of an odour that savoured of aromatics.

In the sarcophagus there was water, which through damp or rain had perhaps seeped in there. Besides the said garments there was a bulla of copper that had once been gilded, on which in half-relief is seen a soft young woman, who stands in a circle of waves, clothed over the flesh in a thin shift, and who ends from the waist downward in heads [?8] of sea dogs and other animals which issue from her flanks and thighs, which she bears carved like a Siren. She raises, moreover, her right hand and arm in the act of striking, and with the left hand grips the neck of a sea horse as if she wished to strike it. There were besides in the sarcophagus two gold rings of great weight. In one, of uniform thickness, narrower than what could enter the base of a finger, was set a flat emerald, not cut, very beautiful; in the other, of more modern make, a green jasper with a boy carved on it, who, seated, gathers flowers.

And because the desire to know whose the said adornments were has made some people say strange things, I have determined to make trial, if I can, of reasoning about them with some verisimilitude; reckoning that it is not unbecoming in a person who loves these studies to go investigating those particulars which antiquity and time have sought to withdraw from our eyes and from our knowledge. Now it is to be known that this sarcophagus, or sepulchre, was upon the Appian Way, which kept the name of Appius Claudius the Censor, who had it paved across Italy as far as Brindisi.

Furthermore, the ancients were accustomed to build their sepulchres upon the roads, so that, as Varro says, they might in that way admonish passers-by that they too had to die. And it is very well known that in cities it was not lawful to bury, which was the custom not only among the Romans but among the Hebrews also, as is gathered from the sepulchre of Lazarus in St John, and from the widow's son raised in St Luke. For this purpose they chose for the most part the roads, as I have said, and Pliny the Younger proves it, who, speaking of the sepulchre of Pallas, freedman of the Emperor Claudius, says thus:

> There is the Tiburtine road; within the first milestone (I noted it recently) is the monument of Pallas, inscribed thus: “TO HIM THE SENATE, FOR HIS LOYALTY”, etc.
> *(Pliny the Younger)*

And for this reason many cemeteries of the Christians lay beside the Salarian, Ardeatine, and Ostian roads, and others. At Capo di Bove, on the testimony of Cicero in the first book of the *Tusculan Disputations*, it is a clear thing that many noble families had their sepulchres. He writes:

> Or do you, when you have gone out of the Porta Capena and see the sepulchres of the Calatini, the Scipios, the Servilii, the Metelli, think them wretched?
> *(Cicero)*

So that one must almost conclude that the present one, of which we are treating, is one of those named, and this can be proved without difficulty, so far as antiquity and the injury done by him who left the chest bare and carried off the rest allow.

> Since fates have been given to the sepulchres themselves also.
> *(Juvenal)*

And truly the diggers attest that around this sarcophagus solid buttresses of peperino have been found, which argue a notable building. But whose do we wish it to be? I for my part, if it is permitted me to guess, incline to Attilius Calatinus, who in the year 496 from the foundation of the City, as stands in the Fasti, and 254 before the Nativity of Christ, triumphed “from Sicily over the Carthaginians” (EX SICILIA DE POENEIS), a man of great valour, as is read in Florus and Cornelius Nepos. And although there is not seen on the sarcophagus what in the time of Cicero was read on the sepulchre:

> Very many nations and peoples agree with one voice that he was a foremost man.
> *(Cicero)*

we have already said that the ornaments of the sepulchre have gone, which has happened to so many others that are seen along this road left despoiled. The reasons of my conjecture are the aforesaid appearance of a large and magnificent sepulchre, the fragments of an equestrian statue seen by me, and the cloth, which I judge to have been a chlamys, very rich and abundant in material. Certain people, however, have judged that there were many garments. I believe not, since the statues show us that a man wore about him a cloth so great that the warehouses now would not hold it at all. For no other reason, I think, did Juvenal call the toga *Auloca* [?9], or, as we should say, arras hangings.

The colour of the said cloth I do not doubt was purple at the edge, since the mark of it is still seen. Nor was the purple of one colour only, as some imagine. For Vitruvius writes that there were found some drawn toward black, some red, and some violet, which differences he ascribes to the different positions of the world where the shellfish were fished. This one of ours will be of the dark, of the colour of porphyry, which has likewise got from purple the name of Porphyrite. The odour is that very one which is perceived in the corpses that come to us from Egypt, called by an Arabic name mummies, and it is of myrrh, if the sense of smell does not deceive us.

The copper bulla served as the cover of the fibula, with which the chlamys was buckled over the left shoulder, and an example of it is seen on the Capitol, in the Court of the Conservators, in a cuirassed statue of Constantine, and in the house of the same Conservators in a head with the bust of Julius Caesar; or, as they more frequently did, over the right shoulder. Isidore says:

> A chlamys is that which is put on from one side and is not sewn, but is fastened with a fibula.
> *(Isidore)*

And this fibula was either turned plain in the cover, or made of some gem, or else had some figure, as on a marble head with the bust of the Emperor Hadrian I have myself seen on the cover of the fibula the portraits of Hadrian and Sabina, husband and wife, well made in low relief. On the present one Scylla, symbol of Sicily, with which Calatinus had to do, is delicately carved. And it seems that the master (though more ancient) had before his eyes those verses of Virgil in Eclogue 6, repeated a little differently in the *Ciris*:

> Why should I speak of Scylla, daughter of Nisus, or of her whom rumour has followed,
> white, her loins girt about with barking monsters,
> to have harried the Dulichian ships, and in the deep gulf,
> ah, to have torn timid sailors with sea dogs?
> *(Virgil)*

And those others of Silius Italicus, from which it is gathered that she was borne as a device in war upon the helmet also:

> The man had a helmet of defence, on whose crown there rose
> a triple crest, streaming a mane of Suebian hair.
> Above it Scylla, hurling the weight of a broken oar,
> leaned over and spread wide the savage jaws of dogs.
> *(Silius Italicus)*

Dalechamp notes on Athenaeus that the Magi of Persia kindled fire on the mountains, and with certain little bundles of vervain in their hands sang certain verses of theirs and prophesied. And I remember reading in Maximus of Tyre that in Persia, when adding wood to the sacred fire, they were accustomed to say, “Eat, Lord Fire.” Let him not laugh who can. So it will be no wonder if Calatinus wore her as an ornament of the chlamys, or paludamentum, as we choose to call it. The rings also breathe much antiquity; and first, the one that holds the emerald, being narrow in the bore, has given some people cause to think that it might be a woman's or a child's. But they have not noticed in Pliny what he writes, that in his time they were accustomed to wear rings on all the fingers and at all the joints.

> This one alone (he speaks of the infamous finger) is now excepted; all the others are weighed down, and even the joints severally with other, smaller ones.
> *(Pliny)*

And it will be no wonder that Attilius Calatinus, much earlier, as an excellent man, used some singularity, since Duilius also, who conquered the Carthaginians at sea, a contemporary of Calatinus, as Cicero writes:

> He was frequently delighted with a torch and a flute-player, which as a private man he had taken for himself without any precedent.
> *(Cicero)*

The emerald is not cut, because, as the same Pliny writes, the masters who cut gems of this kind make use of it to refresh the sight. Therefore, he writes:

> By decree of men they are spared, being forbidden to be engraved.
> *(Pliny)*

Nor is it a wonder that the rings are of such weight, since the same Author elsewhere, speaking likewise of rings, says:

> Others now display their weight.
> *(Pliny)*

In contrast to Crispinus, who, effeminate, as Juvenal writes, found for his delights summer rings thin and light. And let this much suffice, for a man occupied with other things, on this particular. And if the narration is more conjectural than definitive, let the blame be given to long age, which “corrupts not a few things and takes away many”, so as to triumph not only over men but over their names also.

The upper cameo has been taken from a medal of the Emperor Commodus, in which Fortuna Manens is figured thus, whom we should call stable and firm. And to this thought a passage of Horace tends, in Ode 29 of Book 3:

> Fortune, glad in her savage business, and
> obstinate in playing her insolent game,
> shifts uncertain honours about,
> kind now to me, now to another. I praise her ABIDING.
> *(Horace)*

And the person who had the medal struck perhaps wished to allude to some victory in the Circus games. The lower cameo is from a medal of Trajan; but a ship's prow has been omitted there, which is seen in the middle of the rudder that Fortune holds in her hand. And this will have been struck in memory of some good outcome by sea or river, having much conformity with Annona, or Abundance. For ordinarily the Fortuna of the land had a wheel added, as is seen on the medals where Fortuna Redux is represented. And see A. Agostini in the second Dialogue.

<aside>Fol. 376.</aside>
These figures are seen very frequently in the sepulchral memorials of the Greeks. And I for my part do not believe that he who had them made put into them so much mystery as the Author goes tracking out in them.

<aside>Fol. 378.</aside>
The Nemesis with wings has been taken from an ancient carnelian of mine, the other, without wings, from a Greek medal of Aurelius Caesar, on which it is read that the figure set above is the Nemesis of the people of Tyana. It is quite true that on the medal what she draws in front of her face is not a veil but rather I know not what, which is drawn out from the garment about the neck, in the manner in which, further above, at fol. 286, it is seen in the cameo of winged Peace. On high Justice is seen, as on the medals of Hadrian.

<aside>Fol. 380.</aside>
In the upper cameo there has been represented the intaglio of an ancient gem, in which Abundance is seen joined with Justice, in a very graceful knot of figures. In the hand of Justice is this bundle of those rods that the lictors anciently carried before the magistrates, taken from an ancient sepulchre.

<aside>Fol. 383, line 15.</aside>
A thought little different from that of Apelles was had in our days by Federigo Zucchero, a skilled painter.

<aside>Fol. 392.</aside>
In the cameo Fortuna is seen stretched on a bed, who, according to the opinion of Erizzo, will be the golden Fortuna of the Chamber of the Emperors. I hold her for the secure and unchangeable Fortuna. And perhaps the golden Fortuna had another form. And Julius Capitolinus makes mention of her in the life of Antoninus Pius, at the end. See the Author at fol. 427.

<aside>Fol. 397, last line.</aside>
The god Kairos, or Cero, is gracefully described by Phaedrus, freedman of the Emperor Tiberius, in Book V of his fables, although the title says TIME; which in no way contradicts the essential nature of Occasion, since this is nothing other than opportuneness of time. And in what follows about Callistratus the sculptor, I suspect that the Author has been mistaken in some way. For Callistratus did indeed describe in words the god Cero, already made by Lysippus, but did not sculpt or form him himself.

Of the Scythians, moreover, Q. Curtius does not say that they had Fortune without feet, nor that she had among them wings about her hands; but he puts into the mouth of one of their ambassadors sent to Alexander words either similar or little dissimilar:

> Put a bridle on your felicity, for in this way you will rule it more happily. They say that Fortune is without feet, and has only hands and feathers; take note that when she holds out her hands, she does not for all that allow anyone to lay hands on her feathers, etc.
> *(Quintus Curtius)*

The ambassador wished in this way to give Alexander to understand the slipperiness of Fortune.

<aside>Fol. 412, line 18.</aside>
This design was once printed in a rather large form, in imitation of which the small one has been made that we give here. And some hold it to be a design of Doni.

<aside>Fol. 395.</aside>
This whole image has been taken from an ancient intaglio.

<aside>Fol. 397, line 35.</aside>
That the things recounted are modern inventions I hold not to be so; for besides my having an ancient carnelian in which Fortune is seen at sea, with the sail, just as our painters paint her, Signor LUIGI CORRADINO, a man of exquisite understanding about these things, has an ancient intaglio in carnelian by an excellent master, of which this is the figure.

Here are seen the waves and the sail, and moreover a swan that carries Fortune as if on its wings; a bird of very prosperous augury, so that it is said to Aeneas in Virgil at the beginning of the Aeneid:

> Behold twice six swans rejoicing in a column,
> whom the bird of Jove, gliding from the region of the aether, was harrying in the open sky:
> now in a long line they seem either to be settling on the earth
> or already to be looking down upon the earth they have taken.
> As they return, they sport with whirring wings. Etc.
> *(Virgil)*

On this passage see Servius.

<aside>Fol. 404, line 8.</aside>
The Heavenly Cupid is seen in a most beautiful painting in the gallery of Signor LUIGI CORRADINO, from the design of the late Signor PAOLO AICARDO, a most learned and most courteous gentleman.

And the cameo has been taken from an ancient intaglio.

<aside>Fol. 408.</aside>
In the cameos Eros and Anteros are seen represented: either beneath the chariot of their mother, or wrestling together. On these see Enea Vico in the *Medals of Julius Caesar*, and Giraldi in Syntagma XIII. And let it be noted, in connection with Servius on Book 4 of the Aeneid, cited by Giraldi, that I have seen in more than one ancient intaglio Cupid in the act of tormenting and punishing him who does not love in return. And perhaps they were magic charms.

<aside>Fol. 420.</aside>
In the upper cameo the Cupid citharede has been taken from an ancient sardonyx given to me by Signor MARTINO SANDELLI, a man of exquisite letters and of the finest judgement, of whom I should say more if friendship did not forbid me. The lower ones are taken, one from the medal, the other from a gem.

<aside>Fol. 421, line 30.</aside>
This jest of Arcesilaus has in our days been gracefully expressed by Theodorus Gallo, a Flemish painter.

<aside>Fol. 423, line 30.</aside>
Ausonius's thought has been represented here below; and in a cameo the portrait of an ancient gem, which was in the study of Monsignor the Patriarch of Aquileia, in which two Amorini were seen binding to the cross that Lipsius calls decussate, and that we should call St Andrew's, Venus their mother.

<aside>Fol. 432.</aside>
In the cameo there has been represented the temple of Venus, as it stood in Paphos, a city of the island of Cyprus, taken from medals or ancient intaglios. And I have a small medal of the Empress Severina, on the reverse of which Venus holds in her hand this, which by others is called an apple, not very truly.

<aside>Fol. 334.</aside>
The principal figure, and the Amorino who plays with the swan, are from the antique. And the form of the scourge in the hand of Venus is to be noted, which has more of the fan than of anything else.

<aside>Fol. 437.</aside>
The cameo in which Cupid attends Venus his mother, who bathes, is an ancient work; but it ought to have been better made. And in such an act Venus is seen in a thousand antiquities.

<aside>Fol. 439.</aside>
The principal figure ought to be round, but the painter has made it oval for his own convenience. And this drawing of a gem is set down not only by Apian but also by Gio. Mario Mattio in Book 3 of the *Opinions*, and by Ramírez on Martial. The figure, moreover, that is entitled JOCUS I have seen expressed in some other ancient intaglio.

<aside>Fol. 443.</aside>
In the cameo is seen Venus Kallipyge, as she appears on the medals.

<aside>Fol. 457.</aside>
In the cameo stands the portrait of an ancient ring found once in the ruins of Spello; it is in Foligno in the hands of Signor NATALITIO BENEDETTI, an exquisite collector of ancient elegances.

In it is seen the most fortunate throw among the ancients of the game of knucklebones. This was the one that they called VENUS. And it was with four knucklebones, when all the faces of the knucklebone came up different, as is seen in the drawn gem. That this was the throw of Venus is inferred also from Cicero in the first book of *On Divination*, and from Martial in the *Apophoreta* at Epigram 14, with the title, IVORY KNUCKLEBONES.
