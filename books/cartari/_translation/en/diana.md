# Diana

<aside>Image of Diana</aside>
<aside>Claudian</aside>

The ancients called Diana the goddess of the hunt, and said that the forests and woods were entrusted to her, because she often exercised herself there in hunting, fleeing the company of men the better to guard her virginity. And so she was depicted in the dress of a nymph, all girded up, with a bow in her hand and a quiver full of arrows at her side, as Claudian describes her, who, having described Pallas, says of her:

> Less fierce, but far more graceful and fair
> was Diana, in whom the eyes and cheeks
> seemed Phoebus's, and the radiance too; and the sex
> alone would have revealed which of the two was which.
> Her bare arms shone with a heavenly whiteness,
> and her loosened hair, scattered from her shoulders
> over her breast, ran playfully about.
> Her bow was slack and her arrows hung
> at her back, and her fine gown, well drawn in by two belts,
> with small folds
> fell to below the knees.
>
> *(Claudian)*

<aside>Companions of Diana</aside>

And they gave her as companions a few young maidens, who are likewise described by Claudian in this way:

> Their arms are bare, and their shoulders, from which
> hang quivers full of arrows;
> their hands are armed with light darts,
> and they have no ornament about them
> made with art, yet they appear no less beautiful
> while they follow the wearying chase,
> and sweat at times
> bathes their flushed cheeks,
> by which it is hard to tell
> whether they are bold and charming maidens
> or fierce youths; their hair
> is tied without order, and loose,
> two belts hold back their fine gowns,
> so that they reach only to below the knees.
>
> *(Claudian)*

And the same Claudian says that the bow of Diana is of horn, contrary to what Ovid wrote of it, who depicts it as gilded and makes the bow of the nymphs the one of horn, saying of Syrinx that she was so beautiful that she might have been taken for Diana, had it not been that Diana's bow is of gold and hers was of horn.

<aside>Sacrifices of human blood</aside>

So the fables have imagined her, because just as the Sun was worshipped under the name of Apollo, so the Moon was worshipped under the name of this goddess called Diana, as if *Deviana*; for the Moon strays (*deuia*) in Heaven from the straight path of the ecliptic, which the Sun always keeps, not otherwise than hunters often go by roundabout roads (*per deuie strade*) following the wild beasts. Of these, none was more pleasing to this goddess than the deer; as was seen when, because Agamemnon had killed a deer, she was so angry with the Greeks and did them so much harm at Aulis that it was decided to appease her with the blood of the one who had offended her, by sacrificing to her Iphigenia his daughter; and the sacrifice was ready when Diana, moved to pity for the girl, made her vanish at once, putting a hind in her place, with which the Greeks performed the appointed sacrifice and appeased the goddess.

And Iphigenia, carried to the land of Tauris, was made priestess of Diana there, where strangers were sacrificed, and above all Greeks, who happened to arrive there: after some prayers they were struck on the head with an axe, and the body was thrown into the sea from a high cliff, where the temple of the goddess stood, while the head remained there, fixed on a stake.

Now while Iphigenia was in charge of this grim sacrifice, it happened that Orestes her brother, who had gone to Colchis to purify himself of the sin of having killed his mother, arrived there and was recognised by her; and for that reason she did not wish him to be sacrificed like the others. But because the people of the country seemed unwilling to endure it, she fled away with him, carrying with her the idol of the goddess wrapped in certain bundles (*fasci*) of rods, from which the goddess was afterwards given the surname Diana Fascelina; and she went to set it up at Aricia, ten miles from Rome, continuing there in the same way the impious sacrifice of human victims. This afterwards seemed too cruel to the Romans, although only slaves were sacrificed, and therefore they let this goddess pass with her sacrifices to the Lacedaemonians, who took up the practice of such a ceremony in this manner.

<aside>Custom of the Lacedaemonians of flogging the young men</aside>

They chose by lot some of the young men of the city and, having placed them on the altar of the goddess, they beat them in such a way that the poor wretches shed their blood freely from their tender, delicate limbs; and not only did they not complain of this, but we read that they often competed with one another as to which of them could endure the cruel blows more manfully.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

Meanwhile the priestess went round the altar with the idol of the goddess in her arms, and Pausanias writes that if the man whose office it was to beat the young men happened to have shown more respect to one than to another, whether because he was handsomer or of nobler birth, the idol of the goddess, which was quite small and light, became so heavy and weighty that the priestess could hardly hold it up; and so, when this happened, she cried out that through the fault of the one who beat them she felt herself oppressed by the great weight of the idol, which still had to keep about it those rods in which it had been carried away.

Although so cruel a sacrifice seems ill suited to a virgin and gentle goddess such as Diana was, nevertheless some of the ancients believed that she took delight in seeing human blood shed on her altars, as was done, according to what we read in the same Pausanias, also at Patras, a city of Achaea, where every year they sacrificed to her a young man and a young girl, the most beautiful in the city, to appease her anger, which she had conceived at the little reverence shown her by one of her priestesses, who had lovingly stayed several times with a young man in love with her in the very temple of the goddess, whereupon a little later both of them died wretchedly, and a famine and a very great plague followed in the city, which was remedied by the cruel sacrifice that I have mentioned.

But perhaps the fault for such wicked sacrifices lay with the nations, who liked to exercise their cruelty in that way, as can be seen from what was done to many other gods, to whom human victims were likewise offered; for Diana showed well enough that these were not pleasing to her, when she put the hind in place of Iphigenia. Hence some hold that the custom of sacrificing a hind to Diana was introduced from this, and it was observed afterwards by the Romans too at certain times; and for this reason the horns of deer were hung up in all the temples of Diana, except one, which was on the Aventine hill, where in their place they hung up the horns of oxen.

<aside>See Livy</aside>

And we read that the reason for this was as follows: among the Sabines there was born once a very beautiful ox, or cow, whichever it was, to a man named Antronius, and the soothsayers said that whoever first sacrificed it to Diana on the Aventine hill would win for his country the empire of Italy. Antronius, delighted at this, went to Rome with the ox to make the great sacrifice, but the priest of Diana, secretly warned by a servant of that man, made Antronius go and wash in the Tiber, saying that otherwise he could not perform a sacrifice that would be pleasing to the goddess; and so in the meantime the priest sacrificed the ox himself and fastened its horns to the doors of the temple. Thus, because he was a Roman, the empire of Italy was won for Rome; and afterwards the custom was introduced of putting up the horns of oxen in this one temple of Diana, which was, as I said, on the Aventine.

And it could perhaps also be that this was done because this animal suits Diana very well, since by her we understand the Moon, as I shall say afterwards.

> Image of Diana of the hunt and of the woods, lover of the deer sacred to her, which signify her swift course in 29 days, and that she is the illuminator of the night, being taken for the Moon, and the guide of travellers in the night.

<aside>Idol of Diana</aside>

Now I return to speak of the deer, who were believed to be so pleasing to Diana that the ancients sometimes clothed her idols in their skins, as we read in Pausanias: in Arcadia there was one clothed in the skin of a deer, from whose shoulders hung a quiver full of arrows, and which had in one hand a lighted torch and in the other two serpents, and at its side stood a hunting dog.

<aside>Chariot of Diana</aside>

And in a certain part of Achaea, as the same Pausanias reports (where they made a most solemn sacrifice to Diana, whose idol was of gold and ivory in the form of a huntress), on the day before the sacrifice there went about, as we would say, a great procession with most beautiful pomp, and behind everyone was the virgin priestess of the goddess on a fine chariot drawn by two deer. And the poets likewise give Diana a chariot drawn by snow-white deer, as Claudian does, when he says:

> The goddess who has charge of the hunt descends
> from the high mountains, and with her swift chariot
> drawn by white deer she crosses the sea.
>
> *(Claudian)*

<aside>Horses of the Moon</aside>

And it is said that they set Diana on a chariot drawn by very swift animals to show her swiftness, since the Moon makes her circuit in a very short time, which is twenty-nine days and about twelve hours, as the one that has a smaller orb than the others. And to the other gods likewise chariots were given as a sign of the turning that the heavenly spheres make, over which they are set; and according to their qualities so are the animals that draw them.

<aside>Propertius</aside>

And for this reason Propertius has the chariot of the Moon drawn by horses, when he says:

> Although lazy sleep did not weigh down
> the drooping eyes, and with her horses
> the Moon were reddening in mid-Heaven.
>
> *(Propertius)*

<aside>Boccaccio</aside>

Of these one was black and the other white, says Boccaccio, because the Moon appears not only by night but is also seen by day.

<aside>Mule at the chariot of the Moon</aside>

Pompeius Festus writes that a mule drew the chariot of the Moon, and that the reason for this was that she is barren in herself, being cold by her nature, and the mule likewise does not generate. Or else the ancients wished to show by this animal that the Moon has no light of her own but shines with another's light, as if the Sun lent it to her; just as the mule is not born of animals of its own kind but of others, which are asses and mares.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

Pausanias, where he relates the great things that were in the temple of Olympian Jupiter among the Eleans in Greece, says that there was a Diana there who seemed to him to be driving a horse; although he adds afterwards that some have said that she is drawn not by horses but by mules, because of a certain idle fable that is told about the mule; and he says nothing more of it.

<aside>Prudentius</aside>

Prudentius, against Symmachus, writes that the ancient Romans sacrificed a barren cow to the Moon, and that two cows, which had likewise to be barren, drew her chariot.

<aside>Young bulls at the chariot of the Moon</aside>

Besides this, there have been some who have put young bulls at the chariot of the Moon, as Claudian did, when he imagined that Ceres, to search for her lost daughter, lit the felled pines on Mount Etna, saying:

> So that they may keep within them greater power
> of that liquor with which Phoebus is wont to sprinkle his horses,
> and the two-horned Moon her young bulls,
> and bathe them, as much as they need.
>
> *(Claudian)*

<aside>Ausonius the Gaul</aside>

And Ausonius the Gaul did the same, when writing to Paulinus he said:

> Already the Moon was showing her fair young bulls.
>
> *(Ausonius)*

Of these we read the same reason that I gave for the mules, that is, that they show barrenness. For, as Xenophon writes, and as indeed is seen done every day, bulls are castrated to make them gentler and more fit for cultivating the ground, whence it is that they can no longer generate. Or else this animal was given to the Moon because of the resemblance there is between them in the horns: since on her idol, which was, as I have said, in the form of a fair nymph, they put two little horns on the head.

And in Egypt the ox that was kept there in such great reverence was consecrated to the Moon, and it had to have a white mark on its right flank and small horns, such as those of the Moon when she begins to wax, as we read in Pliny. And they also sacrificed one to her six months old; some say on the seventh day, and some others on the tenth after the birth, which was when, with their ceremonies, they gave the name to newborn children.

<aside>The Moon helps childbirth</aside>

And the ancients did this then for the Moon, perhaps thanking her, as if through her the ripe birth had come to light, because they say that the Moon, being a moist planet, sometimes hastens the time with her influence, so that children are sometimes born in the seventh month, which is subject to her, and she makes the birth almost always easier. And for this reason they invoked her then and prayed to her, naming her Lucina, as if she quickly, and without danger to the mother, brought the ripe birth out into the light (*in luce*).

> Statue of Lucina, goddess of Childbirth, taken for the Moon, the Moon being a moist planet able to facilitate the speed of birth, and represented out of regard for the modesty of the woman in labour.

But the fables have said that Diana was called by women in childbirth under the name of Lucina, because when she had come out of the womb of Latona her mother, she turned round at once and, all nimble and deft, helped her to give birth to her brother Apollo, as though they were praying her to come forth with her numen and give them the help that she once gave her mother with her own hands. Nor was Diana alone understood under the name of Lucina, but Juno also, as is seen in her image.

And others have said that it was neither the one nor the other, but a certain woman, who came from the Hyperborean mountains to Delos to help Latona give birth; and that from there her name then spread in such a way that she was worshipped almost everywhere, and had temples, altars and idols, like the other gods, before whom she must have existed, since she helped them to be born.

And so it seems that a certain Lycian poet understood it, who, as Pausanias reports, in certain hymns that he made to this goddess said that she had existed even before Saturn, and gave her certain names by which one could also easily believe that she was one of the Parcae; for these too had a good deal to do with human birth, as we shall see when we speak of them.

<aside>Idols of Lucina</aside>

But leaving it to others to inquire who this goddess Lucina was, or whence she came, let us speak of her idols, which were always kept entirely covered by the Athenians, though only by them, as Pausanias writes. So among them the statue of Lucina could be a piece of wood or of some other material without any figure at all, as well as being formed as a woman or as something else, since it always stood covered and was never seen.

In a certain part of Achaea there was a very ancient temple of this goddess, with an idol made entirely of wood except the face, which was such that it could represent Diana; the hands and feet were of marble, and a fine linen veil covered the whole of it, except for those parts that were of marble, which remained uncovered.

<aside>Festus</aside>

One of the hands was stretched out, with nothing in it, and a key could well have been put in it, because Festus writes that the ancients used to give one to women, showing by this (it being an instrument for opening) that they wished them an easy and pleasant delivery, since if the way is well opened for the baby when it is about to be born, it comes out without giving torment to the mother. But perhaps they wished to show the same by that stretched-out and open hand of Lucina.

<aside>Torch in the hand of Diana</aside>

The other carried a burning torch, which showed either that women in childbirth feel very grievous pains, which constrict them, just as fire constricts everything it takes hold of; or that this goddess was the bringer of light to the children being born, because she gave them aid to come out of the mother's womb. For which reason the Greeks put wreaths of dittany on her head, an herb that, placed under women when they are about to bear, helps them greatly.

> Image of Diana Cynthia, or the Moon, goddess huntress, with a leopard in her right hand and a lion in her left, thus sculpted in Corinth in the temple of Juno on the Chest of Cypselus the tyrant.

<aside>Diana</aside>

We read also that the ancients, when depicting Diana with a bow in her hand, wished to show the sharp stings of the pains that women have in childbirth, and they depicted her so almost always.

<aside>Marcus Tullius</aside>

So Marcus Tullius, writing against Verres, describes an idol of Diana carried off by him from Sicily, in this fashion: it was tall and large, with a garment that covered it entirely down to the feet, young of face and of virginal aspect, and in its right hand it carried a burning torch and held a bow in its left, and the arrows hung from its shoulders.

The lighted torch in Diana's hand can also show (as Pausanias too writes, that in Arcadia there was a metal idol of her perhaps six feet tall, besides the one I have mentioned) that, shining by night, she acts as escort to travellers; and for this reason she was called there Diana the escort and leader, just as in Rome, in the temple that she had on the Palatine hill, she was called Noctiluca. And she had various other names too, which will be spoken of afterwards.

<aside>Triform Diana</aside>
<aside>Ovid</aside>

Pausanias, when he describes the Chest of Cypselus, tyrant of Corinth, placed there in the temple of Juno, says that many figures of gold and ivory were carved and engraved on it, and that among them was Diana with wings at the shoulders, who held out a leopard with her right hand and a lion with her left, and that he cannot give any reason for it; so I shall not be ashamed to say the same, not having found so far anyone who has written about it. I leave it, then, for everyone to interpret in his own way, and I go on to say that Virgil gave three faces to the virgin Diana, and that she was therefore called Triform, Trigemina and Trivia; and not Diana alone, but Hecate also was so called, whence Ovid wrote:

> See how Hecate with three faces watches over
> three ways, which then all end in one.
>
> *(Ovid)*

Although they were afterwards all one and the same thing, and only the names were different, these names were meant to show, as I have already said so many times, the different powers and different qualities that the ancients gave to their gods, and the various effects that were believed to come from them. And for this reason the fables said that Hecate, born of Jupiter, had from him authority and power over all the elements, and that she was so named because among the Greeks a similar word means a hundred, which among them is often taken for an infinite number, as if she were of infinite might; for it seems that by her, who is, as I have said, the Moon, the elements are governed, and almost all the things composed of them, and that these change according as she changes.

<aside>Hecatomb</aside>
<aside>Feigned victims</aside>

Or she was so called because, as some say, they sacrificed to her with a hundred altars of green shrubs and killed a hundred victims for her, such as pigs or sheep; but if the sacrifice, which was therefore called a hecatomb, was made in the name of the emperor, the victims were a hundred lions, or a hundred eagles. I do not believe, however, that they always had these animals real, but rather that they sometimes feigned them; for the ancients often used in their sacrifices to make, of dough or of some other material, the animal that was to be sacrificed, when it could not be found except with the greatest difficulty. And the poor, who could not afford the expense of real animals, as Suda reports, often did this, sacrificing simulated and feigned ones, as is seen in Herodotus also, who says that the Egyptians did not sacrifice the pig to any god other than the Moon and Bacchus, and even then only at those festivals that they held at the time of the full Moon, taking care at all other times not to touch this beast, of which they ate on that day only, when it was sacrificed, and never again in all the rest of the year; and those who through poverty could not sacrifice a real pig made one in imitation, and sacrificed that.

<aside>Appian</aside>

And Appian writes that the Cyzicenes, a people of Greece, whose city it was that Jupiter gave as a dowry to Proserpina, and who therefore worshipped her above all other numina, sacrificing to her an entirely black cow, being besieged by the fleet of Mithridates and unable to find the cow that was necessary for the solemn sacrifice of their goddess, made one of dough in order to sacrifice it; but while they were preparing the sacrifice, one came out of the middle of the sea, entirely black, as it had to be, which, swimming under the ships of Mithridates, passed into the city and, going to place itself before the altar of the goddess, was sacrificed by that people, who took good hope from this of being freed from the siege, as they were, because not long after Mithridates, through many mishaps that befell him, was forced to go away.

<aside>Dido</aside>

Dido in Virgil, in the last sacrifice that she makes at the departure of Aeneas, sprinkles the simulated waters of Avernus; and here Servius notes that in sacrifices the ancients often feigned the things that they could not have, or could have only with great difficulty. And in another place he says also that for this reason they nevertheless said that the water they sprinkled in the temple of Isis was from the Nile, even if it was not.

<aside>Kissing the hand</aside>

And not only did feigned victims excuse those who could not sacrifice real ones, but going humbly to kiss the hand of the god to whom one had to sacrifice was often in place of sacrifice for one who could do nothing else. The ancients were also accustomed to kiss consecrated idols out of devotion, as we gather from Cicero, when he speaks against Verres, where he says that in Agrigento, a city of Sicily, there was a very beautiful metal idol of Hercules, which had the mouth and the chin almost worn away, so often was it kissed by those who went to worship it.

> Image of Hecate, three-faced goddess, also called Proserpina, wife of Pluto, queen of the underworld, signifying the three aspects of the Moon and the lunar power over elemental things.

<aside>Prudentius</aside>

And Prudentius, writing of how the Sun, believed to be Apollo, was worshipped, adds at the end that they also kissed the feet of the horses that drew his chariot. But returning to Hecate: she was worshipped at the crossroads of the ways, and there they sacrificed the dog to her, praying to her with disordered words and with shouts, in imitation of what Ceres once did when she went searching for her daughter Proserpina, who was the same as Hecate; to whom the rich among the ancients used to sacrifice every month at the crossroads of the ways, leaving there bread and other things necessary for living, which were afterwards carried off by the poor, and this was called the supper of Hecate, as Suda reports, who says also that she sometimes showed herself in a horrible and frightening form, which was that of a very large man with the head of a serpent.

<aside>Triform Hecate</aside>

She was called, and depicted, triform in order to guard better those roads that were consecrated to her, which by joining together made a crossroads, as some have said; but others have held, and perhaps better, that the giving of three faces to her was a fiction of Orpheus, who wished in this way to show the varied aspects in which the Moon shows herself; and that her power has force not only in Heaven, where they call her Moon, but on earth too, where they call her Diana, and even down in the underworld, where they call her Hecate and Proserpina, because she is believed to descend to the underworld for all the time that she is hidden from us.

These things are set out by Eusebius as follows. The Moon is called Hecate and Triform for the various shapes that she shows in her body, according as she is found to be more or less distant from the Sun, and so her powers too are threefold. The first is when she begins to show her light to mortals, bringing increase to things by it; and this first and new aspect was shown by the ancients with white and gilded garments, which they put around her idol, and with the lighted torch that the same idol held in its hand. The second is when she has already half of all the light, and this was shown with the basket in which they carried her sacred things: for, while the light of the Moon goes on growing, every day the fruits ripen more, which are then gathered in baskets. The third is in the full light, shown with garments of a dusky colour.

<aside>Laurel consecrated to the Moon</aside>

To her they gave the laurel also, which properly belongs to Apollo, because she receives her light from the Sun, and that fiery colour that she sometimes shows in her face.

<aside>Poppy consecrated to the Moon</aside>

And they gave her the poppy likewise, for the multitude of souls that were believed to inhabit her orb, as if that were a great city all full of numerous people, since the poppy shows and signifies cities, because it has its heads so notched at the top as are the walls of cities, and holds gathered within itself a great number of tiny grains, as a great number of people stay together united in cities. And it was the opinion of some philosophers that it was inhabited up there on the orb of the Moon just as the earth is down here, and they said that the cities, forests and mountains that are there make those spots that we seem to see on her face; but Pliny holds that they are made by the moisture that she draws from the earth.

> Idol of the Moon, signifying that the Moon receives her light from the Sun and has no light in herself, but rather is a dark and darkened body made resplendent by the Sun, signified by the head of a hawk.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

Pausanias writes that in Aegina, a city of the Corinthians, Hecate was worshipped more than all the other gods; and that there she had a wooden idol made by Myron with one face only, and the rest of the body was like a trunk, as though she were not always made with three faces; but it is believed that Alcamenes before all the others made her so for the Athenians.

Of the three heads, then, that the idol of Hecate had, the one on the right was of a horse, the other of a dog, and the third, which was in the middle, of a rustic and coarse man, as some say, or, as others will have it, of a boar, which perhaps suits better what is said of the Moon, since, considered when she spreads her light over us, she is called Diana, and huntress, which can be understood by means of the boar, because this beast is always in the forests and woods; just as the head of the horse, a swift animal, shows us that she goes round Heaven very swiftly; and that of the dog denotes that the same one, when she hides from us, was believed to be the goddess of the underworld, and was called Proserpina, because the dog is given to the god of the underworld, as Cerberus, so celebrated by the fables, bears witness.

<aside>Prudentius</aside>

And Prudentius, writing of the vanity of the Gentiles defended by Symmachus, says of the Moon in this way:

> Now on a fair chariot drawn by two cows
> she goes white through Heaven; now in the underworld
> the wicked sisters with a viper's whip
> she punishes, and makes them go out against mortals;
> now through the woods the swift does
> she wounds and pierces with sharp darts.
> And hence it comes that in three different forms
> under three different names she shows herself:
> for she is called Moon when she appears
> to us dressed in a fair shining veil,
> when girded she unfolds the arrows,
> she is the virgin daughter of Latona;
> and when seated on a high throne she gives laws
> to Megaera, and as their queen
> cries out and commands the lost souls,
> she is Proserpina, wife of Pluto.
>
> *(Prudentius)*

He then goes on to say that the truth is that this is a wicked demon, who deceives mortals, persuading them that in three different places there are many different gods: in Heaven, on earth and in the underworld.

<aside>Theodoret</aside>

Porphyry, as Theodoret, bishop of Cyrrhus, reports, writing about the wicked demons what will be told of them in the image of Pluto, states that Hecate is mistress of them, and that she keeps them in three elements: in the air, in the water and on the earth.

<aside>Aristophanes</aside>

Besides this, the ancients also said that Hecate often showed to one who found himself in some great calamity and in some great misery a certain shadow or phantom, which kept changing, almost at once, from one shape to another, as Aristophanes says, and Suda reports it; and it showed itself now as an ox, now as a mule, sometimes it seemed to be a most beautiful woman, and at other times a dog; and this kind of thing was called Empusa because it seemed to go on one foot only, and some have held that it was Hecate herself, who showed herself in this fashion at high noon, when with certain ceremonies the shades of the dead were appeased.

And because of the various and different aspects that this beast showed of itself to others, it became a proverb among the ancients, and of one who made out that he wanted now one thing and presently another, and never let it be known what he was, they said that he changed more than Empusa did. And Lucian, speaking of dances, said that they make the person change in so many ways that one may say the person represents Empusa, who changes into a thousand forms.

There was also, as Eusebius writes, in Apollinopolis, a city of Egypt, a statue of her, which likewise showed that the Moon has no light of herself but receives it from the Sun, because it was made in the form of a man all white, who had the head of a hawk. The whiteness signifies that the Moon of herself has no light, but receives it from another, that is, from the Sun, who gives her spirit also and strength; and this is signified by the head of the hawk, because this bird was consecrated to the Sun, as I said in his image.

<aside>Isis</aside>

We read also that in Egypt they depicted Isis dressed in black, to show that she is of herself a dusky and dark body; and this too was the Moon, as was known from her statue made in the form of a woman with two little horns of an ox on her head, as Herodotus writes, so that the Egyptians could not sacrifice cows, as if they were all of this deity, although they sacrificed oxen and calves.

Or perhaps it was also because the fables say that she was changed once into this beast by Jupiter, after he had enjoyed her, so that Juno should not notice it, and that she was then named Io, and so the Greeks call her, and they likewise depict her with horns on her head; but having passed afterwards into Egypt she was called Isis there, and her idol held a certain cymbal in the right hand, and in the left it had a vessel. Whence, as Servius says, some believed that she was the Genius of Egypt, as if through her the nature of that country were seen, the cymbal showing the noise that the Nile makes when it rises, so that it floods all the fields, and the vessel the lakes that are there.

<aside>Servius and Macrobius</aside>
<aside>Nature</aside>

Others have said that she is the earth, as the same Servius reports, and Macrobius also, or else the Nature of things, which is subject to the Sun; and hence it comes that they made the body of this goddess all full and laden with breasts, as if the universe takes nourishment from the earth, or from the hidden power of Nature, because Nature too was represented by the ancients with this image.

> Image of the goddess Nature, all full of breasts, to show that the universe takes nourishment from her hidden power.

<aside>Medal of Hadrian</aside>

And I understand that such an idol was once found in Rome at the time of Pope Leo X, and this same figure with so many breasts is seen on an ancient medal of Hadrian.

<aside>Vulture of Nature</aside>
<aside>Aelian</aside>

In Egypt, when they wished to draw Nature in their sacred figures, they depicted the vulture, and the reason for this, says Ammianus Marcellinus, was that among vultures none is found that is male, but all are females, as Aelian also writes; and it was believed that the Eurus, the east wind, served these birds instead of males, as the Zephyrus seems to impregnate the earth and the trees in spring.

<aside>Lactantius</aside>

There have been some, then, who put on the head of the idol of Isis a wreath of abrotano, and gave her in the left hand the same herb, and in the right a little ship, by which they perhaps wished to show that she passed into Egypt, since there was celebrated there, as Lactantius writes, a festival dedicated to the Ship of Isis; for although the fables feigned that she, changed into a cow, crossed the sea swimming, nevertheless history has written that she crossed it by sailing, and for this the Egyptians believed her to be over navigation, and able to give with her numen a happy course to sailors.

<aside>Lucian</aside>

Hence in Lucian, Jupiter commands Mercury to go and lead Io by sea into Egypt, and there to have her called Isis afterwards, and have her worshipped as a numen that should have power to make the Nile overflow, to make the winds blow, and to preserve sailors.

<aside>Apuleius</aside>

And Apuleius has Isis herself speak thus of her festival: “My religion will begin tomorrow, to last then for ever, and the storms of winter being already calmed, and the sea having become, from troubled and stormy, quiet and navigable, my priests will sacrifice to me a little ship in demonstration of my passage.”

<aside>Valerius Flaccus</aside>

To this thing perhaps some peoples of Germany also had regard, who, as Alexander ab Alexandro reports, worshipped a liburna, which is a certain sort of small and swift ship, and we might perhaps say that it was like what brigantines or frigates are today, believing that this was the true image of Isis, whose idol, says Aelian, had in Egypt its head girt and crowned with a serpent, and the same is read in Valerius Flaccus, who likewise gives her the cymbal in her hand.

<aside>Ovid</aside>

Ovid, when he makes her appear in a dream to Telethusa, depicts her thus, putting with her certain others also of the gods of Egypt:

> To Telethusa at midnight appeared
> the daughter of Inachus, accompanied
> by fair mysteries with unfeigned phantoms;
> her brow was marked with two horns,
> which with white and ripe ears of grain
> was adorned with wondrous charm.
> Anubis, who with voices friendly to the good
> barks like a dog, and bears the sceptre
> that friendly peoples placed in his hand.
> Holy Bubastis, and Apis, and he who urges
> people to silence was with her,
> guiding with his hand to fair silence.
> And he whom they go with sorrowful cries
> always seeking, Osiris, who was set
> afterwards by his wife among the eternal gods.
> And at her side were the serpents and the sistra.
>
> *(Ovid)*

> Image of Isis, Egyptian goddess, who is the Moon, held to be the goddess of sailors, and was Io among the Greeks, who, transformed into a cow by Jupiter after she had been violated, and having returned to her own form, fled by sea to Egypt, and there was worshipped by those peoples for benefits received.

<aside>Apuleius</aside>

Apuleius likewise feigns that he saw her in a dream when he was an ass, and describes her in such a way that it can very well be seen that she was the Moon, whom the people of Egypt worshipped with shadowed mysteries.

<aside>Martianus</aside>

Hence Martianus Capella describes Philologia entering the orb of the Moon and seeing there the cymbals, which I have already named so many times, the torches of Ceres, the bow of Diana, the timbrels of Cybele, and that triform figure of which I have already said that it had also horns on its head, and a hind; as if all these things together, and each by itself, signified the Moon.

But returning to Apuleius, he says that as he slept it seemed to him that he saw this goddess, who with a venerable face was coming out of the sea (because the poets feigned that the Sun, the Moon and all the other stars, when setting, went to plunge into the sea, and that they came out from there at their first appearing), and little by little then showed her whole shining body. She had her head adorned with long and thick hair, lightly curled, which spread over her neck, girt with a beautiful wreath of various flowers, and in the middle of her forehead she carried a certain round thing, flattened and smooth, which shone like a mirror, and on one side and on the other were some serpents, above which were a few ears of grain. Her gown of various colours was of the finest gauze, and now white, now yellow and golden, now it seemed to be inflamed and red. And she had another too, all black, but clear and shining, and covered almost entirely with glittering stars, in the middle of which was a Moon all resplendent, and around the hem there were fixed in fair order flowers and fruits of every sort.

> Image of Isis, goddess of the Egyptians, who is the Moon, with implements in her hands denoting the nature of the Nile and of Egypt, the lunar eclipses, and other effects of hers both in the waters and in elemental things.

Then the goddess carried in her right hand a certain thing of copper made in the manner of a cymbal, which when she shook her arm made a very great sound, and from the left there hung a golden vessel, for which a serpent served as handle, that seemed all swollen with venom, and at her feet she had a certain ornament made of palm leaves.

<aside>Virgil</aside>

This is Apuleius's portrait of Isis, to whom for a certain natural reason he gives a gown of white, yellow and red, because the Moon often changes colour, from which many divine the quality of the weather that is to follow, for redness in her signifies that there will be winds, a dusky colour rain, and the bright and clear shows that the air will be serene; as Virgil also sang, saying:

> When the Moon begins to regain
> her light once lost, if with dusky
> horns she comes embracing the black air,
> farmers and sailors will have
> great rains; but if with a modest blush
> she spreads her fair cheeks, there will be wind;
> for wind is always shown when the Moon
> turns red; and if at the fourth rising (for this
> never fails) she goes fair and serene
> through Heaven with her bright horns,
> that day and the others that will come after
> through the whole month will be dry and calm.
>
> *(Virgil)*

The other gown, all black, shows that the Moon, as I have already said several times, has no light of herself but receives it from another.

Some have said then that Apuleius puts that cymbal in the hand of this goddess to show the custom of the ancients, who, going out into the open, made a certain din and noise with vessels of copper and iron, thinking to help the Moon in that way when she loses her light because the earth comes between her and the Sun, which is at the time of the eclipse; not knowing the cause of it, they said that the Moon was drawn down to earth by the force of spells, because then certain enchanters had given the world to understand that they could do this, and more besides.

Thus Virgil said, in the person of a certain sorceress, that enchanted verses have the power to draw the Moon down from Heaven; and of Medea we often read that she made the Moon descend in spite of herself; and Lucan, speaking of the enchanters of Thessaly, says that they were the first to do violence to the stars, and that they made the Moon black and dark at the time when she ought to be clearest and brightest, and kept her so until she had come down to earth to do what they wanted.

<aside>Porphyry</aside>

And in Apuleius one of these enchantresses boasts that she can do any great harm to the gods and can darken at her pleasure the light of the stars, because the force of those diabolical spells was effective not only against the Moon but against the Sun also and all the stars, and against all the other gods, both of Heaven and of the underworld; whom, besides all the other accursed ceremonies, they used to threaten (as Porphyry writes to a certain great priest of Egypt, and Theodoret reports it) to break and shatter Heaven (perhaps so that they should all fall down), to reveal the hidden mysteries of Isis and to publish all her most secret affairs, to make Charon's boat carry no more souls across, to give the limbs of Osiris to Typhon to tear apart and scatter everywhere, and other such follies, always putting forward what they thought would displease most the god on whom they wished to use force, so that he should come to obey them.

And perhaps similar to this is what is read in Ovid, that Faunus and Picus, numina, or demons, inhabitants of the Aventine hill, drew Jupiter down from Heaven by magic art and by force of spells, to come and answer them; although the Romans afterwards condemned this diabolical art and would not have it in any way, as is seen from Apuleius, who was accused of it; and the masters of it were reputed to be those of Thessaly, because, as Suda reports, Medea, passing over it, poured out the basket of her poisons and her charms.

<aside>Seneca</aside>

And for this reason, when the poets make some prayer to the Moon under whatever name, whether of Diana, or of Hecate, or of another, to make her more ready to hear them, they wish for her that she may have her light pure and clear, and that the spells of Thessaly may never be able to draw her from Heaven, as the nurse of Phaedra does in the tragedy of Hippolytus in Seneca, saying:

> O queen of the woods, dweller
> on the high mountains, where you are worshipped,
> O great goddess of the forests, O bright light
> of Heaven, O of the dark damp night
> true ornament, whose torch gives
> alternate light to the world, O triform goddess,
> holy Hecate, grant your favour
> to the work begun.
>
> *(Seneca)*

And a little later he adds:

> So bright and pure may always appear
> your face, and may no cloud
> ever hide from us your fair horns,
> so may the enchanted verses
> of Thessaly have no force in themselves, while
> you hold the reins of the nocturnal light;
> and may there never again be a shepherd who
> can have any glory from your love, and go off proud.
>
> *(Seneca)*

<aside>Endymion</aside>

She says this because the fables feigned that the Moon fell in love with the shepherd Endymion and put him to sleep on a certain mountain, only to kiss him at her pleasure. But as Pausanias reports, there was something else between them than kisses, for some say that he had fifty daughters by her.

We read also that the Moon gave herself to Endymion, or to Pan, god of Arcadia, as Virgil sings, not for love alone but to have from him a flock of fine white sheep.

And all these are fables, but they have nevertheless some sense of truth, because Pliny writes that Endymion was the first to understand the nature of the Moon, and that therefore it was feigned that they were in love with each other.

<aside>Alexander of Aphrodisias</aside>

And Alexander of Aphrodisias says in his *Problems* that Endymion was a man very studious of the things of Heaven, and that he sought with great diligence to understand the course of the Moon and the causes of the different aspects that she shows; and because he slept by day and watched by night, it was said that the Moon took pleasure in him.

And so it might be said of the people of Thessaly too, that because they wished to investigate the course and nature of the Moon, it would have been feigned afterwards of them that they drew her from Heaven to earth, at the time when the common people believed that she suffered much and endured the gravest toil, and that that sound, represented by the cymbal placed in the hand of Isis, much eased the pain of the violence done to her, as poets often sing, and Pliny writes also, as if that noise did not let the murmur of the spells reach the ears of the Moon, and therefore they then had no force against her.

Whence Propertius says that spells would draw the Moon down from her chariot, if the resounding metals did not remedy it.

<aside>Juvenal</aside>

And Juvenal, speaking of a certain extremely talkative woman, says that there is no longer any need to make a noise with copper vessels or other metals, because she alone with her chattering makes so much din that she can defend the Moon from spells.

It is written of some peoples who worshipped the Sun and the Moon, believing that they were husband and wife, that they fasted at eclipses, women especially; and the married women dishevelled their hair and scratched themselves, and the maidens bled themselves with fish bones, drawing blood, thinking that the Moon at that time was wounded by the Sun for some displeasure that she had caused him.

<aside>Sistrum</aside>

Others have held that the cymbal, called by the ancients the Sistrum, in the hand of Isis shows the sound that the Moon makes in the turning of the celestial orbs.

<aside>Celio Calcagnini</aside>

They made it not only of copper but also of silver and of gold, as Apuleius says, when he speaks of the mysteries of Isis, and (as Celio Calcagnini reports) it had four faces, which moved along the upper circuit, which signified that the part of the world that is generated and corrupted is beneath the globe of the Moon, where things change according to the movement of the elements, shown by the four faces.

Inside, also in the upper part, they engraved a cat with the face of a man, and there were two other heads, which moved under the four that I mentioned: one was Isis's, the other Nephthys's, and these signified the birth and the death of things, which come from the changes of the elements.

The cat signified the Moon; so the fables, feigning, as Ovid relates, that the gods fled from the fury of Typhon as far as Egypt, and did not hold themselves safe there unless they changed into various animals, said that Diana changed into a cat, because it is a very changeable animal, which sees at night, and whose eyes change, the light in them increasing or diminishing according as the light of the Moon wanes or waxes; and they made it with a human face, to show that the movements of the Moon are not without a superior intelligence.

These were the mysteries contained in the Sistrum, so celebrated in the ceremonies of Isis, and often placed in the hand of her image, as I have already said that Apuleius put it in her right hand.

And of the vessel that hung from her left hand, besides what I have already said of it, we read also that it can signify the movement of the waters swollen by the moist nature of the Moon. Whence some have held that her waxing and waning is the cause of the flow and ebb that the waters of the sea make.

<aside>Saint Ambrose</aside>

And so that this image of the Moon, besides the natural things that are shown in it, may teach us something else more useful to human life, let us look at what Blessed Ambrose says, who by the example of her, whose light may reasonably be called uncertain, because ever changing it now waxes and now wanes, admonishes us that among human things there is no firmness whatever, and that all with time come undone.

And for this reason some said that the ancient Romans of noble family wore certain lunulae on their feet, in order to be often admonished by them of the instability of human things, so as not to grow proud even though they were rich and abundant in many goods, because riches and other things so much esteemed by mortals do exactly as the Moon does, which is now all luminous and resplendent, now so thins her light that she shows little of it, and at last becomes so dark that she no longer seems to be there.

But let us say no more of her, but rather of that custom of the Romans of wearing the lunulae on their shoes, because some others derive it from the Arcadians, saying that these among all the peoples of Greece held themselves to be the most ancient, and therefore the most noble, because they claimed to have existed even before the Moon was born or made.

And they had been led to believe this because Arcadia lies in the middle, along the length of the Peloponnese, higher than all the other lands of Greece, and mountainous; whence it was said that at the time of the flood the Arcadians alone were saved, having withdrawn to the summits of the mountains until the waters had gone down.

So coming out then of the caves and seeing the Moon, as though the one that had been before the flood had perished together with the other things, and this were another, they believed her to have been made or born only then, and so after themselves, who had been born long before; and from this they drew the argument that they were the most ancient and the most noble of all the other Greeks, since they had existed before the Moon.

And from this the Romans took the custom of wearing the lunulae on their shoes as a sign of antiquity and nobility of the family, as though it were equal to that of the Arcadians, born before the Moon.

<aside>Athenaeus</aside>

And the Athenians likewise, wishing to show that before them there had been no other men, but that they themselves had been born of the earth, wore golden cicadas on their heads, arranged in various fashions among their hair, as Suda reports. And Athenaeus, writing of the delicacies of the Athenians, notes that out of wantonness the young men who wished to adorn themselves more delicately did this, putting little golden cicadas around their forehead.
