**Addition to Cartari's Images, by Signor Lorenzo Pignoria.**

<aside>Fol. 25, line 10.</aside>
Saturn had a sickle, as can be seen on the ancient denarii of the Memmian family, from which Ortelius took it, and on my coin of Caepio, and on the statue of Mithras that belonged to Orazio Mario Tigrino. Moreover, in Grotius's *Aratus* [?1], printed by Raphelengius in the year 1600, on page 81 one sees the head of Saturn surrounded by a nimbus, with the harpe beside it, which resembles a sickle, as Grotius notes in the same book, at the figure of Perseus.

<aside>Fol. 39, line 12.</aside>
Janus was very often depicted by the ancients with two youthful faces, and so Ortelius has represented him. So too he stands on one ancient denarius, and on a piece of money of the sort that the ancients called *aes grave*; I have it. It is a thing of the sort that the common run of antiquaries call Weights, which they are not. For in antiquity weights were indeed made of copper, but in the form of balls, flattened, however, at the bottom to make them stand, and on top to mark on them either the mark of the weight or the name of the magistrate. But for the most part they were of stone, green or black, very hard.

Sebastiano Erizzo and Luca Peto record many of them. I have several, and on one, with letters formed of dots, one reads a Marcus Fusius; it is of exactly one ounce. Two others I have with the mark of the semuncia, and others larger. In later times, then, they were made of copper in square and round form, in various ways. And the collection of them made by Smetius and by Gruterus is worth seeing. The image of the young Janus is this one, unless it is, however, the Iana of Nigidius.

<aside>Fol. 110, line 27.</aside>
Cartari took this wreath of abrotano from Giraldi, in Syntagma XII, at fol. 369, who, however, says it on the opinion of others. I do not know of any ancient author who says so, but I defer to the truth. I know well that the female abrotano was a plant of the priests of Isis, and I say so in my Commentary on the Hieroglyphic Tablet of His Most Serene Highness of Mantua. And perhaps from here the error was born, if there is an error here at all.

<aside>Fol. 188.</aside>
Whoever wishes to see something concerning panic terror should read our notes on Emblem CXXII of Alciato, and the *Rhesus* of Euripides. In our own days a night in the year 1606 in the Paduan territory was notable for a terror more than Panic.

<aside>Fol. 158.</aside>
Signor Ottavio Rossi has recorded many fine particulars about the priest whom the Athenians called Bouphonos, in his *Brescian Memoirs*, at fol. 187. And of this ceremony, which Cartari touches on here, Porphyry speaks at length in Book II of *On Abstinence from Eating Meat*, at fol. 40 of the Latin version.

<aside>Fol. 325, line 32.</aside>
In Lycophron I have not managed to find what the author says here. It is found, however, in Alessandro Sardi, who wrote *On the Customs and Rites of the Nations*, in Book 3, at chapter 6, and in Caelius Rhodiginus, in Book 8, at chapter 2. And of these Fire-bearers (Πυρφόροι), as they were anciently called, Herodotus makes mention in Book 8, at fol. 452 of the Wechelian edition, and the Suda, and Julius Pollux; from these authors it is gathered that they were inviolable, like trumpeters and drummers nowadays.

<aside>Fol. 406, line 2.</aside>
The passage of Pausanias cited by the author is in Book 4, where he relates that Pindar called her Pherepolis, that is, guardian of the cities. Perhaps for this reason, on a small metal statue a span in size, in the possession of Signor Pompeo Pasqualino in Rome, towers are seen on the head of Fortune.

<aside>Fol. 412.</aside>
It is a great wonder that Cartari made no mention at all of the goddess Themis, of whom Pausanias nevertheless made mention in several places. According to what Eusebius writes of her in Book 2 of the *Preparation for the Gospel*, at chapter 4, she was the wife of Jupiter and the mother of Minerva. Diodorus Siculus also speaks of her in Book 5, at chapter 15. Arnobius relates in Book 5 that at her command Deucalion and Pyrrha took stones from Mount Agdus in Phrygia, to restore the human race. And so it is seen that she was a prophetic goddess, as Apollodorus relates in the 1st book of his *Library*, and Diodorus Siculus, and Ovid in the 1st book of the *Metamorphoses*.

Now if Themis (as Cornutus writes) was she of whom he speaks thus:

> under whose protection we conclude contracts, make agreements, and keep the pacts in good faith
> *(Cornutus)*

Then she will be Equity, whom the first emperors on their coins depicted standing, with scales in her right hand and a spear in her left. Those who succeeded them afterwards gave her the cornucopia in place of the spear, wishing to imply that abundance was more fitting [?2] to Equity than the spear, often a symbol of war.

But if Themis were Justice, as Budaeus holds in the *Annotations on the Pandects*, she was depicted seated, with a patera in her hand, resting on a spear. And so she is on the medals of Hadrian, of Antoninus Pius, of Alexander Severus. Stephanus Pighius has printed an elegant discourse on the goddess Themis.

<aside>Fol. 488, line 27.</aside>
This authority of Macrobius reported by Cartari has made me believe that the fragment placed below belongs to a statue of Proserpina, or Libera, if we would rather call her that;

I had the drawing of it from the late Signor Paolo Gualdo, who was the owner of the statue and was archpriest of our cathedral, my most loving patron “while the fates allowed.” And on the figured band that goes around her chest and over her shoulders a most elegant sonnet has been written by Monsignor Antonio Querenghi, a great splendour of our city. And the same band has been explained in Latin by my Signor Girolamo Aleandro in his *Heliaca*, with a singular apparatus of varied and copious erudition, being praised as such by the same Monsignor Querenghi in another sonnet.

And worthy of notice (inasmuch as on the band one sees Minerva, Diana and Hercules) is the account of Pausanias in his account of Arcadia, which is the eighth book: that in Megalopolis there were to be seen statues of Proserpina and of Ceres; and that before Proserpina stood two young girls, who carried on their heads a little basket each, full of flowers; and that they were called the daughters of Damophon, but that others held that they were Minerva and Diana, who in company with Proserpina gathered flowers; that before Ceres stood Hercules, a cubit in size; and that Onomacritus writes that this Hercules was one of the Idaean Dactyls.

Lions in that manner are fitting for Pluto more than for Cybele. It is known that Pluto was the god of the earth; and yet, as Signor Aleandro says, no one is found who gives them to Pluto, as far as it has been possible to observe. The serpents that draw the chariot of Ceres I remember having seen thus fashioned in relief on a very ancient vase of gemstone. And on an ancient medal that I have, they draw the chariot of Triptolemus, fashioned in this manner.

<aside>Fol. 191, line 22.</aside>
The image of Attis is seen on fol. 188 [?3], taken from an ancient marble that is in Rome, and from a ring gem. However, since a very beautiful one was found in Flanders in past years, I wished to place it in this spot.

And whoever wishes to see more of them can read about them sufficiently in a Commentary of ours printed in Paris and reprinted in Venice with the title *Mysteries of M. D. M. I. and of Attis*. And Signor de Peiresc, Royal Councillor at Aix in Provence, a most learned and most noble gentleman, has a similar one; but his is in a more agitated cast and with a rougher face. With the one from Flanders there was found a hand of the same material, that is, of bronze, which I have likewise explained in the booklet cited above.

> Which has some resemblance to a hand placed on a spear of the god Tillinus, explained and set out by Signor Ottavio Rossi in his *Brescian Memoirs*, on pages 128, 129.

> To Tillinus.

But since Signor Girolamo Aleandro has done me the favour of writing to me his opinion about the hand of Tornax, somewhat different from what I have written about it, I did not wish to withhold it from the scholars who have a taste for this learned knowledge. He writes thus:

> On the occasion of having considered the little work of Your Worship, full of most beautiful erudition, I shall not fail to tell you that the bronze hand could have been made by someone out of his devotion, who was initiated both in the mysteries of Cybele and of Isis and also of Bacchus, it seeming to me that the figures contained in it may pertain to all three. For there is no doubt that to Cybele pertain the pine cone, the flutes, and the cymbals; to Bacchus the sickle for the grape harvest, the phallus, which may likewise belong to Isis, from what was written of Osiris.
>
> The thyrsus, which I believe to be what Your Worship calls “a sceptre marked with a pine cone,” because on ancient wall paintings I have observed that the thyrsi that the Bacchantes hold in their hands are of the same form. The cithara too, which is seen next to the vine-dresser's sickle, may belong to Bacchus. To Isis it is clear that the sistrum belongs, and perhaps also the half-moon and the whip that was depicted everywhere in the hand of Horus, and to this Horus the tortoise also belongs, since I consider most true the explanation given of it by our Sandelli, because in a book of drawings that belonged to Fulvio Orsini, which today is preserved in the Vatican Library, among the other drawings of statuettes of that same Horus Harpocrates, there are two at whose feet a dog and a tortoise are seen. The serpent, as is known to Your Worship, may regard both Bacchus and Isis, and perhaps also the mysteries of Attis, as you discuss.
> *(Girolamo Aleandro)*

So far Signor Aleandro, very learnedly.

## Doubtful passages

- [?1] “Aratro del Grotio” (scan 0571): the printed word is “Aratro” (“plough”); the sense requires “Arato” (Grotius's work on Aratus, *Syntagma Arateorum*, 1600). Rendered “Grotius's *Aratus* [?]”.
- [?2] “contraceuole” (scan 0573, on the cornucopia and Equity): the word does not exist; probably a misprint for *confaceuole* (“fitting”). Rendered “more fitting [?]”.
- “l'Equità” (scan 0573): rendered “Equity”, to distinguish it from “Giustitia” (“Justice”), which the author discusses below; the glossary has the entry (Equità, “Equity”).
- “la mano di Tornax” (scan 0585): *Tornax* is kept as printed (“the hand of Tornax”); it may be a place of discovery (Tournai, Latin *Tornacum*), but the text does not say so, so the name is not altered.
- [?3] “si vede 188” (scan 0578): after “si vede” the words “a car.” (“at fol.”) are missing; rendered “is seen on fol. 188 [?]”. The marginal note at this paragraph is “Fol. 191, line 22”.
- “M. D. M. I. & Attidis initia” (scan 0580): the first part of the title is given in initials (probably “Magnae Deum Matris Idaeae”, “of the Great Idaean Mother of the Gods”); kept as printed, “M. D. M. I.”; *initia* belongs to both genitives and is rendered “mysteries” (initiatory rites): “Mysteries of M. D. M. I. and of Attis”.
- “Atide” (scans 0578, 0580, 0586): “Attis” (as in the chapter on the Great Mother).
- “Veretro” (scan 0586, letter of Aleandro): *veretrum* is the Latin word for the male member; rendered “phallus”.
- “Horatio Marij Tigrino” (scan 0571): the genitive “Marij” stands with the name; rendered “Orazio Mario Tigrino” (the name as in the glossary; the surname may be “Marius”).
- “nella mia di Cepione” (scan 0571): *mia* is feminine, the noun is omitted (not *denario*); rendered “on my coin of Caepio”.
- “Aes graue” (scan 0572): the name of the Roman cast coinage (*aes grave*).
- “Pesi” (scan 0572): “Weights”; both times capitalised in the original, in the translation capitalised at the first mention (as the name of a thing) and lower case after; “oncia” is “ounce”, “Semuncia” is “semuncia” (half-ounce).
- “la Iana di Nigidio” (scan 0572): *Iana* is the female counterpart of Janus in Nigidius Figulus; rendered “the Iana of Nigidius”.
- “Abrotano” (scan 0573): abrotano (southernwood), as in the chapter on Diana; “Abrotano femina”, “female abrotano”.
- “Πυρφοροι” (scan 0573): the Greek word is printed without accents; rendered as “Fire-bearers” with the Greek word in brackets (with accents).
- “Ferepoli” (scan 0573, in Pausanias on Pindar): *Φερέπολις*; rendered “Pherepolis”, with the author's gloss (“tutrice delle Città”) as “guardian of the cities”.
- “della editione Vecheliana Suida, & Giulio Polluce” (scan 0573): the comma after “Vecheliana” is missing; read as three authors: Herodotus (in the Wechelian edition), the Suda, Julius Pollux.
- “memorie Bresciane” (scans 0573, 0583): title of Ottavio Rossi's work; rendered *Brescian Memoirs*.
- “palmo” (scan 0573): a measure of length (about 22 cm); rendered “span”.
- “dum fata sinebant” (scan 0578, in italics): rendered “while the fates allowed”.
- “Dattili Idei” (scan 0578): “Idaean Dactyls”.
- “Gioia” (scan 0578): “gemstone”, “gem”.
- “Ayx di Prouenza” (scan 0580): Aix-en-Provence; rendered “Aix in Provence”.
- The caption to the engraving on scan 0583 begins with the word “Chi” (probably a misprint for “Che”); the caption continues the sentence from scan 0580 (“…la mano … nel sopra citato Libretto”), a relative clause attached to “mano”; rendered “Which has some resemblance…”. The inscription “TYΛΛINI” on scan 0584 (dative) is rendered “To Tillinus” (after the caption of the previous engraving, the god Tillinus).
- Misprints silently corrected: “figura ono”, “queste” (for “questa”), “nõ” and “brõzo” (tildes), “Apolledoro”, “Sicolo”, “Pirrha”, “ora” (for *era*, scan 0573, “l'Abrotano femina ora pianta”), “haunto” (for *hauuto*, scan 0578), “particularità”.
- “Fornuto” (scan 0573): rendered “Cornutus”, following the glossary.
- “murari antichi” (scan 0586): the word does not exist; possibly *murali* (“wall paintings”); rendered “on ancient wall paintings”.
- Marginal notes: on scan 0573 there are six, given in the printed order: “Fol. 110”, “Fol. 188”, “Fol. 158”, “Fol. 325”, “Fol. 406”, “Fol. 412”; the numbers 188 and 158 are out of sequence, but they are matched to the paragraphs in order (“Panic” and “Bouphonos”). Paragraphs without notes: the continuations on scans 0578 (first), 0580 and 0585–0586, and the paragraph of Aleandro; scans 0575–0577, 0579, 0581–0582 are engravings only.
- The letter of Aleandro (scans 0585–0586) is set as a quotation with the author's name; the forms of address “V. Sig.” in the letter are rendered “Your Worship”.
