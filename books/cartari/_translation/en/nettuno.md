# Neptune

Source: books/cartari/_text/TEXT_reading.md, scans 0243–0269
Rules: books/cartari/_translation/en/RULES.md

---

<aside>Statius</aside>

Neptune was, of the three brothers, the one to whom the kingdom of the waters fell by lot, and therefore he was called god of the sea. The ancients depicted him in various ways, showing him now calm, quiet and peaceful, and now all disturbed, as one sees in Homer and in Virgil, because the sea likewise shows itself so according to the variety of the times. And sometimes the ancients have shown him with the trident in his hand, and upright in a great sea shell, which serves him as a chariot, drawn by horses that from the middle backwards were fish, as they are described by Statius, when he speaks thus:

> Crossing the Aegean Sea, Neptune leads into harbour
> his weary steeds:
> for they have the head, the neck, the breast and the forehooves
> of a horse that obeys the bridle,
> and for the rest, then, are darting fish.
>
> *(Statius)*

<aside>Lucian</aside>

And sometimes they have dressed him as well, putting about him a cloth of sky-blue colour, as Cornutus says, which represents the colour of the sea. And Lucian in his *Sacrifices* imagines him with hair likewise sky-blue, and black as well; although Servius says that among the ancients all the gods of the sea were depicted with hoary and white hair, and for the most part old, inasmuch as their heads grow white from the foam of the sea.

<aside>Philostratus</aside>
<aside>Glaucus</aside>

Thus Philostratus, painting Glaucus, who was likewise a god of the sea, says that he has a white beard, all wet and sodden, and his hair, equally wet, spreads over his shoulders; the eyebrows are thick, bushy and joined together, and the arms are like those of one who, wishing to swim, cuts the waves with them, and makes them easy for swimming; the chest is all laden with green down and with seaweed, and the belly gradually changes in such a way that the rest of the body, the thighs and the legs become fish, which is shown with its tail raised out of the water.

> Image of Glaucus, sea god, denoting the colour and foam of the sea, and that the fish live long and healthy lives; it also denotes the effects of the humidity of the waters.

<aside>Ovid</aside>

And in Ovid, when Glaucus tells Scylla, his beloved, how from a fisherman he became a god of the sea — after he saw that the fish he had caught, no sooner laid on the grass, threw itself back into the sea, so that he, having likewise tasted that grass, was driven to throw himself in after it — he at the same time describes his own figure in this way:

> Then at once I saw this beard
> and this hair, all green,
> cover my chest and my broad [?1] back, and I saw
> these arms likewise turn green,
> and the thighs and the legs become fish.
>
> *(Ovid)*

<aside>What the trident signifies</aside>

The same Philostratus says next of Neptune that he goes over the calm and quiet sea upon a great shell drawn by whales and sea horses, holding the trident in his hand, which some say signifies the three gulfs of the Mediterranean Sea that come from Oceanus, and according to others shows the three natures of waters; for those of springs and rivers are sweet, the sea waters are salt and bitter, and those of lakes are bitter, but not pleasant to the taste either.

<aside>Triton</aside>
<aside>Statius</aside>

He is likewise given the buccina, that sounding shell which the Tritons always carry. They too were placed by the ancients among the gods of the sea, and they accompany Neptune almost always. Hence in Statius two of them go at the bridles of the horses, when he says:

> There comes the king of the sea, high and sublime,
> drawn by most fierce steeds,
> and at their foaming bridles go
> the Tritons, swimming, and give a sign
> to the waves that they should grow calm.
>
> *(Statius)*

And the fables say that the Tritons are the trumpeters and the heralds of the sea, because they carry in their hand that shell, twisted upon itself, with which they make a terrible sound. Hence Hyginus writes that when the Giants were fighting with the gods of Heaven, a Triton came with the buccina, which he had found only a little before, and with it made so terrible and frightening a sound that the Giants, unable to bear it, all went off in flight.

<aside>Virgil</aside>

And these were animals — which it seems to me more reasonable to call Tritons than gods or men — with the upper half of human form and that below of fish, as Virgil says,

> That his first aspect is that of a man, and the rest fish.
>
> *(Virgil)*

<aside>Sea men</aside>

This double form, as some say, signified the double power of water, because it sometimes does good and sometimes does harm. Nor was this matter of the Tritons altogether feigned by the poets; for histories relate that men of the sea are really found, who are half fish.

<aside>Pliny</aside>

And Pliny writes that in the time of the emperor Tiberius ambassadors came to Rome expressly from Lisbon, the chief town of Portugal, to say that on their shores a Triton had been heard sounding the buccina, and had also been seen by many.

<aside>Alexander ab Alexandro</aside>

And Alexander ab Alexandro tells of a gentleman from his own country, who said that he had seen a man of the sea, preserved in honey, sent to Spain from the farthest parts of Africa as a monstrous thing, and he described him in this way: he had the face of an old man, hair and beard bristly and rough, the colour sky-blue, and he was of great stature, larger than a man; he had certain fins, such as fish have, and was covered with a hide all shiny and almost transparent.

<aside>Theodorus Gaza</aside>

And the same Alexander adds that Theodorus Gaza affirmed that he had seen, when he was in the Peloponnese, a Nereid, cast up on the seashore by a great storm, with a human and quite beautiful face, covered from the neck down to the thighs with hard scales, and the thighs, joined together, turned into a fish.

<aside>Nereids</aside>

Hence it is no wonder that the poets afterwards imagined the Nereids to be most beautiful nymphs, who accompanied their gods, such as Oceanus, Nereus their father, Neptune, Tethys, Doris and many others; who show the diverse qualities and the various effects of the waters; and they were worshipped by the ancients, as though they could do much good and much harm.

<aside>Galatea</aside>

And although there were many Nereids, since Hesiod counts fifty and names them all, I shall nevertheless speak of only one, who is Galatea, who was so called from whiteness, which perhaps represents in her the foam of the water, or, to put it better, from the name Gala, which means milk; and so Hesiod gives her white hair and a face like milk. Polyphemus, in love with her, wishing to praise her in Ovid, likewise calls her whiter than the whitest privets.

<aside>Philostratus</aside>

And Philostratus, in a picture that he makes of the Cyclops, shows Galatea going over the quiet sea upon a chariot drawn by dolphins, which are guided and steered by some daughters of Triton, who stand around the beautiful nymph, always ready to serve her; and she, raising her beautiful arms, stretches out to the gentle breeze of Zephyrus a purple cloth, to make a cover for the chariot and shade for herself, and has her hair not scattered by the wind, but wet, lying stretched out partly over her white shoulders.

> Images of Tritons and Nereids, sea men and women, according to Alexander ab Alexandro, Theodorus Gaza and other ancient and modern authors; with the image of Galatea, chief of the Nereids, and her chariot, signifying the double power of the waters.

I shall not omit to tell this too, which the same Alexander reports as true, as having once happened in Albania: a Triton, or let us call him a man of the sea, if that seems good to us, saw from a certain cave on the seashore a woman going for water not far from there, and lay in ambush so long that he was suddenly at her back, without her noticing it; and having seized her and used force on her, he dragged her with him into the waves. For which reason the people of that country spied on him so long that they caught him; but once drawn out of the water he did not live long.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>
<aside>Tritons</aside>

Pausanias, writing of Boeotia, depicts the Tritons thus. They have hair like marsh celery in colour, in such a way that one hair cannot be told from another, but they are woven together like the leaves of parsley, and the whole body is covered with fine scales, rough and hard. They have gills under the ears, the nose of a man, a mouth much wider than the human, teeth like those of panthers, and eyes of a greenish colour; the fingers of the hands and the nails are like the upper shell of shellfish, and on the chest and on the belly, like dolphins, they have certain little fins instead of feet.

<aside>Sirens</aside>

From these and from the Nereids the Sirens are not very different, for the fables tell of them that they likewise have the face of a woman, and the rest of the body as well, except that from the middle downwards they become fish; and some depict them with wings, and add to them the feet of a cock. And they say that they were three daughters of Achelous and of Calliope the Muse: of whom one sang; the other played the pipe, or the flute, as we choose to say; the third the lyre, and all together they made so sweet a harmony that they easily drew the wretched sailors to break upon certain rocks of Sicily, where they dwelt.

But, they say, seeing themselves scorned by Ulysses, who, passing that way, had himself tied to the mast of the ship, and had his companions' ears stopped with wax so that they should not hear them, they threw themselves into the sea in despair, and it was then perhaps that they became fish from the middle downwards. It is said that they were allowed to live until there should come one who, notwithstanding their song, with which they led everyone to death, should depart from them free; and that therefore at the departure of Ulysses they died, as has been said.

<aside>Servius</aside>

Servius depicts them not as fish but as birds in the part that is not a woman's, as Ovid also does, when he relates that these were companions of Proserpina, who, after she was carried off by Pluto, were changed into such animals, which had the face and breast of a woman, and the rest was bird.

Suda likewise reports that the Greek fables imagined the Sirens to be birds with a beautiful woman's face, who sang most sweetly. But, it says, in truth they were certain rocks, between which the waves of the sea made so sweet a murmur that sailors, drawn by the sweetness of the sound, willingly passed there, where they then wretchedly perished.

<aside>Pliny</aside>

And Pliny, speaking of fabulous birds, says that Siren birds were believed to exist in India, which with the sweetness of their song put others to sleep and then devoured them. But whether the Sirens were fish or birds, as I said, it is enough that they are altogether a fiction. Hence some hold that by them are understood the beauty, the wantonness and the allurements of prostitutes, indeed that they were the prostitutes themselves, and that it was feigned that by singing they put the sailors to sleep, and that coming up to the ships they then killed them; for so it befalls those wretches who, overcome by the pleasing ways of rapacious women, close the eyes of the intellect, so that these then make a rich prey of them and almost devour them.

<aside>Virgil</aside>

For which reason Boccaccio reports that the ancients depict the Sirens in green meadows strewn all over with the bones of the dead, as though they wished thereby to show the ruin and the death that accompany, or follow after, lascivious thoughts. And in Virgil the rocks of the Sirens are likewise described as covered almost entirely with the bones of the dead, and extremely difficult and very dangerous.

<aside>Xenophon</aside>

But Xenophon on the contrary maintained that the Sirens were something pleasant and virtuous. For, narrating the sayings and deeds of Socrates, he writes that they sang only the true praises of those who were worthy, exalting their virtues in them; that therefore in Homer they sang of Ulysses that he was worthy to be praised supremely, because he was a great ornament to all the Greeks; and that these were the enchantments and the sweet accents with which they drew virtuous men to themselves. For these men, hearing virtue praised, which they love so much, seek each time to draw ever nearer to it, and easily and willingly go after the sweet song of the praiser.

<aside>Aristotle</aside>

And perhaps it was for this reason that, as Aristotle writes in the *Marvellous Things of the World*, in certain islands, called the Islands of the Sirens, lying within the bounds of Italy, they had temples and altars, and were worshipped by those peoples with great solemnity, and their names were Parthenope, Leucosia and Ligeia.

> Images of Parthenope, Leucosia and Ligeia, Sirens, sea goddesses, daughters of the river Achelous and of Calliope the Muse; all these images signify prostitutes and their blandishments and allurements; they also denote certain rocks, and eloquent praisers, and flatterers.

<aside>Scylla</aside>

Now let us return to Neptune. For there are, indeed, in the sea many other monsters, both real ones and ones feigned by the poets, as Homer feigns of Scylla, who lived in a dark and frightening cave and made the sea resound with a terrible barking; and he feigns that this monster had twelve feet and six necks, with as many heads, and each mouth had three rows of teeth, from which deadly poison seemed to drip continually. And out of the dreadful cavern she often thrust her frightening heads [?2] into the sea, watching whether any ship should pass that way, so as to make a wretched prey of the sailors, as she once did with the companions of Ulysses, of whom she seized and cruelly devoured as many as there were voracious mouths.

<aside>Charybdis</aside>

And when in Virgil Helenus shows Aeneas the course he is to hold in order to sail safely to Italy, he says to him that he must beware of two cruel and frightening monsters for those who pass the strait of Sicily; of which one is Charybdis, who wretchedly gulps down and swallows ships, and draws them almost into the depths, and then casts them up again, driven by furious waves that lift them almost to the sky. Of her the fables tell that she was a most rapacious woman, who stole the oxen of Hercules, for which she was struck by lightning by Jupiter, and, thrown into the sea, became the rock that has ever since kept her former rapacious nature.

The other is Scylla; she hides in a horrible cave and often puts her head out, to see whether a ship passes of which she can make a cruel prey. This monster has the aspect of a beautiful girl down to the girdle, where the other limbs are then wolves and dogs joined together, with dolphins' tails, which make horrible barking resound there everywhere. And the wretched Scylla, who had once been a most beautiful nymph, became such through the jealousy of Circe, who was in love with Glaucus, who loved not her but Scylla. So the terrible enchantress sprinkled her enchanted juices where the beautiful nymph used often to go to bathe, and made her become such as I have depicted her, so that unhappy Scylla, unable to bear the fright of the animals that had been born around her, went and threw herself into the sea, and there remained the horrible monster of which I spoke, following the fables, which in this way sought to express with some charm the nature of these dangerous rocks.

> Image of Scylla, a Sicilian rock, called by the poets a most atrocious sea monster; it signifies the dangers to which sailors are exposed: storms, rocks, shoals, pirates, and a thousand grave and deadly evils.

<aside>Nereids</aside>

Although, then, as I have said, there are still other monsters in the sea, it is not for me to speak of all, but of some only, which the ancients placed among the gods, or added to them for company, as were the sea nymphs and the Tritons, of whom I have already spoken, because these accompanied Neptune. And of the Nereids Plato writes that there were a hundred of them, who sat upon as many dolphins, when he describes that great and marvellous temple which was among the Atlanteans consecrated to this god. The god stood there upon a chariot, holding with his hand the reins of the winged horses, and was so great that he touched with his head the roof of the lofty temple.

<aside>Palaemon</aside>

A good part of the company of Neptune was also to be seen in a temple of his in the land of Corinth, as Pausanias recounts; for he stood with Amphitrite his wife upon a chariot, where there was also the boy Palaemon supported by a dolphin: they were drawn by four horses, and had at their side two Tritons. In the middle of the base that supported the chariot the sea was carved, and Venus, who came out of it accompanied by most beautiful Nereids. Palaemon was among the Greeks the one whom the Latins called Portunus, god of harbours, to whom sacrifice was made by sailors who had returned safely to harbour: therefore he goes with Neptune, universal god of the sea.

> Images of Neptune, god of the sea, and of Amphitrite his wife, signifying the foam of the sea and the three qualities of the water of the sea, of rivers and of lakes: salt, sweet and neutral; with the swift and frequent motion of the waters.

<aside>Canopus</aside>

In the temple of Neptune in Egypt Canopus was also worshipped, once the pilot of Menelaus, and afterwards placed among the stars. His image there was thick, short and almost entirely round, with a twisted neck and very short legs.

The cause of this figure was that the Persians went about with the god Fire, whom they chiefly worshipped, and destroyed all the other gods, of whatever material they might be, to which they brought it near, to see which of them had the greater strength. The priest of Canopus, in order not to let his god be destroyed, took that hydria with which they purified the water of the Nile, and having stopped up well with wax all the holes that were around it, filled it with water, and having set upon it the head of Canopus, painted and dressed it in such a way that it seemed to be the idol of that god, and so put it to the proof against the god Fire. The fire melted the wax, the holes opened, and the water came out in such abundance that it put out the fire, and therefore the god Canopus remained victor over the god of the Persians, as Suda reports, and ever afterwards for this reason his idol was in the form that I have described, and as can be seen on an ancient medal of Antoninus Pius.

<aside>Dolphins dear to Neptune</aside>
<aside>Aelian</aside>

We read also that dolphins were dearer to Neptune than all the other fish. Hence Hyginus writes that on all his statues they put one in his hand, or under one foot, as is also seen in that statue set at the top of the stairs that goes into the palace in Venice, alongside that of Mars; perhaps because, according to Aelian, dolphins are kings of the fish, as lions are of the wild beasts and eagles of the birds.

<aside>Martianus Capella</aside>

In Martianus Capella, at the wedding of Philologia, Neptune is present too, and Martianus describes him naked, all green like the water of the sea, with a white crown on his head, which represents the foam that the agitated waves of the sea make.

<aside>Ovid</aside>

And when in Ovid Pallas, weaving, contends with Arachne, and puts on the cloth the quarrel she had with Neptune over the city of Athens, before twelve gods:

> She shows Neptune, in lofty mien,
> striking a hard rock with his trident,
> from which a steed comes forth, proud and fierce.
>
> *(Ovid)*

<aside>Virgil</aside>

Virgil too, at the beginning of his *Georgics*, says that Neptune, striking the earth with his trident, made a fierce horse come out of it. Servius holds that this was feigned in order to show by this animal the swift and frequent motion of the waters of the sea. Hence it was said that horses are also under the guardianship of Castor and Pollux, because their stars are very swift. Others have said that the invention of the horse was attributed to Neptune because it is an animal that wants to have level, open and spacious places, which are very well represented by the sea.

And the same Servius, where in Virgil Turnus raises the standards of war against Aeneas, says that the Romans likewise raised two of them at certain times, and that the one was vermilion, for the foot soldiers, the other sky-blue, for the cavalry, because this is the colour of the sea, and the god of the sea was the inventor of the horse.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

Diodorus writes that Neptune was the first to tame horses and to teach the art of riding, and that for this reason he was surnamed Equestrian, as Pausanias also writes; and Pausanias says that for this reason Homer, describing the horse race, introduces Menelaus, who requires an oath by the numen of Neptune that no fraud will be used there. And Pausanias adds that the surname Equestrian is the most notable of all in this god, because it is common to all nations.

<aside>Consus, god</aside>

Hence it was perhaps also that among the Romans the Circensian games, where the horses ran, were celebrated in honour of Neptune, and the festival was called the Consualia, on whose day the horses rested from their labours and the mules were seen with their heads wreathed with flowers of various kinds; which was the festival, as Livy writes, that Romulus had celebrated when he carried off the Sabine women. For, according to what Plutarch reports, he had already found there underground an altar, where there was a god called Consus, either because he was believed to give counsel (*consiglio*) to others, or because the counsel of great affairs must be secret and hidden. And therefore that altar was never opened, except at the festival of the Circensian games that I mentioned, which led people to believe that the god Consus was Neptune, of whom it will suffice to have made this small sketch, because I have not yet found any idol of him.

And that horses belonged to Neptune is also shown by what Pausanias writes: that in Greece, in a certain place where horses ran, there was on one of the sides of the course an altar, quite round, at which they worshipped Taraxippus, so called from putting fear into the horses; for these, on arriving at that altar, were at once so greatly frightened that they behaved in the strangest ways in the world, to the very grave harm of those who drove them. From this it came about that, before setting off on the course, they always went to the said altar and prayed that god, with certain ceremonies and vows, to be kindly and gracious to them and to their horses.

Pausanias next relates many opinions of him, as to who he was; but from all of them he resolves to believe that the truest is that this Taraxippus was a surname of Neptune Equestrian, because the first origin of horses came from him; from whom, as we also read, Juno had two horses as a gift, afterwards given by her likewise to Castor and Pollux. And with all this accords the fact that Ops showed Saturn that she had produced a little foal when she gave birth to Neptune; which Festus puts among the reasons he gives why Neptune was called Equestrian: and he says that for this reason in Illyria every nine years they threw four horses into the sea for Neptune.

<aside>Philostratus</aside>

Some have also held that the horse suits him, because the sea thus brings us from every side the necessary things, as horses do. So Philostratus, painting two little islands, which had a single square common between them, where the one brought what it gathered from its cultivated fields, the other what it went plundering from the sea, says that there was set up there a statue of Neptune with the plough and with the cart, as of a cultivator of the land; he who made it wishing to show that the people of those islands owed to him also whatever comes from the land; but lest it should seem that he had represented him as a god of the land only, he added to the plough the prow of a ship, so that it seemed that Neptune, sailing, ploughed the land.

> Image of Neptune, god of the sea, in Philostratus; or, rather, image of Taraxippus, the frightener of horses, taken for Neptune; and this signifies that by sea are conveyed all the things necessary for livelihood, and every sort of merchandise destined for human use.

And among the Eleans in Greece there was a certain statue, as Pausanias writes, of a beardless youth, who kept one foot over the other and stood leaning with both hands on a spear; this was dressed at certain times now in garments of linen and now of wool. And it was believed to be of Neptune, who, brought there from some other place in Greece, was then held in the greatest reverence by all the people of the country, although he was named not Neptune but Satrap.

Two ancient medals are also to be seen, one of Vespasian and the other of Hadrian, on which the image of Neptune is represented in the form of a man, who stands on his feet quite naked, save that from the left shoulder a cloth hangs, and has in the right hand a whip of three thongs, holding the trident aloft with the left. And on a certain other medal, likewise ancient, Neptune is indeed represented naked and upright on his feet, but with the left hand raised and resting on the trident, he holds out a dolphin with the right, and keeps one of his feet upon the prow of a ship.

<aside>Foundations of Neptune</aside>
<aside>Virgil</aside>

Besides this, the ancients held that the gates of cities were given to Juno, the citadels and fortresses to Minerva, and to Neptune the walls and the foundations, as Servius notes, where in Virgil Venus shows Aeneas that the ruin of Troy cannot be repaired, because these gods were labouring to lay it low, each ruining what was his own, and so she says to him:

> Here, where you see the lofty buildings,
> broken and undone, go to the ground, and the smoke
> mixed with dust billows up to the sky,
> Neptune with his great trident shakes and beats down
> the walls, and from the deep foundations
> tears them up, and ruins the whole city.
>
> *(Virgil)*

<aside>Ennosigaeus</aside>
<aside>Earthquake attributed to Neptune</aside>

And for this reason he was called by the Greeks Ennosigaeus, which is to say shaker of the earth, holding that the frightful earthquake came from him, and was produced by the movement of the waters. For which reason the people of Thessaly said that Neptune had given an outlet to the waters that formerly flooded all that country surrounded by high mountains, because, shaking the earth, he opened between them a rather wide way for the river Peneus, as Herodotus recounts, and says that it seems to him that the separation of those mountains did not come from anything other than the earthquake, and that all those who hold that from him come the shaking of the earth and the ruins that follow from it will always say that Neptune made it. This I have said, not because it serves much for the image of Neptune, but because it shows that he serves very well to depict the earthquake.

<aside>Oceanus</aside>
<aside>Tethys</aside>

Not very different from him was the image of Oceanus, whom the ancients called father of all the gods, and understood by him, besides the outer sea that surrounds the whole earth, the universal power of water also; which Thales of Miletus held to have been the beginning of all things; from this the fables took occasion to call Oceanus father of the gods, and therefore gave him a wife, who was Tethys, likewise a goddess, who bore a great number of sea gods, rivers, springs and nymphs.

<aside>Proteus</aside>

Tethys was old, quite grey and white, so that the poets often call her mother, and venerable, and in such an aspect she may be placed with her husband. He, as Boccaccio reports, was depicted upon a chariot drawn by whales over the broad sea, and the Tritons went before him with buccinas in their hands, who had the upper part human and that below of dolphin or of whale, as Cornutus holds, and many nymphs accompanied him around, and a numerous flock of sea beasts followed him, under the custody of Proteus, who was its shepherd. Proteus was likewise one of the gods of the sea, and often foretold to others things to come, but did not do so, however, unless compelled; and he even tried to deceive anyone who wished to use force on him, changing himself into diverse forms to get out of his hands; for it was necessary to bind him and hold him tight until he returned to his first figure; then he answered what was asked of him.

<aside>Diodorus</aside>
<aside>Proteus: why in diverse forms</aside>

Of him Diodorus writes that he was once elected king in Egypt, as the wisest to be found at that time in that country, and skilled in all the arts, with which he changed at his pleasure into diverse forms, which perhaps meant among those peoples that with his great prudence he knew how to adapt himself to all things. And the Greeks held that this was said of Proteus because of the custom the kings in Egypt had of wearing on the head, when they showed themselves in public, as the insignia of a king, now the front of a lion, now of a bull, or of a serpent, and at times a tree, or some plant, and at other times a flame of fire, as though in that way they were more conspicuous. The Greeks, then, feigned that Proteus thus changed into diverse forms, as they changed the royal insignia.

<aside>Shepherds of marine flocks</aside>

We read also that he was lord in Carpathos, an island from which the Carpathian sea toward Egypt takes its surname; and because this sea has a great number of seals, otherwise called sea calves, because they have the forward parts with the hide and hair of a calf, and of other similar beasts, it was feigned that Proteus was, as I said, shepherd and keeper of the flocks of Oceanus.

Eurynome too was called a daughter of Oceanus; for in Homer she accompanies Thetis when she goes to find Vulcan, although someone has preferred to believe her rather Diana, as Pausanias says; which does not at all accord with her idol, which was in the form of a woman above and of a fish below, bound across with golden chains. This was a certain numen worshipped in Arcadia by the Phigalians in a temple most holy to them, which they did not open except on one day of the year, and then they celebrated a solemn festival and made many sacrifices, in public and in private.

<aside>Derceto [?3]</aside>

And it brings to my mind another fabulous goddess, as Pliny calls her, named Derceto [?4] by the ancients, who was likewise all fish, except the head, which was a woman's.

<aside>Diodorus</aside>

Of her Diodorus writes that she was first a nymph, and that, having become pregnant without its ever being known by whom, she gave birth to Semiramis, with the gravest indignation at having lost her virginity, for which reason, throwing herself into a certain lake of Syria, she was afterwards worshipped as a goddess in the form that I have described, by those peoples, who afterwards would not for anything in the world have eaten any fish of that lake; because they judged that all were consecrated to her.

> Images of Eurynome and Derceto [?5], sea goddesses, the one daughter of Proteus, the other mother of Semiramis, signifying the property of the waters, and the effects and accidents that are seen in them.

But returning to Oceanus, to explain the rest of his image: the chariot shows that he goes around the earth, the roundness of which is shown by the wheels, and the whales draw him, because these run through the whole sea just as the waters of the sea surround the whole earth, and, spread also within it, occupy the greater part of it. The nymphs, then, mean to signify the property of the waters, and the diverse accidents that are often seen in them: which were understood by the ancients not only under the name of Oceanus, of Neptune, of Tethys, of Doris, of Amphitrite, and of other gods of the sea, but of Achelous also. Although some hold that those signified the nature of salt waters, and that by Achelous were understood the sweet ones, such as those of the rivers, whom the ancients likewise worshipped, and depicted in human form.

<aside>Winds</aside>
<aside>Virgil</aside>

But before I speak of the rivers, I shall depict the winds. For having spoken of the sea, where they perhaps show their forces better than in any other place, it seems to me reasonable to put them here. And although they would not have been out of place beside Juno either, who represents the air, since the naturalists hold that wind is nothing other than air moved with impetus; hence Aeolus, king of the winds, answered Juno thus, when she begged him in Virgil to disturb the sea with a very great storm, to the harm of the Trojans, who were sailing to Italy:

> You, whatever my kingdom may be, make me
> king, you make highest Jupiter a friend to me,
> and from you it comes that in my power are
> the fierce winds, the storm clouds and the tempests.
>
> *(Virgil)*

Nevertheless it will not be out of place now either to say of them the little that I have found written. The ancients worshipped these too as gods, and made sacrifice to them, either because they had already been favourable, or so that they should be favourable in the future: and they depicted them with wings, with the head all dishevelled, and with swollen cheeks, in the manner of one who blows with great force. And according as the effects that they produce by their blowing are diverse — for some gather the clouds together and make the rains, some drive them away, and in many other ways they show their power — so they were described diversely by the poets.

<aside>Principal winds</aside>
<aside>Ovid</aside>

And although of many we read, only four are the principal ones, which blow from the four parts of the world, each from his own, as they are portrayed by Ovid in the first division of the universe. But there have also been, according to Strabo, some who held that there were no more than two.

<aside>Boreas</aside>

The one is called Aquilo, and Boreas as well, and by the sailors of our times Tramontana; he blows from the north. Pausanias writes that he was carved on one side of the Chest of Cypselus in the temple of Juno among the Eleans in Greece, carrying off Orithyia, as the fables feign; nor does he say how he was represented, except that in place of feet he had serpents' tails. But because by his blowing he makes great cold, brings snow and hardens ice, he is given a beard, hair and wings all covered with snow.

<aside>Notus</aside>
<aside>Ovid</aside>

The other is Auster, also called Notus, and Ostro by the sailors, who comes from the parts of the south. Because by his blowing he brings for the most part rains, Ovid describes him thus:

> Notus spreads his dripping wings and comes
> with dark face, and laden with terror.
> His white hair is full of rain,
> and his bearded, bristling chin of storm clouds.
> Thick mist girds his brow, and he holds
> his brows heavy against the stormy wind;
> whose feathers and breast the waters wet at every hour,
> and never serene is his cloudy aspect.
>
> *(Ovid)*

<aside>Eurus</aside>

And of the four that I mentioned, the third is called Eurus, or Levante by our people, who blows from the parts of the east, and is depicted all black on account of the Ethiopians, who are in the east, from where he comes; and he is painted with a blazing sun on his head, because if the sun, when it sets, is red, it shows that this wind will blow the day that follows, as Virgil wrote.

<aside>Zephyrus</aside>
<aside>Flora</aside>

The fourth, whose light breathing is felt with a temperate and gentle breeze from the west, is Zephyrus, or Ponente according to the moderns, who therefore in the spring clothes the earth with green grasses and makes the greening meadows bloom. Hence it came about that the fables feigned him the husband of Flora, of whom we have already said that she was worshipped by the ancients as goddess of flowers, whose image was that of a beautiful nymph; so that she herself, when she tells Ovid the reasons for her festivals, says thus to him of her beauty:

> And out of modesty I do not tell you whether I
> was beautiful; but it is enough that I was such
> that a god did not disdain, only to have me,
> to come and make himself son-in-law to my mother.
>
> *(Ovid)*

Flora wore on her head a wreath of diverse flowers, and a dress likewise all painted with flowers of diverse colours, because they say that there are few colours with which the earth does not adorn itself when it flowers. And of Zephyrus Philostratus gives such a description. He is a youth with a soft and delicate face, has wings at the shoulders and on his head a wreath of beautiful and charming flowers.

> Images of Boreas, Auster, Eurus and Zephyrus, four principal winds, and of Orithyia and Flora, the one wife of Boreas, the other of Zephyrus, which show the effects of the said winds while they blow and hold sway in the seasons and countries subject to their blowing.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

I say no more of the winds, but return to the rivers, which the ancients likewise held to be gods, or numina, as one chooses to say, and they prayed to them with solemn vows, and made sacrifice to them no less than to the others, and used to offer them hair cut for that purpose with a certain ceremony; and all the Greeks did so by ancient custom, as Pausanias says, which can be gathered from Homer, where he puts it that Peleus makes a vow to the river Spercheus to cut his hair and give it to him, if Achilles returns safe and sound from the Trojan war. And in the land of Athens, near the river Cephissus, there was a certain statue of a youth who cut his hair to give it to the river.

> Images of Cephissus and the Po, rivers, the one of Greece, the other of Italy, and of a youth who, having cut his hair, offers it to the former; and they denote the nature and impetuosity of rivers with their murmur and winding course.

<aside>Philostratus</aside>
<aside>Inachus</aside>
<aside>Statius</aside>

Rivers were depicted in the form of a man with a beard and long hair, lying and leaning on one arm, as Philostratus says, when he paints Thessaly, because rivers never rise upright in height; and sometimes, and for the most part, the river leans upon a great urn that pours out water, and therefore Statius speaks thus of Inachus, a river that passes through Greece:

> Inachus, his head adorned with two horns,
> seated, rests his left hand on the urn,
> which, tipped over, pours out the waters widely.
>
> *(Statius)*

<aside>Tiber</aside>
<aside>Virgil</aside>

Rivers are depicted with horns, says Servius, either because the murmur of the waves represents the lowing of oxen, or because we often see the banks of rivers curved in the manner of horns. Hence Virgil, where he calls the Tiber king of the rivers of Italy, calls him horned too, and so depicts him when to Aeneas

> among the poplar leaves there seems to show himself
> one already aged, with shoulders and chest girt
> in a greening veil, and shady reed
> covers and surrounds his wet hair.
>
> *(Virgil)*

<aside>River Po</aside>
<aside>Probus</aside>
<aside>Aelian</aside>

Of the Po, who is also called Eridanus, Virgil says in another place that he has the face of a bull with both horns gilded. Probus, explaining this passage, says that the Po is feigned with the face of a bull because the sound that his course makes is like the lowing of bulls, and his banks are twisted like horns; and Aelian likewise writes that the statues of rivers, which at first were made without any form, were afterwards made in the form of an ox. So it is read also in Pompeius Festus, where he says that the idols of rivers were made in the form of bulls, that is, with horns, because they are fierce and cruel like bulls. Besides this, the ancients crowned rivers with reeds, because the reed is born and grows better in watery places than elsewhere, and hence it came about that Virgil, as I said just now, gave the Tiber a head covered with reed.

<aside>Acis, river</aside>
<aside>Ovid</aside>

And when Ovid tells the fable of Acis, already changed into a river, after Polyphemus had thrown that rock upon him which crushed him entirely, Galatea speaks of him thus:

> Suddenly, above the waters, the whole
> youth appeared as far as the waist,
> and into nothing else, it seemed to me, was he changed,
> except that he was of much greater stature.
> And the colour he had before also vanished,
> so that the face, once bright and pure,
> grows green, and the head is adorned with one horn and the other,
> around which goes green reed.
>
> *(Ovid)*

However, in Rome, in the Vatican, there is to be seen a statue of the Tiber that has no horns, nor the head girt with reeds, but with diverse leaves and fruits, he who made it perhaps wishing in that way to show the fertility and the abundance that this river brings about in that country; yet he did not altogether abandon the fiction of the poets, for he put a reed in its hand.

> Image of the Tiber, showing the abundance and the beginning of the Roman Empire in the two brothers.

<aside>Achelous</aside>
<aside>Horn [?6] of plenty</aside>

When in Ovid Achelous tells Theseus of the quarrel that he had with Hercules over Deianira, it is said that he is leaning on one of his arms, and has his head girt with green reed, and has a mantle, likewise green, about him, and he has not two horns like the others, but one only, because the other was broken off by Hercules, according to the fables; and this horn, filled with diverse flowers and fruits, was then given to the people of Aetolia, who called it the horn of plenty. And it was so feigned, as Diodorus recounts, because Hercules with no little labour turned one branch of that river aside from its first course, and diverted it to another place, which, where it was at first arid and bore no fruit, became fruitful beyond measure through the waters that this river sometimes spread over it by the diverted branch.

<aside>Nile</aside>

And so rivers are described diversely by the poets, who look now to the quality of the waters and their course, and now to the nature of the country through which they pass. So Pausanias, writing of Arcadia, says that in a certain part of that country there are some statues of the most noble rivers, celebrated by the ancients, all of the whitest marble, except that of the Nile, which is of black stone. And he adds then that the statue of the Nile was reasonably made of black stone, because the Nile, running to the sea, passes through the Ethiopians, a wholly black people. Lucian writes that the Egyptians, when painting the Nile, set him to sit upon a crocodile or on a river horse, which is a certain four-footed beast, as Herodotus describes it, of the size of a large bull, with a head like an ox's, the nose flattened like a goat's, the mane like a horse's, and the voice; the teeth sticking out, and uneven [?7], the tail shining, and the hide so thick and hard that when it is dry they make darts of it; and this animal was called by the Greeks hippopotamus. Around the Nile they depicted some little boys, who all cheerfully played, as is read also in Pliny, who, writing of a certain sort of marble, hard and rough as iron, says that Vespasian placed in the great Temple of Peace a statue of the Nile, the greatest that had ever been seen, with sixteen little children, who played around it, and signified that the waters of that river at the greatest rise that they made reached the height of sixteen cubits.

> Image of the river Nile, seated upon the Sphinx, with many boys around, who denote the degrees of the rise of that river, which are sixteen cubits as a rule.

<aside>Vertumnus</aside>

We read also that the statue of Vertumnus set in the Roman Forum represented the Tiber, which formerly passed through there, but was afterwards diverted to another place, and it was adorned with flowers and fruits, to show, as I said just now, the fertility of the fields near to him. Yet Vertumnus was also believed to be a god who presided over human thoughts and who changed into diverse forms, because men often change their thought.

<aside>Propertius</aside>

And some called him the god of the year, who according to the seasons takes on diverse faces, and gives men occasion to do now one thing and now another, as Propertius says, who gives the reason for his name and at the same time describes him so well that, since I have not the heart to say more of him, nor better, I shall set down only what he says of him, bringing some of his verses into the vernacular in this manner.

> Image of Vertumnus, with Pomona beside him; held to be the god of human thoughts, of the year, of gardens; changer of diverse faces, also understood as the river Tiber.
