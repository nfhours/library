**Preface to the Reader.**

What Anaxagoras said, that man was the most intelligent of beings because alone among all the other animals he had hands, was not so far beside the point as it seemed to Plutarch, and before him to Aristotle. For that great man was thinking, in saying so, of the conjunction that the hands ordinarily have with the mind. This is so true that Aristotle somewhere left it in writing for us that nature gave man two great instruments, the hand to the body and the mind to the soul. Now, just as these, when united, do works worthy of praise and wonder, so, when disunited, one cannot imagine what confusion they produce and what disorder in any invention that is otherwise well regulated.

All this has been confirmed notably, up to now, in many cases; but chiefly in many works of the intellect that are in need of figures, which, to their misfortune, abandoned by their own authors through death or some other accident, have come into the hands of painters or draughtsmen who knew well what a stroke of the pen or of the brush was, but were then stripped of that knowledge which the complete perfection of such a work required. For my part, I know that I have observed many such, and many others will have been observed by whoever has laboured more than I in similar matters; but to prove my point it will be enough for me to touch on just a few, in passing.

Now Horapollo (to begin from here), if that book of Hieroglyphics which we have is by Horapollo, or is rather an abridgement of the same made by others — Horapollo, I say, has been printed in Italy and elsewhere more than once with figures, and yet there is perhaps not even one among them that is right. The *Notitia Dignitatum* of the Eastern and Western Empire, the one that Guido Poncirolo, my teacher, a most illustrious man, has elucidated with a learned commentary, circulates with many drawings which do retain [?1] some tinge of antiquity, but with many absurdities inserted into them, which have reduced a very useful book to little less than a pastime for children.

How much the ancient astronomers have likewise suffered in this respect, let the figures seen in them say, which are quite without grace — with the exception, however, of the edition of Hugo Grotius, who some years ago brought out the *Phenomena* (if I am not mistaken) of Germanicus Caesar, with most elegant figures and most learned annotations. Giovanni Pierio Valeriano turned over and read carefully, in vain, all the beautiful and good that antiquity holds, in order to form from it his curious commentary on the Hieroglyphics, if he was then to be murdered (may Modesty forgive me) in the rendering into figures of what he had so gracefully expressed with the pen. Andrea Alciato, if he came back to life, would certainly not recognise as his own the *Emblems* that circulate with figures full of errors, both as to propriety and as to truth.

The *Fables* of Gabriele Faerno, a most elegant composition, have not been able to escape this audacity of painters; even though their author tried to arrange, as I have seen in an unprinted letter of his, that Pirro Ligorio, a man most expert in antiquities, should make the figures for this book of his, conformable to the author's mind and not far from the truth in dress and in bearing.

The *Dialogues* of Don Antonio Agostini, which have come out into the light twice within our memory through the work of diligent men who understood what they had in their hands, nevertheless have many blemishes (not to say more) and depart considerably from the delicate manner of that most exquisite man. Fulvio Orsini, too, did not know how to keep his draughtsmen in line, since his book on the Roman Families (a learned and elegant work otherwise) came out so spoiled (as regards the figures) that in Rome in my time there was someone who thought it labour not wasted to correct it. And so Abraham Gorlaeus in Holland also laboured over this shortcoming with a curious book of his, which, however, itself retains some small error of this kind. Justus Lipsius, the honour of our age, I know complains somewhere of the labour he endured in keeping in check the painters he needed in order to depict certain figures in his books of the *Saturnalia*.

And indeed, in the author we have in our hands, how foully they have sinned in the past will be easily seen by whoever takes the trouble to compare this edition of ours with the earlier ones, which were all defective and lacking in this respect, not excepting even the one in which, as regards the figures, the skilled painter Salviati was the architect. And although I did all that I knew how to do, labouring in a matter of this sort; and although Signor Filippo Ferroverde, with his skill and facility in drawing, neglected nothing to bring this work to complete perfection; nevertheless the negligence of some run-of-the-mill engraver proved stronger than all our effort.

Nor, truly, could it be avoided that the work be given into the hands of one of these spoilers, because the good and understanding ones were very busy, and the work could not be dragged out for ever, to the tedium of those who had so long been awaiting it. So this concern will be left to others, just as will the enlarging of the annotations, which I put together in a very short space of time amid a thousand occupations of another sort, and the adding to the second part of some curious figures of the Indian deities, which have not come into my hands, not for lack of diligence on my part, but for lack of courtesy in those who, even when asked, have begrudged the public what in some places, in private cabinets, for the pleasure of an envious eye, is kept hidden. I confess, however, that I owe much to the friends and patrons whom I have mentioned throughout the work, if not as they deserved, at least as affection, joined with the true limits of good breeding and courtesy, dictated to me.

As for what I myself have done in this work, it is not for me to say. I know well that I toiled greatly over it, since I had to go in search of the true and real images, of which this book, in as many editions as I have seen, has until now been deprived [?2]. Nor was it little labour for me to review (so to speak) the author's text, and to form upon it a few annotations, in which, from desire for the truth and to undeceive the readers, I was obliged to disagree at times with Cartari. This, I believe, will be forgiven me by the kindness of whoever reads these things: for it was not from a wish to play the master over anyone, but from a motive little short of conscience, that I arrived at times at a point to which I am not accustomed, as a rule, to come. It is true, however, I believe, that Modesty will not complain of me. In short, honest gentlemen will see my labour, and with their kindly censure I shall be content. God be with you.

## Doubtful passages

- [?2] "primo" (scan 0015): "delle quali questo Libro, in quante Editioni io ho vedute, e stato fin'hora primo" — "primo" ("first") does not fit the sense; probably a misprint for "priuo" ("deprived"). Translated "has until now been deprived [?]".
- [?1] "che ritengo pure qualche colore dell'Antichità" (scan 0013): the subject is "disegni" (drawings), yet the verb stands in the first person singular ("ritengo"); probably a misprint for "ritengono". Translated "which do retain [?]".
- "opera dotta e gentile per per altro" (scan 0014): the doubled "per" is an evident misprint; translated "otherwise".
- "tantotempo" (scan 0015): "tanto tempo" ("so long") printed run together.
- "ritiene… erroruccio", "molti nei (per non dir più)" (scan 0014): *nei* means "moles, spots"; rendered "blemishes" (that is, flaws).
- "Notitia dell'Imperio Orientale, & Occidentale" (scan 0013): this is the *Notitia Dignitatum*; Poncirolo (Panciroli) is the editor with a commentary; rendered *Notitia Dignitatum* of the Eastern and Western Empire, the surname as printed.
- "Guastatori" (scan 0015): literally "spoilers, wreckers"; rendered "spoilers" (printed with a capital in the source).
- "Architetto il Saluiati Pittore" (scan 0014): *architetto* rendered "architect".
- "sgratiatissime" (scan 0013): "quite without grace" (literally "deprived of grace").
- "Fenomeni… di Germanico Cesare" (scan 0013): the work of Germanicus (a translation of the *Phenomena* of Aratus); rendered *Phenomena*.
- "assassinato" (scan 0013): said figuratively of Valeriano; rendered "murdered".
- "A Dio" (scan 0016): a formula of farewell; rendered "God be with you".
- "confussione" (scan 0012): a slip for *confusione*, silently corrected.
- Forms of names: "Poncirolo" (*Poncirolo*, that is, Panciroli) and "Ferroverde" (*Ferrouerde*) are given as printed; "Gorlaeus" (*Gorleo*) and "Salviati" (*Saluiati*) follow the glossary.
