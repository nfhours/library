# Apollo, Phoebus — the Sun

<aside>How the gods of the ancients were introduced</aside>

Because the ancients held different opinions about the beginning of things, both as to what they were made of and as to who had created or made them, the poets, who were the first, as Aristotle says, to write about the gods, invented various fables about them, making the foolish people believe that there were many of them. For by calling gods the first makers of things and the principal matters of which things are composed, they expressed the various opinions of the different schools. And in this way, telling fables, they made gods of the elements, the stars, the Sun, and the Moon.

<aside>Lucian</aside>

From this they were afterwards given temples, altars, and images almost everywhere, except among some of the Assyrians, as Lucian writes, who said that images should indeed be made for those gods who are not seen in any other way, but not for the Sun or the Moon, because they are seen every day; and if they show themselves to us of their own accord every time we raise our eyes to heaven (said those people), why make other statues of them?

<aside>Macrobius</aside>

Nevertheless Macrobius reports that in a certain other part of Assyria, where the Sun and Jupiter, who signifies the soul of the world, were believed to be one and the same thing, there was a gilded image without a beard, which, standing with its arm raised, held in its right hand a whip like a charioteer's, and carried in its left the thunderbolt and some ears of grain; these things showed that the power of the Sun and of Jupiter is joined together.

And because it seems that of all the heavenly bodies the Sun has the greatest power in created things, and shows its effects in them more plainly than the others, some have held that in place of all the other gods only he is always to be understood, according to how he shows his powers in different ways. And therefore the ancients made statues of him in different ways, and he was called by different names, not only by different nations on account of the diversity of languages, but even by those of one and the same people, as will be said of some of them, as occasion arises, when I describe his image.

The Greeks sometimes called him Apollo, a name which comes from *a*, a privative particle that signifies "without", and *pollo*, which means "many", since he is alone; and sometimes they called him Phoebus, which among them means as much as light and life; and the Latins have called him so too, not having given him any other name in their language than Sun, as I too shall call him.

<aside>Alciato</aside>
<aside>Tibullus</aside>

The ancients depicted him young of face and without a beard; for this reason Alciato, wishing in his *Emblems* to represent youth, painted Apollo and Bacchus, as if to these two more than to the others it had fallen to be always young; hence Tibullus said:

> That Bacchus alone and Phoebus are eternally
> young, and have their heads adorned,
> both with beautiful shining hair.
>
> *(Tibullus)*

<aside>Dionysius the Tyrant</aside>

From this Dionysius, the tyrant of Syracuse, took occasion to cover his sacrileges with a jesting remark, when he removed the beard from the golden statue of Aesculapius, saying that it seemed most unseemly for the father to be beardless and the son to have one so long. For we read that Aesculapius was born of Apollo, whom they depict with fine blond hair, so that it seems of gold; and this shows the shining rays of the Sun.

<aside>Apollo always young</aside>
<aside>Hebe</aside>

His youth gives us to understand that his power, and that warmth which gives life to created things, is always the same, and never grows old so as to become weak. This seems to be proper also to all the other gods, who never grow old; hence Homer said that Hebe (a word which among the Greeks means the flower of age, and signifies the first down that youths grow) served the wine, or nectar as it may have been, and gave drink to all the other gods, just as Ganymede did to Jupiter alone.

<aside>Goddess of youth</aside>

For she was the goddess of youth, likewise worshipped by the ancients; and the Romans depicted her in the temple that was dedicated to her in the Circus Maximus by Gaius Licinius (vowed sixteen years before by Marcus Livius on the day he routed the army of Hasdrubal, as Livy writes), in the form of a most beautiful young girl, in garments of various colours and with wreaths of fine flowers on her head, little different from the goddess Pomona.

But whether she was depicted by the Greeks I could not say, because Pausanias writes that in the temple dedicated to her in the territory of Corinth, in a certain grove of cypresses, this goddess had no statue at all, neither one that showed itself nor one kept hidden, for a certain mysterious reason which he, however, did not wish to give, nor have I been able to find it written by others. Nevertheless those peoples worshipped her and paid her great honours, and the greatest was that whoever fled there humbly supplicating the goddess was freed, out of respect for her, from every punishment and penalty that he had deserved for any grave sin; and those who, being captives and in irons on their feet, were set free, used to bring their fetters there and hang them on the trees near the temple.

> Image and temple of Hebe, goddess of youth and cupbearer of the gods, daughter of Juno, without a father, with fetters and chains hung on the trees of her little grove, to show that the vigour of youth does not as a rule tolerate the blows of ill Fortune.

<aside>Lyre in Apollo's hand</aside>

Apollo then held a lyre in his hand, to show the sweetest harmony that the heavens make, moving with that proportion which best suits each of them, and which comes from the Sun; for he, standing in the middle of them, as Macrobius reports, and as was the opinion of the Platonists, gives law to all, so that they go now quickly and now slowly, according to how much or how little vigour they have from him.

<aside>Apollo head of the Muses</aside>

And because every heaven has its Muse according to the same Platonists, whom they also sometimes call Siren, because she sings most sweetly (which refers to the sweet sound of the celestial spheres, which are nine, exactly as many as the Muses), it was said that Apollo is the head and leader of these, and is always with them, as Pausanias says he was in the temple dedicated to them in common, that is, to Apollo and the Muses.

<aside>How many Muses</aside>

At the beginning they were named no more than three, with names in the Greek language such that in ours they meant Meditation, Memory, and Song. But Pierus of Macedonia, from whom a mountain of that country took its name, then ordained, as Pausanias writes, that the Muses should be nine, and gave them the names that they have always borne since. And from that mountain they were all together also surnamed Pierides, just as from various other places consecrated to them they had various other surnames.

<aside>Images of the Muses</aside>
<aside>Virgil</aside>

They were called daughters of Jupiter and of Memory, and the proper deities of the poets and of music, because whoever has a good intellect and a great memory easily becomes learned in that to which he applies his mind, and when he often makes fine and graceful compositions in it, he is said to have the Muses favourable to him. The ancients depicted them young of face and very beautiful, dressed like graceful Nymphs, with various instruments in their hands, according to the different inventions that they gave to each of them, as we read that Virgil did, who in certain verses of his makes history belong to Clio, tragedy to Melpomene, and comedy to Thalia; to Euterpe he gives the wind instruments, to Terpsichore the cithara, and to Erato the lyre; he makes heroic compositions come from Calliope, astrology from Urania, and rhetoric from Polyhymnia; and he says in the end that all their power comes from Apollo, and that Phoebus, standing in the middle of them, embraces everything.

The Muses were so named, and are of so great a number too, because exactly nine properties must be in anyone who desires to attain the perfect knowledge of any science. The first, who is called Clio, signifies glory, as if it is chiefly through glory that a man is induced to give himself to the sciences; the second, who is Euterpe, means the grace of God, whose favour is needed by whoever wishes to learn perfectly; the third, who is Melpomene, is interpreted as delight; for if science did not delight, hardly anyone would toil to acquire it; the fourth, who is Thalia, signifies capacity, it being necessary for him who wants to learn to be capable and intelligent about what he reads; the fifth, who is Polyhymnia, amounts to much memory, memory being one of the things principally necessary for learning; the sixth, who is Erato, means the invention of similar things, because he who learns must have the faculty of reasoning, so as to find new similar things himself too; the seventh, who is Terpsichore, signifies judicious, because the learned man must have good judgment in choosing good things and rejecting bad ones; the eighth, who is Urania, amounts to something celestial, because by choosing the better part (as has been said) one comes to acquire the name of celestial and divine; the ninth, who is Calliope, means as much as perfection of knowledge, and is the superior and the head of all the others, since when a man is perfect he no longer needs the help of others, but is himself the superior of all.

> Image of the nine Muses, referred to the harmony of the celestial spheres, and inventors of rhetoric, astrology, music, and dramatic fables.

<aside>Crowns of the Muses</aside>

They then crowned the Muses with various flowers and different foliage, and sometimes also with wreaths of palm, or else they bound feathers of various colours about their heads, either on account of the Pierides, who challenged them to sing and, afterwards defeated by them, as the fables say, were changed into magpies (*Piche*, that is *Gaze*), which even today know how to imitate the human voice, or else on account of the Sirens, likewise overcome by them in singing. And in our times one can still see in Rome some very ancient images of the Muses that have a feather planted on the top of the head, and it is believed that it was from the Sirens.

<aside>Apollo: why in the middle</aside>

To show that the liberal arts and the sciences all follow one after another and are, as it were, knotted together, the ancients depicted the Muses, their discoverers, as I said, holding one another by the hand and leading a fine dance in a circle; and Apollo either led them, being that supreme light which illuminates the human intellect, or stood in the middle of them. And the middle place is given to Apollo not only here but in the universe as well, because he spreads his power everywhere; hence he was called the heart of Heaven;

and to show that he had power here, and on earth too, and even in the underworld, the ancients put the Lyre in his hand, understanding by it the celestial harmony; at his side a shield, which represented our hemisphere, made in a circle and round like the shield; and they gave him arrows, which, because they penetrate with great force when they are shot from the bow [?1], show that his rays penetrate with their power as far as the bowels of the earth, where the lowest part of the world is, which is therefore called the underworld. All this Servius reports, taking it from a certain book of Porphyry called *The Sun*.

Some say that Apollo is called the god of the underworld, and that the arrows were put in his hand because the excessive heat of the Sun often does great harm to mortals, causing plague and other sicknesses; but because his temperate warmth also benefits us, he held the Graces in his right hand, as will be said when I come to their image, and the bow and arrows in his left: as if, by drying the moistures that constantly rise from the earth, he made the air purified and healthy.

> Apollo in the midst of the Muses, to give us to understand that the Sun has a diffusive power, and that the verses of the Muses would be worth little if Enthusiasm did not help.

<aside>Python killed by Apollo</aside>

From this the poets took occasion to feign that Apollo had killed with his arrows the great serpent Python, born of the earth as soon as the waters of the flood had ceased. For Python means nothing other than putrefaction, which often arises from the earth through too much moisture, and would cause the gravest ills if it were not consumed by the hot rays of the Sun, which are the sharp arrows of Apollo.

<aside>Wolf: why given to Apollo</aside>
<aside>What the Sun and the stars feed on</aside>

The same thing was shown also by the one who first consecrated the wolf to this god: for as the wolf seizes and devours flocks, so the Sun with his rays draws to himself and consumes the moist exhalations of the earth. Therefore it was also said that the Sun, the Moon, and all the other stars feed and nourish themselves on the moistures that the sea and the earth send them, as Marcus Tullius writes, reporting the opinion of Cleanthes the philosopher, when he discusses the nature of the gods. And Homer means this same thing too, when he feigns that Jupiter with the other gods, that is the Sun with the other stars, went to a banquet at Oceanus.

<aside>Apollo's wolf</aside>

It is also said that the wolf has so good an eye that he sees by night, just as the Sun, when it appears, overcomes the shadows of night. Hence in Delphi, in the temple of Apollo, there was one made of metal; because Latona, as the fables say, having been made pregnant by Jupiter and afterwards changed into this beast, fearing lest Juno perhaps should learn of it and, having found her, do her some harm, gave birth to Apollo as a she-wolf, just as she was.

Or else it was because we read that a wolf discovered the theft of the sacred things from that temple, in this way: he killed the thief, finding him asleep, and afterwards went so many times howling and crying that he moved some people to follow him, and led them to where he had seen the stolen things put away; and for this reason the wolf was made of metal and dedicated there to Apollo, in his temple. So Pausanias relates.

<aside>Apollo Lyceus</aside>

Pausanias also gives the reason for the temple dedicated in Argos to Apollo, surnamed there Lyceus, which in our language means Lupine. He says that Danaus, having come to Argos, was in dispute with Gelanor over the rule of the city; and the case being before the people, each stated his arguments so well that the judges were left in suspense, and the matter was put off to the following day, on which, early in the morning, a wolf was seen to attack a large herd of oxen and cows that were grazing around the walls, and, having flung himself upon the bull, the leader of the herd, killed him.

From this the Argives took the argument for the judgment they had to make, likening Danaus to the wolf; for as this beast is not at all domestic, so he, having come from outside, had not until that hour had any familiarity with the Argives; and Gelanor they likened to the bull, because he had always been in that country. And therefore, the wolf having killed the bull, Danaus was judged to have prevailed, and the rule of the city was given to him; and he, believing that Apollo had sent the wolf, afterwards built him the temple I mentioned, and called it Lyceus, that is Lupine, as I have also said. And besides the statue of the god that was in the temple, outside there was to be seen a great base, on which were carved the bull and the wolf fighting together, and a young girl throwing stones at the bull; and they said that she was Diana.

> Images of Apollo and of the animals and birds sacred to him, which signify the effects of the Sun; and that Apollo was the god of divination. He has the Graces in his hand, which signify the benefit we have from the Sun, and the usefulness that comes to us from it.

<aside>Apollo's raven</aside>

Besides the wolf, Apollo also had the raven, and Martianus Capella says that this was on account of divination, of which he was believed to be the god, since the raven by its nature divines rain and fair weather, and predicts them to us with a voice now clear and free, now hoarse and broken, as Virgil wrote where he teaches how to know when the weather is going to change. And the raven was believed to divine many other things as well, and to predict them likewise with different voices; for which reason the ancients observed it very closely in auguries.

<aside>Ovid</aside>

So it is no wonder that the raven was given to Apollo, of whom the fables also made it the minister and servant, as Ovid relates, who likewise says that Apollo, having fled with the other gods to Egypt to be safe from the hands of that great Typhon who was pursuing them all, changed himself there into a raven.

<aside>Apollo's swan</aside>

With the raven they also placed the swan, to show, as some say, that the Sun, when he comes to us, makes the day like the whiteness of the swan, and, departing from us, likewise makes the night black, as the raven is. And some have held that there is no bird more fitting to Apollo than the swan, both for its whiteness, which can represent the light of the Sun, and because it sings sweetly, and also because it divines its own death, and it is then that it sings most sweetly; either because it rejoices in death by a certain natural instinct, or because when it is about to die a great quantity of blood goes to its heart, and, all warmed by it, it seems to melt with sweetness, and therefore sings so tenderly. Others have said that the swan weeps, and does not sing, when it is about to die, because certain feathers that it has on its head grow so far inward that they pierce its brain, from which it dies.

<aside>Apollo's cock</aside>
<aside>Boeotians</aside>

Pausanias writes that in Greece they revered the cock as a bird of Apollo, because by crowing it announces in the morning the return of the Sun; and perhaps also because the ancients often divined from its voice the things, good or bad, that were to come, according to whether it crowed in season or out of season. So the Boeotians foretold that noble victory which they won over the Lacedaemonians, when the cocks crowed almost all night: for this bird, when it is beaten, is silent and hides, and when it is the victor shows itself quite joyful, and by crowing publishes its victory.

<aside>Apollo's hawk</aside>

Homer too represents the hawk as likewise consecrated to him, and calls it the swift messenger of Apollo, where he writes that Telemachus, having returned home to Ithaca, saw a hawk in the air tearing a dove; from which he took a good omen that he would deliver his house from those enamoured of his mother. And in Egypt under the image of the hawk they often understood Osiris, that is the Sun, both because this bird has the sharpest sight, and also because in flight it is the swiftest.

> Ship of the Sun carried by a crocodile, which signifies that the first cause governing the universe after God is the force of the Sun, joined in the generation of things with moisture; and that he purges the bad qualities of it.

<aside>Diodorus</aside>
<aside>Red cap: to whom given</aside>

The Egyptians worshipped it, as Diodorus writes, telling of the beasts that were kept by them as gods; besides the other reasons, for this one too, that in the earliest times a hawk came (no one knew from where) and brought to Thebes, a city of Egypt, to the priests a book written in red letters, in which it was told how and with what reverence the gods ought to be worshipped. Hence it came about that the writers of sacred matters there always afterwards wore on their heads a red cap with the wing of a hawk.

<aside>Porphyry</aside>
<aside>Ship of the Sun</aside>

Porphyry, writing of the abstinence of the ancients, says that those of Egypt, distributing different animals to different gods as their own, gave to the Sun the hawk, the scarab, the ram, and the crocodile. And therefore, as Eusebius reports, the theologians of Egypt placed the image of the Sun in a ship, which they had carried by a crocodile, wishing to show by the ship the motion that takes place in the moist for the generation of things, and by the crocodile fresh water, from which the Sun removes every bad quality, and purges it with his temperate rays.

<aside>Iamblichus</aside>

Iamblichus, speaking of the mysteries of Egypt, says that when they put God on the ship, and at the helm of it, they mean to signify the first cause that governs the universe, and that this, from above [?2], without itself moving at all, makes the second causes, and the others in turn, move everything, as the helmsman, lightly touching the rudder, moves the ship at his pleasure.

<aside>Martianus Capella</aside>

Martianus Capella likewise, when he has Philologia enter the sphere of the Sun, says that she saw there a ship which, governed by various wills, goes according to the courses of nature; it is full of the most lively flames, and carries most precious merchandise; seven brothers stand at the helm; on the mast is painted a lion, and on the outside a crocodile is likewise painted, and inside it has a fountain of divine light, which spreads through the world by hidden ways.

<aside>Scarab highly esteemed</aside>
<aside>Aelian</aside>

Of the scarab we read in Eusebius that those of Egypt made great account of it and revered it much, believing it to be the true and living image of the Sun; because all scarabs, as Aelian writes, and Suda also reports it, are male, and have no females among them. Hence the men of war there were commanded to carry them constantly on their hands, engraved in rings, to show that they needed to have a wholly virile spirit, and not at all effeminate.

The scarabs repair their progeny in this way: they scatter the seed in dung, which they then roll with their feet, and make little balls of it, which they keep turning for twenty-eight days, so that, warmed as much as they need, they take on a soul, and new scarabs are born of them; and therefore they are like the Sun, because he likewise scatters over the earth the seminal power [?3], and turns around it continually, and, revolving around Heaven, makes the Moon renew herself every month, in as much time as the scarab renews its offspring.

> Apollo embracing Daphne, to show the conformity the laurel has with this god in being always green and having a purgative force; besides showing the protection that Apollo has over emperors and poets.

<aside>Apollo's laurel</aside>

And because besides animals the ancients also consecrated trees and plants to the gods, the laurel was given to Apollo, and they made wreaths of it for him, either for the fable that is told of Daphne, loved by him and changed into this tree, or because the laurel was believed to have something divine in itself, and that therefore, when burned, it makes a crackling, showing things to come, of which the ancients judged that they would come to pass happily if the laurel, burning, made a great noise, and on the contrary if it made no noise at all.

Some of the ancients also believed that whoever tied laurel leaves to his head when going to sleep would see in a dream the truth of what he desired to know. Besides this, the laurel seems to have in itself some hidden power of fire: for its wood rubbed against that of ivy makes fire, as is done by striking flint with steel; and nothing better represents the Sun than fire. Since then the laurel was so proper to Apollo, the poets, who are so much commended to him, were afterwards crowned with it, and emperors likewise wore it, perhaps because, they say, this tree is never touched by the bolt of Heaven.

<aside>Tiberius the emperor</aside>
<aside>Apollo father of Medicine</aside>

Hence we read of the emperor Tiberius that he always wreathed his head with laurel whenever he heard thunder, to make himself safe from lightning. And on the Kalends of January the Romans gave new magistrates some laurel leaves, as if by those they were to keep healthy all the year, because the laurel was believed to benefit health greatly; of which Apollo too had care, indeed medicine was born of him, as we shall see in the image of Aesculapius, since the temperateness of the air, preserver of human bodies, comes from the Sun.

<aside>Eye of Jupiter</aside>
<aside>The Sun sees everything</aside>

Of the Sun we read that before the use of letters those of Egypt noted it in this way: they made a royal sceptre, and put an eye on top of it; and so they also sometimes called it the eye of Jupiter, as if it saw the universe and governed it with supreme justice, because the sceptre shows government. And Homer often says of the Sun that it sees and hears everything.

<aside>Apollo with four ears</aside>

Hence among the Lacedaemonians there was a statue of Apollo with four ears and as many hands, and some say that they made it so because he was once seen in that form fighting for them. But perhaps they wished to show in this manner the prudence that comes from this god, which is slow to speak, but always stands with its ears open to hear. And therefore a proverb among the Greeks said: Hear him who has four ears, meaning a wise and shrewd man.

> Image of Apollo, or of the Sun, signifying that he is the god of prudence and of power, and that the wise man should listen and act much, but speak little; and it also signifies the Sun, that is, God, hearing and seeing everything.

Apuleius bears witness that the Sun sees everything, when he says that in Thessaly there were enchantresses and evil women who, in order to steal and carry off something by their witchcraft, entered where some dead body lay so secretly that they would not even have been seen by the eyes of the Sun: as if it were impossible, or beyond measure difficult, to do anything that the Sun does not see.

<aside>Image of the Sun</aside>

Those of Phoenicia made the image of the Sun a black stone, round, and broad at the bottom, but tapering toward the top, which, as Herodotus writes, they boasted of having received from Heaven, and therefore said that this was the true image of the Sun, made divinely, not by human art. Nor, presumably, was a certain stone like a great pyramid, kept by the Megarians under the name of Apollo, unlike this one in shape; about its colour I do not know, because Pausanias, who writes of it, makes no mention of it.

<aside>Alexander ab Alexandro</aside>

And in another place, as Alexander ab Alexandro reports, they set a certain flattened and round stone on the top of a long rod, and worshipped it as the effigy and image of the Sun.

<aside>Lactantius</aside>

Lactantius on Statius writes that in Persia the Sun was the greatest god that was worshipped there, and those peoples worshipped him in a cave, or grotto, and his statue had the head of a lion, and was dressed in the Persian manner with a certain ornament that the women of Persia wore on their heads, and held with both hands by force a bull, or cow it may have been, by the horns. The lion's head shows that the Sun has greater strength in the sign of the Lion than in any of the other signs of the Zodiac; or that among the stars the Sun is such as the lion is among the wild beasts. He is in the cave when the Moon places herself before him, so that he is not seen by us at the time of the eclipse.

And for the reasons that will be given afterwards in her image, the Moon is feigned in the form of a cow, which the Sun grips by the horns, because he often takes her light from her; and he forces her, the law of nature also constraining her to it, to follow him. Some hold that this showed rather a certain mystery of those peoples of Persia, because no one could be admitted to the sacred rites of that god of theirs unless he first gave, in a certain cave, clear proof of his fortitude and his patience.

<aside>Alcaeus</aside>
<aside>Oxen dear to Apollo</aside>
<aside>Homer</aside>

In Patras, a city of Achaea, as Pausanias writes, there was an Apollo of metal, quite naked, except that his feet were shod, because he held one of them on the skull of an ox; which they say was because oxen pleased Apollo, as Alcaeus sings in a certain hymn that he makes to Mercury, who stole them from him; and before him Homer said it too, supposing that for a certain reward Apollo guarded the herds of Laomedon, and has Neptune say so:

> I surrounded with high and beautiful walls
> the great city of Troy, and made it such
> that for human strength it was impregnable,
> when you, Phoebus, like a shepherd,
> kept watch over the lovely herds in the field.
>
> *(Homer)*

> Image of Apollo Mithras, signifying the force and effects of the Sun on the Moon and on all things, and that the Sun is among the stars as the lion is among the wild beasts, and that in this sign here near us he shows his greatest force.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>
<aside>Plutarch</aside>
<aside>Ox and cultivation</aside>

The ox was the most welcome victim that was given to Apollo, so the Carystians and certain other peoples of Greece dedicated one to him made entirely of metal. But Pausanias believes that those peoples wished to show in that way that at that time, having already driven out the barbarians, they could freely cultivate the land and gather its fruits; for the ox often showed this. Hence Plutarch, writing that Theseus had the ox put on the coins of his time, gives some reasons for it, among which is this, that he wished in that way to remind his peoples, and rouse them, to cultivate the earth.

<aside>Apis</aside>

In Egypt they worshipped an ox in place of Osiris, by whom they understood the Sun, persuading themselves that he had appeared to them in such a form after Typhon his brother had killed him, envious of the honours that those peoples paid him, worshipping him as a god for the fine and useful arts that he had shown them; and they called him Apis, which exactly means ox in their language. But some have said that the ox was worshipped by the Egyptians because Osiris so ordained, together with Isis his wife, thinking that that beast deserved it for the great usefulness that mortals draw from it in the cultivation of the earth.

<aside>Herodotus</aside>
<aside>The solemn ox</aside>

Nor were they content with the image alone, but wanted the beast to be alive, though they gave it life only for a few years, and when these had passed they drowned it in a certain place, so that it died there. At this the people made the greatest mourning in the world, weeping and tearing their garments and their hair; nor was justice administered until another was found, because not all oxen or calves (a calf Herodotus calls it) were fit to be the god Apis, but it had to be born of a cow that had never calved before, and they pretended that she had become pregnant by a certain splendour that had come upon her; that it be all black, have a white square spot on the forehead and on the back a certain mark of an eagle; have on the tongue, or in the palate, a black mark, which was perhaps like a scarab, and at the tail double hairs.

When the Egyptians had found this beast of theirs, all rejoiced and made a very great feast of it, and gave it to the priests to keep with great reverence and with all those honours that they paid to the divine deities; who first led it to the City of the Nile, where they fed it for forty days, and afterwards introduced it into a gilded ship, and so carried it to Mensi, where as a god they placed it in the temple of Vulcan. Only in these days was it lawful for women to see it, because at other times it was forbidden them.

> Image of the sacred oxen among the Egyptians; and they signified the Sun, Osiris, and Agriculture.

From it they also took certain responses, as from the Oracle, in this way: they offered it with the hand either hay or fodder, and if it took it willingly and ate, things were to turn out happily, and the contrary was to happen if it did not want to eat. And in Mensi, the principal city of Egypt, they said that Apis appeared at times, and so for his appearance they celebrated some days of festival with the most solemn rejoicing.

<aside>King Cambyses</aside>

At this King Cambyses, having never seen such a solemnity before, was once angered, when, routed by the Ammonians, he returned to Mensi; and thinking that those peoples were rejoicing at his misfortune, because he knew that they loved him little, he had some of the chief men put to death, unwilling to believe, as they affirmed, that the festival was held for the appearance of their god Apis; and he said that it could not be that any god should come into Egypt without his knowledge.

<aside>Cambyses killed Apis</aside>

The priests, called for this reason, confirmed what the others had said, and he commanded them to show him this god, and they at once brought him, with great solemnity, the revered ox. At which Cambyses began to laugh, and, drawing his scimitar, cut its throat, saying to those priests and to the others who had accompanied the beast: "O worthless men that you are, are the gods then of this sort, of flesh and blood? and do they feel blows and wounds? This is just a god worthy of you, but you shall not have made sport of me at your pleasure." And having said this he commanded that the priests be very well flogged, and that everyone be killed who was found going about the city making festival. And so the festival was ended, as Herodotus relates.

<aside>Varro</aside>

Varro writes, and St Augustine reports it, that Apis was a king of the Argives, who went into Egypt and was so dear to those peoples that after death they worshipped him and held him as their principal god, calling him Serapis (*Serapi*); and before they made any temple for him they worshipped him in the ark, or tomb, where they placed him as soon as he was dead, which is called by them Soro (*Soro*); and so, putting these two words together, the one of the ark, the other of the dead man, the name Sorapis (*Sorapi*) was made, which, the first letter being afterwards changed, was called Serapis (*Serapi*). And the ox was called simply Apis, because it was alive, and worshipped without an ark and outside the tomb.

The Egyptians held this man in such veneration that they did not want it to be known that he had been a man, and the penalty for whoever said it was death. Hence in all his temples there was the image of Harpocrates, to warn people to keep silent and not to dare say that Apis or Serapis had ever been a man.

<aside>Josephus</aside>
<aside>Cleomenes</aside>

Besides the ox they also worshipped in Egypt the billy goat, as we read in Josephus, where he writes against Apion, and that beast which they called Cynocephalus, of which something will be said in the image of Mercury, and the crocodile too, on which a joke almost like the one Cambyses played on the ox Apis was played by Cleomenes, one of the chief captains of Alexander the Great, who was then passing through that part of Egypt where the crocodile is worshipped as a god; and having learned that a boy of his had been destroyed by one of those beasts, he had all the priests called, and, complaining of their god, who had come to offend him, though he had never thought of doing him any harm, said that he was resolved to take vengeance on the crocodiles, and for this commanded that preparations be made for a great hunt of them, which was not, however, afterwards held, because Cleomenes was content to draw a large sum of silver, which those priests gave him, so that their god should not be mocked and destroyed, as he would have been if the hunt had been held.

> Image of Apollo nude, lover of oxen, signifying that the Sun gives life to the things of agriculture, because with his moderate warmth he gives strength to the seed, the herbs, the plants, and everything, that they may reach their due perfection, maturity, and end.

<aside>Aristotle</aside>
<aside>Apollo the shepherd</aside>

This Aristotle records, writing in the *Economics* of those who knew how to find money by new methods. But let us return to Apollo, who, for the things already said, and for the fables that are told of him (as that he once guarded the herds of Admetus, and the like), had from the ancients, besides many other surnames, this one too, that he was called Shepherd, because the temperate power of the Sun pastures and gives nourishment to all things.

<aside>Herodotus</aside>
<aside>Table of the Sun</aside>

From this, perhaps, came the mad superstition of the Ethiopians who inhabit Africa on the side of the Southern Sea. For among these peoples there were certain meadows in which roast meats of every sort of animal were found almost always, and everyone went there to eat of them at his pleasure, believing (although, as Herodotus writes, the magistrates of the country carried them there by night with great diligence and great secrecy) that they were produced there from the earth already roasted, and perhaps by the power of the Sun, because that place was called the table of the Sun, much celebrated by the ancients. Hence arose the proverb that those houses of the rich and powerful, where the poor can go to eat at their pleasure, are called tables of the Sun.

<aside>Image of Apollo</aside>
<aside>Lucian</aside>

Besides this, the Assyrians showed the power that the Sun has in this world, and the effects that he produces in it, with an image of Apollo that had a long and pointed beard, with something on its head like a basket. And Lucian writes that only some of the Assyrians made Apollo with a beard, and reproached the others, who made him without, as if being so young showed some imperfection, which ought not to be in the statues of the gods; and therefore they must be made in the form of an already perfect man, such as one who has a beard.

<aside>Macrobius</aside>

Around the chest it had a breastplate; with its right hand it held a spear, on top of which was a small figure of Victory, and with the left it held out a flower; on the shoulders it had a cloth with the head of Medusa surrounded by serpents; at its side stood some eagles, that seemed to fly; and before the feet an image of a woman, which on one side and on the other had two other images, likewise of women, whom a great serpent bound with winding coils. Thus Macrobius describes this image, and thus he also interprets it.

> Image of Apollo and of the Earth among the Assyrians, signifying the effects of the Sun on the earth and in all things, with the images of nature and of the matter from which things are formed and take their origin. The serpent in which they end denotes the winding course of the Sun.

<aside>Interpretation</aside>

The beard, which hangs down over the chest, signifies that from Heaven to earth the Sun spreads his rays. The gilded basket, which rises on high, shows the celestial fire of which the Sun is believed to be made. The spear and breastplate [?4] are depicted on account of Mars, because they say that through him is shown the vehement ardour of the Sun. The Victory means that everything is subject to the power of the Sun. The flower signifies the beauty of things, which the hidden power of the Sun sows and fosters, and its temperate warmth brings forth, nourishes, and preserves. The woman who stands before his feet is the earth, which the Sun illuminates from Heaven with his rays.

<aside>Adad</aside>
<aside>Adargatis</aside>

The same thing the Assyrians themselves showed also, according to what Macrobius likewise reports, with the image of their greatest god, whom they called Adad, to whom they made the goddess Adargatis subject. To these two, those peoples said, all things obeyed, and by the former they understood the Sun, by the latter the earth. Hence the image of Adad had its rays pointing downward, because the Sun spreads his rays over the earth, and that of Adargatis sent hers upward, showing that whatever is born on earth is born there by the power of the higher lights; and so that the earth might be better understood by this goddess, they placed lions beneath her, because those of Phrygia feigned that the mother of the gods, whom they believed to be the earth, was drawn by lions, as will be seen afterwards in her image.

The other two women, who are at the sides of the one in the middle, show the matter of which things are made, and the nature that makes them; who seem together to serve the earth, doing so much for her adornment. The serpent that binds them gives us to understand the winding path that the Sun makes. The eagles, because they fly very swiftly and on high, signify the height and the speed of the Sun.

<aside>Porphyry</aside>

To the shoulders was then added the cloth with the head of Medusa, which is the proper emblem of Minerva, because (as Porphyry says) Minerva is nothing other than that power of the Sun which brightens human intellects and sends prudence into the minds of mortals.

<aside>Suda</aside>

And that the ancients also wished by Mars to understand certain properties of the Sun, besides what I have said and shall say in his image, a statue no less than thirty cubits in size attests quite fully, which, says Pausanias, was in a certain part of Laconia, consecrated to Apollo, and seemed very ancient, and made at the time when men did not yet know how to make statues too well, which was before Daedalus; because he was the first, as Suda reports, to open the eyes of statues and to make them with their feet apart from one another. This one, apart from the face, the hands, and the feet, seemed in the rest to be a column, and had a helmet on its head, and in one hand the bow and in the other a spear, which are proper emblems of Mars, although Minerva also carries them, but for a different reason, as can be seen in their images.

> Image of Adad and of Adargatis, gods of the Assyrians, understood by them as the Sun and as the Earth, denoting that all that is born on earth comes from the power of the Sun and from the solar rays.

> Image of Serapis, god of the Egyptians, understood by them as the Sun and as the Nile, with the image of a body with three heads signifying the three times, past, present, and to come, and that the Sun goes with order and measure and never strays.

<aside>Macrobius</aside>

Those of Egypt made statues of the Sun in different ways, and one among the others was one that had the head half shaved, so that the hair remained only on the right side, which meant (as Macrobius interprets) that the Sun is never so hidden from Nature that she does not continually feel some benefit from his rays; and the cut hair signifies that the Sun even at the time when we do not see him has the strength and power to return to us anew, just as cut hair grows again, because the roots have remained. Some also hold that the same statue signifies that part of the year that has the least light, when, as if all the growth of it were cut away, the days are shorter, which become long again when it seems to be born anew, and once more begins to grow.

Besides this, in Egypt they made images of the Sun with feathers, and not all of one colour, but one dark and gloomy, the other bright and shining, and this one they called celestial, that one infernal: because the Sun is said to be in Heaven when he goes through the six signs of the Zodiac that make the time of summer and are called superior; and they say that he descends into the underworld when he begins to travel through the other six, of winter, called inferior; and the feathers that they gave to these images were to show the speed of the Sun: so Macrobius expounds it.

<aside>Serapis</aside>
<aside>Suda</aside>

We also read that under the name of Serapis they understood the Sun in Egypt, although they sometimes also took him for Jupiter. Hence they made his statue in the form of a man wearing on his head a modius, as if he wished to show that in all things the fitting measure must be used. And Suda reports that some said that he was the Nile, which, with that modius he had on his head and a certain rod that is used for measuring, meant that its waters had to spread with a certain measure, to make Egypt fertile.

Beside him stood, as Macrobius writes, a figure with three heads which were joined in a single body, around which a serpent was coiled in such a way that it hid it entirely, and put its head under his right hand, as if he were master of all time, shown by the three heads I mentioned. Of these one, the middle one, which was a lion's, signified the present time, because this, placed between the past and what is to come, actually exists, and has greater force than the others. The other, on the right side, of a pleasant dog, showed that the time to come always allures us with new hopes. And the third, on the left, of a rapacious wolf, meant that the time past seizes all things and devours them in such a way that of many it leaves no memory at all.

This god had also in Alexandria, a city of Egypt, in the temple dedicated to him, an image made of every sort of metals and woods, so large that, stretching out its hands, it touched both sides of the temple; and there was a little window made with such art that the Sun, always at his first appearing entering through it, came to illuminate the face of the great image; seeing which, the people began to believe, and to say, that the Sun came every morning to greet Serapis and to kiss him.

> Image of the Sun, varier and producer of all times and seasons, and of all things, of life and death, and of the four vessels in which is the variety of goods and evils, named head of Vulcan, laughter of Jupiter, death of Saturn, and breast of Juno, from which comes everything.

<aside>Pliny</aside>

And in Thebes, likewise a city of Egypt, in the temple of the same Serapis (as Pliny writes) there was a statue of a certain hard marble, dark like iron, which was believed to be Memnon; which every morning, touched by the rays of the Sun at his first appearing, made a certain grating sound, and a light murmur, as if it wished to speak.

<aside>Vessels of Phoebus</aside>

It seems to me that Martianus Capella depicts the Sun better than any other, at the time when Mercury and Virtue go to consult with him whether Mercury should take a wife, where he shows that all the variations of the times come from him, feigning him seated on a great and high tribunal, with four covered vessels before him, into which he looks, uncovering only one at a time.

<aside>Head of Vulcan</aside>
<aside>Laughter of Jupiter</aside>
<aside>Death of Saturn</aside>
<aside>Breast of Juno</aside>

These were all made in different forms, and of different metals. One of hardest iron, from which living flames were seen to issue, and it was called head of Vulcan. The other of shining silver, and it was full of serenity and temperate air, and they called it Laughter of Jupiter. The third of grey lead, and its name was Death of Saturn, full of rain, cold, frost, and snow. The fourth, which stood nearest to Phoebus himself, was made of clear glass, and held in itself all the seed that the air scatters over the earth, and was named Breast of Juno.

From these vessels, now from one, now from another, and sometimes from this and sometimes from that, according to what he needed, Phoebus took that from which mortals afterwards had life, and sometimes also death. For when he wished to offer the world the sweet breeze of the vital spirit, he put part of the temperate air from the silver vessel with part of the seed that was shut up in the glass vessel. And when he threatened plague and death, he added to these the burning flames from the iron vessel, or else the horrid cold hidden in the dark lead.

<aside>Apollo kills the Cyclopes</aside>
<aside>Apollo father of Aesculapius</aside>

Here it is plainly seen that, as I have said at other times, the diversity of the times comes from the hand of the Sun, and that the quality of the air likewise changes through him, from which various accidents then arise, now good, now bad, among mortals; and for this reason the poets feigned that Apollo killed the Cyclopes (who are the mists and the other bad qualities of the air), and that he was father of Aesculapius, of whom was born afterwards Hygieia (*Higia*), which means Health.

<aside>Aesculapius</aside>

For, as Pausanias writes, having heard it once from a man of Phoenicia, Aesculapius is nothing other than the air, which is purged by the Sun in such a way that it offers health to mortals, as physicians too are believed to do, either by keeping bodies healthy or by restoring the sick.

<aside>Solinus</aside>
<aside>Pausanias</aside>

And therefore the ancients said that Aesculapius was the god of medicine, and he was principally worshipped in Epidaurus, a city of Greece, which was much esteemed for his temple (as Solinus writes), because whoever sought a remedy for some infirmity went to sleep in it, and learned in a dream what he needed to do to be cured; and there the image of this god was made of gold and ivory, seated on a fine throne, as Pausanias describes it, with a staff in one hand, while he held the other on the head of a serpent, and at his feet lay a dog.

> Image of Aesculapius, god of medicine, with the animals sacred to him, signifying the difficulty of medicine and the duty of the good physician; understood also as the purified air, bringer of health.

<aside>Festus</aside>

Festus seems to give the reason for all this when he says: they give the serpent to Aesculapius because it is a most vigilant animal, as the good physician must be; they give him the dog because as a little child he was nursed on dog's milk, and the staff, which is all knotted, signifies the difficulty of medicine. And Festus adds (what is not in the image set down by Pausanias) that the ancients made him wreaths of laurel, because this tree helps in many infirmities.

<aside>Aesculapius's cock</aside>

Aesculapius was depicted for the most part with a long beard, as shows what I said of Dionysius at the beginning of this image, but he is found also at times without, as Peter Apian puts him in the book of antiquities collected by him, and he wears a certain garment in the form of a shirt, with another little garment over it, girded up, in which (holding the hem with his left hand) he seems to have certain fruits; and with the right hand he holds two cocks, because the cock was consecrated to him, for the vigilance that the good physician must have, for which reason the ancients also sacrificed them to him.

And for this reason Socrates in Plato, when he is about to die, leaves in his will a cock to Aesculapius, the wise philosopher wishing in that way to show that he rendered to the divine goodness, healer of all ills (understood by Aesculapius), and therefore daughter of divine providence (shown by Apollo, from which he had also received it), the light of day: of which the cock is the messenger, that is, the light of the present life.

<aside>Aesculapius's serpent</aside>
<aside>How Aesculapius was brought to Rome</aside>

The Phliasians too in the territory of Corinth had him beardless; and among the Sicyonians likewise he was such, as Pausanias also writes, made all of gold and ivory, who held a sceptre in his right hand, and in the other a pine cone, which is the fruit [?5] of the pine. And those peoples said that they had had him in this way: that a woman called Nicagora brought him to them from Epidaurus on a cart drawn by two mules, not however made as his statue was, but changed into a serpent, as the Romans too had him, when to remedy a grave pestilence (as Valerius Maximus reports) they likewise sent to Epidaurus to fetch Aesculapius, on the advice of the Sibylline books: for they received a great and beautiful snake, worshipped there as the deity of Aesculapius, which, having come out of the temple, went for three days about the city at its pleasure, to the great and reverent wonder of everyone, and having then entered the ship of the Romans, and placed itself in the most honoured place, coiled in fine rings, with the greatest calm let itself be carried to Rome, where, having entered the temple that is on the Island, which was dedicated to Aesculapius, it was worshipped according to the rite that the Romans brought with the serpent from Epidaurus.

> Image of Aesculapius, god of medicine, with the cocks, birds sacred to him, signifying the vigilance necessary to physicians, and the serpent, symbol of health and length of life, which comes from the care of physicians.

<aside>Philostratus</aside>
<aside>Hyginus</aside>

So with the image of Aesculapius there was always, with good reason, the serpent. He was also at times depicted with it coiled about the staff that he held in his hand, of which many reasons can be gathered from Philostratus, from Hyginus, from Eusebius, from Pliny, from Macrobius, and from others, of which I shall give no more than one, not because this one is truer than the others (it has something of the fable), but because it seems to me more pleasant to read.

<aside>The tale of Aesculapius</aside>

Aesculapius had come into such esteem for the miraculous works that he did in medicine that he was believed not only able to cure every ill, but also able to bring the dead back to life.

<aside>Minos, king of Crete</aside>

Hence Minos, king of Crete, his son Glaucus having died, whom he loved beyond measure, had Aesculapius called, and begged him to bring his beloved son back to life; but when he saw that neither prayers nor promises availed him, because Aesculapius, knowing that this was impossible for him, refused the undertaking, turning to force he had him shut up in a certain place with the very best guard, threatening never to let him out of it until he had restored life to his dead son.

Aesculapius was greatly grieved at this, and saw himself in a bad way, so that he set to thinking, not how to bring the dead man back to life, but how he might escape from there; and while he went on thus turning over various things, it happened that he saw a snake pass before him; he killed it with the staff on which he was leaning, and a little later he saw another come, which, with a certain herb that it carried in its mouth, having touched the head of the dead one, at once brought it back to life. Aesculapius, who saw this, at once took that herb, and, having done the same over the dead body of Glaucus, brought him back to life, and himself to liberty.

<aside>Serpents familiar to Aesculapius</aside>

And for this he wished the serpent to be thereafter always coiled about the staff that he carried in his hand, as is seen for the most part in the statues that are made for him. But whether for this, or for some other reason, since, as I have said, the reasons for it are many, serpents were so familiar to Aesculapius that not only in Epidaurus, which was his own and principal seat, were they all consecrated to him, and more than the others certain ones which are tame and pleasant to men, but at Corinth too serpents were fed in his temple, to which nobody, however, dared to approach, but they put what they wished to give them at the door of the temple, and went away afterwards without any further care of it. And in another city not far from there, among the other images that were in the temple of Aesculapius there was one that sat upon a serpent, which they said was the mother of Aratus, who was the son of Aesculapius, as Pausanias recounts.

<aside>Cave of Trophonius</aside>

He writes likewise that in a certain cave of Boeotia, from which the river Hercyna rises, there were certain images standing, with rods like sceptres in their hands, around which serpents were coiled; so some said that they were of Aesculapius and of Hygieia his daughter, and others believed them to be of Trophonius, because the wood that was there all about was surnamed from him, and from Hercyna, once companion of Proserpina, from whom likewise the river I mentioned took its name; since the ancients consecrated serpents to Trophonius no less than to Aesculapius, believing perhaps that these were certain reporters of the oracle celebrated in the cavern that was called the Cave of Trophonius, because he himself was for a time shut up there to foretell future things, and died there of hunger, and so he was ever afterwards the more esteemed and revered: all the more because the oracle did not cease with his death, but whether his Genius remained there, as some said, or some other demon, friend of his, succeeded there, responses were still given in the same cave.

<aside>Oracle of Trophonius</aside>

And for that reason whoever went to this oracle used first to placate the shade of Trophonius with certain sacrifices, and after some ceremonies, having first washed in the river Hercyna, went to drink of the two fountains: the one was of oblivion, of this he drank first to forget all the past; the other of memory, and of this he drank afterwards to remember better what he should bring back from the oracle; and then, dressed only in his shirt, with shoes on his feet and his head bound with certain fillets, at one of the mouths of the cave he was drawn in there by a certain breath in the way that the waters of a most rapid torrent would do, and there came to meet him certain serpents and other spirits and phantoms, to whom he gave some cakes made with honey, brought by him for this; then, curled up with his head between his knees, he stayed there until he had heard or seen that for which he had gone: for this oracle sometimes spoke, and at other times showed the things to come. And then in the same manner as he was drawn in, he was thrust out, but by another mouth, however, of the same cavern, and so dazed and stunned that he no longer remembered himself nor others.

But the priests who were there for this put him on a seat that was called the seat of memory, and there came back to him then all that he had seen and heard, and he told it to those priests who kept account of it. Afterwards little by little he came back to himself, and one may believe that he had a hard time there, because few were those who ever laughed again after they had been in the cave of Trophonius. Pausanias tells many other things that were done to go to this oracle, and says that he himself was there: but I have spoken of it so briefly only to show who this man was, to whom the serpents were consecrated no less than to Aesculapius.

Cicero, speaking of the nature of the gods, says that there were many Mercuries, and that of these one was under the earth, and was the same as Trophonius.

<aside>Sign of health</aside>

Serpents were among the ancients a sign of health: for as the serpent, having laid aside its old skin, renews itself, so men, recovering their health, seem to be renewed.

> Ancient ring, in the stone of which is engraved the symbol of Health, that is the Pentagon, a solid form, which stands upright on every side.

<aside>Image of Health</aside>

And for this reason the ancients made the image of Health in this way. A woman sat on a high seat with a cup in her hand, and had an altar beside her, on which was a serpent all coiled upon itself, except that it still raised its head.

<aside>Sign of Health</aside>
<aside>Medals of Antiochus</aside>

The sign of health is also made in the form of a Pentagon, as is seen in the ancient medals of Antiochus, of whom we read that, once making war against the Galatians and finding himself in a bad way, he saw (or, to put heart into his soldiers, pretended to have seen) Alexander the Great, who held out this sign to him, telling him that he should give it to the soldiers and have them wear it on their persons, and that he would remain the victor of that war, as he afterwards did.

<aside>Hygieia</aside>

The letters around the sign, the Latin ones say *Salus*, and the Greek ones mean the same, saying *Higeia*. This name was the name of the daughter of Aesculapius, as I have said, who was worshipped by the ancients together with her father, beside whom they often set her statue, as Pausanias says. It was so in a certain place in the territory of Corinth, where the statue of Aesculapius was clothed in a woollen tunic with a cloak over it that covered him entirely, so that nothing was seen of him but the face, the hands and the feet. And Hygieia likewise was all covered, partly with hair that the women had cut off and offered to the goddess, partly with certain very fine veils, all cut into strips.

<aside>Sminthian Apollo</aside>

But let us return to the Sun, whose rays, by purifying the air, bring it about that the earth too produces abundantly, as perhaps those wished to show who in the Trojan country made the statue of the Sminthian Apollo, so called from mice, because he trod on one with his foot; and mice in those parts are called *sminthi*. And it seems to me that the tale which is told of the priest of Apollo who despised sacred things confirms it. His harvest was spoiled every year by mice on this account, and they were afterwards killed by this god, once that man had returned to holding religion in account. For mice, and the other little animals that spring out of the earth, are born of the badly tempered air, by reason of which the earth cannot produce things useful to mortals, except when the rays of the Sun, taking away every bad quality, kill those animals and give the earth strength to produce these things.

One reads in Pliny of another statue made by Praxiteles for Apollo, which might be said not to differ much in meaning from this one of the mice that I have just mentioned, because he stood with the arrow on the bow, as if lying in ambush to kill a lizard that was not far from him.

There is found also another reason why Apollo was called Sminthian and had his statue with the mouse. It is that those of Crete, wishing to send out a colony, had advice from the oracle of Apollo to place the city where the sons of the earth gave them the greatest trouble. And the men of the colony having been sent to the Trojan fields, in one night the mice gnawed through all the straps of their shields. They noticed it in the morning and understood that here they were to stop, by the counsel of the oracle, because those mice were born of the earth; and having founded the city they made a temple to Apollo, calling him Sminthian.

> Image of the goddess Health, and of the serpent sacred to her, signifying that the drinking of medicines, both purgative and preservative, brings back to us lost health, length and stability of life, and health signified by the serpent.

<aside>Mice held in veneration</aside>

And that people afterwards always held the *sminthi*, that is the mice, in great veneration, and had some tame ones fed at public expense, which lived in certain little holes beside the high altar; and for that reason one was also put, as I have said, with the statue of Apollo. From which it can be seen that the statues of the gods, and likewise the others that were dedicated to them, often showed, as I said before, the things obtained from them and the actions that by their counsel and favour had turned out happily, as is seen also in Pausanias in so many that were at Delphi; of which it will suffice for now to give two.

<aside>Goat offered to Apollo</aside>

The one was a goat of metal, offered to Apollo by the Cleonaeans, a people of Greece, because once when they were badly afflicted by plague, they had advice from this god to sacrifice a goat at the rising of the sun, as they did; and the plague ceased, and for that reason they afterwards sent the goat of metal to be offered.

<aside>Ass offered to Apollo</aside>
<aside>Sicyonians, Ambraciots</aside>

The other was of an ass, for this reason. The Ambraciots and the Sicyonians, all peoples of Greece, were making war on each other, and the Sicyonians had laid an ambush for those who were about to leave the town. One night it happened that an ass, driven toward the city by its driver [?6] with some load on its back, chanced to hear a she-ass going ahead, and began to follow her, braying as loudly as anything in the world and walking much faster than the driver would have wished. He therefore began to shout likewise, and as if his beast must understand him better, raised his voice each time more like an ass, so that the noise was great. The Sicyonians, frightened by it as though the enemy had discovered them, came out of their ambush and took to flight, and the Ambraciots, warned of this, went after them and routed them. Then, having made a fine ass of metal, they sent it to be offered at Delphi in the temple of Apollo, in memory of the benefit that seemed to them to have come from that beast, and because they wished also to acknowledge that victory as coming from that god.

<aside>Dove on the shoulder of Apollo</aside>

Alexander ab Alexandro likewise reports that there was once made at Naples a statue of Apollo, which besides the other insignia and ornaments commonly given to this god had a dove on its shoulder, and a woman stood before it, who looked at it and seemed to adore it, and that this was Parthenope, who adored the dove on the shoulder of Apollo, because this good bird, from which she took good augury, had been her guide when she went from Greece to the Neapolitan fields. For the Greeks were never accustomed to pass from one place to another unless they first took augury from it and asked counsel of the gods.

<aside>Horses of the chariot of Phoebus</aside>
<aside>Chariot of Phoebus</aside>

The poets have then given to Phoebus, who is the same as Apollo, as I said before, besides other things, a chariot drawn by four very swift steeds, as Ovid says, although Martianus Capella makes mention of only two. These were named: Pyrois the first, which denotes reddish, since in the morning, when the Sun rises, he appears to us red in colour; the second Eous, which means shining, since the Sun, having risen somewhat above our hemisphere, is seen by us to shine clearly; the third Aethon, which signifies burning, since at midday the solar rays seem to burn wherever they strike; the fourth Phlegon, which is a colour between yellow and black, and means as much as lover of the earth, since the Sun seems to be of just such a colour when in the evening he goes down to set, and seems almost like a lover to run most swiftly to rest in the lap of the wide earth. For these properties, then, and because they are animals of great liveliness and very swift, they were set to his chariot, which Ovid says was all of gold except that the spokes of the wheels were of silver, and that chrysolites and other most brilliant gems were set all over it in most beautiful order, which, touched by the light of Phoebus, gave back a marvellous splendour.

> Image of Hygieia, daughter of Aesculapius, with the dog and the serpent, symbols of her father, signifying the diligence of the good physician and the effects that result from it.

<aside>Crown of Phoebus</aside>

All this that Ovid puts on the chariot of Phoebus, and more besides, Martianus Capella placed about the body of the god himself when he draws his portrait thus. Phoebus has on his head a crown of twelve most brilliant gems, of which three adorn his forehead and shine so much that they dazzle anyone who turns his eyes toward him; and these are Lychnites, Astrites and Ceraunius. Six are at both sides of the temples, three on each side, which are Emerald, Scythis, Jasper, Hyacinth, Dendrites and Heliotrope, which at certain times so paint the earth with their colours that they make it all green; and it is believed that Spring and Autumn gave them to him, so that when he returns in his seasons he may make use of them. The other three, called Hydatis, Diamond and Crystal, generated by frozen Winter, are in the back part of the crown.

His hair is so fair that it seems of gold. His face at its first appearance shows as that of a tender boy, then of a fierce youth, and at the last of a cold old man. The rest of the body seems to be all of flame; and he has feathers at his feet, adorned with the most burning carbuncles [?7]. About him he has a mantle woven of gold and purple. With his left hand he holds a most shining shield, and with the right he holds out a lighted torch. I shall not stop to say more of this image, because it is such that everyone can understand it very well for himself.

<aside>Eusebius</aside>

But I come to set down another, which Eusebius writes was in Elephantine, a city of Egypt, made in the form of a man who had the head of a ram with horns, and was all of a sky-blue colour, which, because it is the colour of the sea, which represents moisture in the universe, signifies (as the same Eusebius interprets it) that the Moon, when joined to the Sun in the sign of Aries, is much moister than at other times. But I do not wish to enter into these matters of the astrologers, because the images they set down have little to do with my purpose.

> Chariot of the Sun, god of light, with the image and adornment of the Sun himself, drawn by four horses, signifying the four effects and splendours of the Sun of the day and of the year, and the swiftness of his motion, and his body.

> Image of the Sun, called also Phoebus and Apollo, god of the day, signifying his effects in the seasons and in the celestial signs and on the Moon, and that the Moon in the sign of Aries is very moist when she is conjoined with the Sun, who illuminates all and gives life to all.

<aside>Claudian</aside>

I shall therefore now bring to an end what I had to say of the Sun, but not before I have set down one more portrait of him, which Claudian draws on the robe of Proserpina, where his sister the Moon was also depicted, whose image will therefore be placed immediately after this one. Claudian speaks thus in our language.

> Here, as twins born at one birth, the Sun and his sister
> she herself had depicted, but not alike
> in looks, for very different
> was the colour of the face of those who from Heaven
> were to be the guides of the day and of the night.
> Then Tethys, sweetly singing in the cradle,
> soothes the little children with caresses,
> quiets them and lulls them to sleep again, or else in her lap
> holds them gladly. If they seem sad to her,
> full of Love she feeds and comforts them.
> Titan she supports with her right arm
> and rests against her breast, for in strength
> he is weak and still tender, and his step
> is little firm, and he tries to walk unsteadily.
> Such was the depicted Sun in his first years,
> when he did not yet keep the little flames of rays
> at his head, nor the burning crown,
> but only a mild warmth from his mouth
> came out of him, and at his small cry
> some spark of splendour could be seen.
> His sister from the soft breast
> on the left side sucks the milk,
> and, not well sated with the nourishing liquid,
> dries the breast of kindly Tethys [?8].
> Her temples swell up a little,
> and from her brow of silver colour
> the young horns already bud forth.
>
> *(Claudian)*

<aside>Hyginus</aside>

Why Tethys had the Sun on her right arm and the Moon on her left, Severian, a Greek author, says, as Janus Parrhasius reports: that the eternal God, maker of the universe, made first the Sun and afterwards the Moon, and placed this one at the bounds of the West and that one, opposite, in the East; and according to Hyginus the East is called in Heaven the right part, and the West the left, although the diviners of Tuscany, as the same Hyginus reports, divided the universe in this way, and made the right part to be from the North, and the left from the South.

> Image of Aurora, and of the horse Pegasus, who draws her chariot, denoting that hour to be the most convenient and of the greatest profit for study, and the glory that results from it to the learned and virtuous man.

<aside>Aurora</aside>

It might be said also that Claudian put the Sun on the right arm and the Moon on the left because the former has more force and is of much greater vigour than the latter, of whom I shall speak as soon as I have drawn Aurora. Although in Heaven she goes before the Sun, I do not believe she ought to take it ill to have been placed behind him among these images of mine, because in any case she is born of him, since Aurora is nothing other than the first reddening that the rays of the Sun make in the East when they begin to appear above our hemisphere. Hence the poets have made many fables about her and have described her in various ways, which serve much more for one who writes than for one who wishes to make an image of her; and therefore I shall speak not of all, but of only a few, according as they seem to me more convenient for making a painting.

<aside>Pausanias</aside>

I do not find that, although the ancients placed Aurora among the gods of Heaven, they ever made any statue of her; except that, as Pausanias writes, there was one of clay in Athens, which was carrying off Cephalus, but he does not say how it was made. I shall therefore draw her portrait from what the poets have said of her.

Homer depicts her with fair and golden hair, and with a seat likewise golden, and the robe also of the same colour. Virgil says that she comes with coloured hands to drive away the stars. And Ovid, that she opens the reddening gates, full of most beautiful roses, when Phoebus wishes to come out of the East.

<aside>Horse of Aurora</aside>
<aside>Homer</aside>

Some besides put a lighted torch in her hand, and give her a chariot drawn by the horse Pegasus, who had wings; and they say that she obtained him from Jupiter after Bellerophon had fallen from him. This perhaps gives us to understand that that hour of the morning is the most convenient, and the best of all others, for one who writes poetry, because it was that horse who, striking with his hoof, made the water of the fountain spring forth, for that reason also called the horse's fountain (*caballino*), so much frequented by the Muses. Nevertheless Homer gives her not this horse but two others, both bright and shining.

Some also imagine that Aurora, at her first appearance, comes all coloured, scattering through the air baskets of flowers and of roses, yellow and crimson. And in sum everyone describes her as pleases him best, always showing that colour between yellow and red which the first rays of the Sun scatter through the air.

> Image of Diana, goddess of the woods and of hunting, who is understood as the Moon, accompanied by some huntresses; she was also held to be the goddess of modesty and chastity, punisher of those who violate it.
